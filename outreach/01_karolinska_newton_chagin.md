I'm an independent researcher building a quantitative, citation-grounded map of the mechanisms
that set human height — every parameter traced to a primary source and graded, with the
unknowns recorded as first-class entries rather than omitted. Working through it, I keep
arriving at two questions that your two 2026 papers are, as far as I can tell, uniquely

**1. The human growth fraction.**

Cell production per column is the product of the growth fraction and the division rate, and a
single-pulse labelling index bounds only their ratio — the growth fraction cancels. So the
human proliferative-zone labelling percentages (4.4% and 3.4%, the only ones I can find, via
Kember's 1993 review) cannot tell us whether human chondrocytes cycle slowly or whether most
of them are simply out of cycle. Kember says this explicitly and leaves it open, and it appears
to have stayed open for thirty-three years.

Wilsman et al. (J Orthop Res 1996;14:927) settled it in rat with continuous BrdU from an
osmotic pump, reading the labelling index to plateau: growth fraction 0.89–0.99 across four
plates. Essentially no idle pool.

Your sequential EdU → IdU → CldU protocol on intact human biopsies (Avijgan Fig. 2e–f) is the
same experiment. Read out on the **proliferative** zone, with the labelled fraction followed to
plateau, it returns the human growth fraction directly.

The reason I think it is worth the tissue: if the human growth fraction is near 1, then raising
growth means accelerating the cycle of a differentiated cell, and the ceiling is low. If it is
0.2, then most of the compartment is idle and recruitment is a multiplicative lever nobody has
tried — in a compartment your own data shows is releasable, since your resting-zone cells
re-enter cycle within days of explant. I recognise explanting releases arrest, so the number is
an upper bound on the *in vivo* value; that still discriminates, because an upper bound near 1
refutes the idle-pool hypothesis and an upper bound near 0.2 establishes it despite the release.

A second, cheaper readout falls out of the same tissue: shortening the inter-label interval from
days to hours gives **S-phase duration in human growth cartilage**, which Kember named as never
measured and which every conversion from labelling index to proliferation rate requires. My own
arithmetic from the two existing human numbers gives 16–21 h against 3.4–6.1 h in rat — but the
same arithmetic is internally inconsistent unless one of those two human numbers is wrong by
about fourfold, so it needs measuring, not deriving.

**2. Sacubitril in the Chu explant.**

Hakata et al. (2024) report dose-dependent skeletal overgrowth in wild-type mice from the
approved neprilysin inhibitor sacubitril, abolished by cartilage-specific NPR-B knockout, and
present in fetal tibial organ culture with no endocrine axis — so it should be detectable in an
explant. Your two-month human growth-plate culture with GH as positive control is the only
preparation I know of that could test it on human tissue.

What makes it worth doing rather than merely plausible is that **a null would be informative**.
Re-analysing GSE288028, I find NPPC sits at the detection floor across all zones and donors
(0.04–0.52% of cells) while MME averages 7.97% — roughly 5× NPR3. If local CNP really is
absent, an inhibitor of its degradation should do nothing in an explant while still being able
to work in a child through *circulating* CNP. So the experiment tests the local-ligand question
whichever way it comes out.

One design note from your own data, which I have not seen stated as a constraint: GH failed to
produce an effect in some donors in Chu Fig. S8C. If the best-validated growth drug in medicine
can be hidden by donor-to-donor variance, any explant screen needs several donors per arm and
is underpowered for anything weaker than GH.
