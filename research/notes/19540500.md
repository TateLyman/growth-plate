# PMID 19540500 — Villemure I, Stokes IAF. Growth plate mechanics and mechanobiology: a survey of present understanding (2009, J Biomech)

Read in full (PMC, ~46k chars; review).

**Key quantitative content.**
- **Growth = N × h_max** (Kember; Stokes 2005): new cells per day × final hypertrophic height.
  - Wilsman 1996: between-plate growth variability is explained by hypertrophic enlargement (44–59%) and hypertrophic-zone matrix (14–28%) more than by proliferation (7–10%).
  - Chondrocytes enlarge mostly *along the growth axis*, with little width increase.
- **Stokes linear stress–growth law:** G = Gm[1 + β(σ − σm)], β = 1.71 MPa⁻¹.
  - That is 17.1% growth change per 0.1 MPa (tibia 18.6%, vertebra 15%), with **no threshold**. It holds for tension as well as compression, across rats, rabbits and calves.
  - Sustained 0.1 MPa gives ~10% growth-plate strain.
  - Arrest occurs at ~0.6 MPa (extrapolated), 0.5–1 MPa measured; a staple stress of ~1 MPa in human tibia.
  - Compression for 12 of 24 h gives half the effect.
- **Tension/distraction** thickens the growth plate (mainly the hypertrophic zone: more cells, taller and larger), often with disrupted columns; proliferation is usually unchanged. Cyclic tension in the cranial base increased proliferation (Wang & Mao 2002).
- **Dynamic loading:**
  - Ohashi 2002: rat ulna at 4–17 N, 2 Hz, suppressed growth dose-dependently.
  - Noonan 2004: lamb tibia microtransducers showed **90% of lengthening happens during recumbency**.
  - Tennis players: playing-side ulna +3% and 2nd metacarpal +3.7% longer (Krahl 1994). Exercise level barely affects final length (Niehoff 2004).
- **Mechanical properties:**
  - Growth plate is ~10× more compliant axially than transversely. The hypertrophic zone is least stiff.
  - Periphery is less stiff and more permeable than the centre. Reserve zone is stiffer.
- **Carter & Wong:** octahedral (shear) stress promotes ossification; hydrostatic stress preserves cartilage.
- **Shear/torsion effects on growth are almost unstudied.** Moreland 1980 found no growth change but deformed columns.
- Open questions the authors flag: time-varying volume, water content and **osmolarity** effects; possible length-sensing by **periosteal tension**.

**What it establishes for post-fusion growth.**
- The output transfer function of a growth cartilage is mechanically tunable: *tension raises output (hypertrophic height), compression suppresses it*, linearly and without threshold. Any engineered adult growth front would follow the same law, so it should be held in **net axial tension or reduced compression**, especially during recumbency.
- Hypertrophic swelling is the main actuator, which suggests osmotic/volume manipulation as a lever.
- Periosteal tension as a length sensor: in an adult, the periosteum is a mechanical constraint and possibly a sensor. Releasing or straining it could be part of a mechanism.
