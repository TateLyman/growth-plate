# PMID 36870529 — Excess Growth Hormone Triggers Inflammation-Associated Arthropathy, Subchondral Bone Loss, and Arthralgia

Poudel SB, Ruff RR, Yildirim G, Dixit M, Michot B, Gibbs JL, Ortiz SD, Kopchick JJ, Kirsch T, Yakar S. *Am J Pathol* 2023;193(6):829-842. PMC10284029. doi:10.1016/j.ajpath.2023.02.010

**What I read:** the full published PDF (publisher open-access copy, 5.4 MB, text-extracted) — abstract, Introduction, Materials and Methods, all six Results sections, the full Discussion and summary, and the reference list. Figures were read via their legends and the Results text, not the images. Not an abstract read.

## Why I read it
This is the only accessible test of the **acromegaly route** — the one human state in which a post-closure skeleton visibly keeps enlarging. The question was whether driving the whole-body "grow" set-point via GH/IGF-1 *grows* adult cartilage or *destroys* it.

## Model
**1-year-old bovine-GH (bGH) transgenic mice vs wild type**, both sexes — lifelong, massive GH excess from birth. Knee joint: micro-CT of distal femur subchondral bone and proximal tibia subchondral plate, safranin-O histology with OARSI scoring, immunohistochemistry, and pain behaviour (von Frey, Hargreaves).

## Exact numbers
- **Femoral length +15% (male) and +19% (female)**; body weight +45% and +77%; serum IGF-1 significantly raised.
- **Pain.** Decreased cage activity from as early as **3 months**. Significantly increased mechanical sensitivity (von Frey) in both sexes; significantly increased thermal sensitivity in males.
- **Subchondral bone lost, not gained.** Male bGH: reduced BV/TV (sex × genotype p = 0.003), reduced trabecular thickness, reduced subchondral BMD (p = 0.012), with increased TRAP staining (osteoclasts). Female bGH: reduced Tb.Th but *increased* trabecular number (p = 0.014), so BV/TV held. **Both sexes: significantly decreased subchondral-plate BMD** at the proximal tibia.
- **Articular cartilage degraded.** Significantly higher OARSI cumulative scores in femur and tibia, worst medially. "Severe loss of matrix from the articular cartilage."
- **The transcriptional signature is the wrong one.** **SOX9 significantly decreased** in both sexes — the factor "required for protecting the AC from degradation and prevents chondrocyte dedifferentiation to osteoblast-like cells." **RUNX2 significantly increased.** Collagen X staining increased.
- **Osteophytosis** — increased cumulative scores in both sexes, femur and tibia, medial and lateral.
- **Ectopic chondrogenesis in 100% of bGH mice** of both sexes (meniscus and synovial membrane), vs 33% of WT males and 50% of WT females, and milder in those.
- **Inflammation.** Significantly increased iNOS and **NLRP3** inflammasome in articular chondrocytes. Synovial hyperplasia with increased Ki-67 and decreased p53.

## What it establishes for post-fusion growth
**It kills the GH/acromegaly route as a cartilage-growth strategy, and it is worth having killed.** The intuition was reasonable — acromegaly is the body deciding post-closure to enlarge its skeleton, and joint-space widening in acromegaly is adult cartilage thickening visible on plain radiographs. What this paper shows is that the *mechanism* is wrong in detail:

- The growth it produces is **hypertrophy-plus-degradation, not controlled elongation**: COLX and RUNX2 up with SOX9 down is the osteoarthritic conversion signature, not the growth-plate programme. §3 of ACTIVATION.md wants hypertrophic *volume* in an intact template; this gives hypertrophic *markers* in a disintegrating one.
- **Subchondral bone mineral density falls.** Any length gained above a weakening plate is structurally worthless.
- **Ectopic chondrogenesis at 100% penetrance** is precisely the §18.7b failure mode: open the actuator in tissue acting on no positional instruction and you get cartilage in the wrong places — here, in meniscus and synovium.
- It hurts, measurably, from three months.

So GH excess is a genuine goal changer that reaches adult cartilage and moves it in the destructive direction. **This is the clearest demonstration in the project of why a systemic "grow" signal without a positional instruction is not enough** — and why §18's ordering (positional layer upstream) is the right one.

## The honest counterweight — the human data disagree in one important way
The authors cite **Wassenaar et al. 2011** (*Ann Rheum Dis* 70:320-325): **"Arthropathy in long-term cured acromegaly is characterised by osteophytes WITHOUT joint space narrowing: a comparison with generalised osteoarthritis."** And **Colao et al. 1998** (*JCEM* 83:2121-2125): joint thickening in acromegaly is **reversible**.

In humans, then, the acromegalic joint keeps its cartilage thickness while ordinary OA loses it, and the thickening tracks GH activity. That is not what the bGH mouse shows. Three reasons to prefer caution over either:
1. **bGH mice have open growth plates and grew 15–19% longer.** The authors themselves flag this as a confound: "the mechanisms by which accelerated growth in bGH mice affects OA development are yet to be defined, but they need to be taken in consideration when using the bGH mouse as a model for acromegalic arthropathy."
2. Lifelong transgenic overexpression from birth is not adult-onset adenoma.
3. GH/IGF-1 is reported **anti**-inflammatory elsewhere — in a rabbit OA model IGF-1 delivery inhibited NF-κB inflammation and prevented cartilage degradation (their ref 53) — while acromegalic patients show a pro-inflammatory state (refs 54, 55). The authors state plainly that "the impact of excess GH on systemic or synovial joint inflammation was not tested directly."

**So the verdict is: GH excess is not a usable lever for longitudinal cartilage growth, and the mouse evidence for active destruction is strong but confounded by open-plate growth.** The human radiographic literature (Colao 1998, Wassenaar 2011, 22170798, 28348071, 14764768 — all still unread, all paywalled) is what would settle whether adult human acromegalic cartilage thickening is real, maintained, and of any axial consequence. Those remain on the needed list and I should not resolve this from the mouse alone.

## Anti-levers (this paper is almost entirely a list of them)
- **GH / IGF-1 excess, systemic** — matrix loss, SOX9 down, RUNX2 up, subchondral BMD down, osteophytes, 100% ectopic chondrogenesis, arthralgia from 3 months.
- A "grow" signal with no positional instruction — ectopic cartilage.

## Levers
- Only indirectly: **SOX9 preservation** is identified as the protective axis ("required for protecting the AC from degradation"). Anything that raises hypertrophic output must hold SOX9 up, not let it fall. That is a constraint on every candidate in COMPOUNDS.md, and I had not stated it as one.
- NLRP3 and iNOS as the inflammatory mediators, i.e. the things to suppress if a hypertrophic programme is ever driven in adult cartilage deliberately.

## Honest limits
1. **Mouse, transgenic, lifelong, open plates.** Not post-closure, not human, not adult-onset.
2. Knee only. Nothing axial, nothing about vertebral endplate.
3. Inflammation was inferred from markers; systemic and synovial inflammation "was not tested directly."
4. No intervention arm — no GH antagonist, no pegvisomant, no rescue.
5. Figures read through legends and text only.

## Chase list
- **Wassenaar MJ et al. 2011, Ann Rheum Dis 70:320-325** — osteophytes *without* joint space narrowing in cured acromegaly. The strongest human counterpoint to this paper; now the priority item in the acromegaly block.
- **Colao A et al. 1998, JCEM 83:2121-2125** (PMID 9626149) — reversibility of joint thickening. Reversible means actively maintained.
- Maor G et al. 1989 (*Endocrinology* 125:1239) — "Human growth hormone enhances chondrogenesis and osteogenesis in a tissue culture system of chondroprogenitor cells," and Tsukazaki 1994 — GH directly stimulates articular chondrocyte growth. **Opposite sign to this paper, in vitro.** Worth reading to see whether the destructive phenotype is a whole-animal/inflammation effect rather than a cell-intrinsic one.
- Chrisman OD 1975 — "The effect of growth hormone on established cartilage lesions."
