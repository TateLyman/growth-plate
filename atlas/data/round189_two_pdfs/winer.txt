Abstract
Context: Our study explores the impact of human parathyroid hormone (PTH) 1-34 injections (PTH therapy) on growth, areal bone mineral 
density (BMD), and bone quality (measured by trabecular bone score, TBS) in hypoparathyroidism due to autoimmune polyendocrine 
syndrome type 1 (APS-1) or an activating variant of the calcium sensing receptor (CaR).
Objective: To assess associations of (1) age and PTH therapy duration with age-standardized Z-scores for height (HAZ), BMD (BMD-Z), and TBS 
(TBS-Z) in CaR or APS-1, and (2) APS-1 disease severity with BMD-Z and TBS-Z.
Methods: This secondary analysis pooled linear growth and lumbar spine (LS) dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry data from studies of 
hypoparathyroidism with mean baseline age of 13.7 ± 5.5 years. Comparing the 2 diagnostic etiologies (18 APS-1 and 9 CaR), we examined 
the impact of age and PTH duration on HAZ, LS-BMD-Z, and LS-TBS-Z using longitudinal mixed-effects modeling.
Results: During PTH therapy, mean HAZ remained below 0 in the APS-1 group at all ages, whereas HAZ increased in the CaR group (age by group 
interaction P < .0001). Mean LS-BMD-Z were normal (BMD-Z: 0 ± 1) for both groups. Mean LS-TBS-Z were near or above 0 and differed by group; 
CaR showed an upward trajectory according to time on PTH whereas the APS-1 group maintained a LS-TBS-Z of approximately 0 (time by group 
interaction P = .02). The APS-1 group with greater disease severity (≥7 manifestations) had lower LS-BMD-Z and LS-TBS-Z than the less severe 
APS-1 or CaR groups.
Conclusion: Our study highlights distinct growth and BMD patterns in APS-1 and CaR and underscores the need for careful monitoring and 
tailored treatment strategies to optimize growth and bone health.

Hypoparathyroidism is a rare disease of mineral metabolism 
of heterogeneous etiologies. Over 3 decades, we studied 
both adults and children with a wide array of etiologies and 
severity of disease (1). Our study of hypoparathyroidism in 
children has focused mainly on 2 genetic forms of hypopara­
thyroidism: autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1 
(APS-1), also known as autoimmune polyendocrinopathy, 
candidiasis, and ectodermal dystrophy (APECED), and hypo­
parathyroidism resulting from an activating pathogenic vari­
ant of the calcium sensing receptor, referred to here as CaR. 
The pathophysiology of both diseases was identified 3 decades 
ago with the discovery of variants in the AIRE and CaSR

genes, the underlying etiology of APS-1 and CaR, respectively. 
Conventional therapy of these conditions with oral calcitriol 
and calcium supplements (conventional therapy) can lead to 
excessive urine calcium excretion, renal damage, and de­
creased renal function (1, 2). Estimates of hypoparathyroid­
ism prevalence in the United States, Denmark, and Italy 
include predominantly adults with surgically induced hypo­
parathyroidism are all in the range of 23 to 37 per 100 000 
(3). The estimated prevalence of APS-1 in the United States 
is 2.7 per 100 000 and is higher in Persian Jews (1:9000), 
Sardinians (1:14 400), and Finns (1:25 000) (4). Based on a 
US cohort predominantly comprising individuals of northern 
European descent, the prevalence of CaR is estimated at 3.9 
per 100 000 (5).
Patients with CaR have hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, 
hyperphosphatemia, and hypercalciuria with a high risk of 
nephrocalcinosis and compromised renal function but are 
otherwise generally healthy. By contrast, APS-1 is a multisys­
tem autoimmune disease potentially affecting every organ in 
the body. By late adolescence, parathyroid insufficiency 
(86% of patients), adrenal insufficiency (70%), and gastro­
intestinal tract dysfunction (77%) are evident in most patients 
with APS-1 (6). Several APS-1 disease manifestations or their 
therapies, including hypogonadism, chronic malabsorption, 
and adrenal insufficiency requiring long-term glucocorticoid 
therapy, may adversely affect areal bone mineral density 
(BMD). Isolated intestinal malabsorption may alter mineral 
metabolism leading to osteopenia in adults or insufficient 
bone accrual in children (7, 8). Chronic glucocorticoids 
needed to treat chronic illness in childhood may have adverse 
effects on the growth plate and bone formation leading to 
growth failure and secondary osteoporosis (9). Although post­
surgical hypoparathyroidism in adults is typically associated 
with increased bone mass, Mäkitie and colleagues reported 
low BMD in one-third of their adult APS-1 cohort (10). 
Subsequently, the same group reported (11) low trabecular 
volumetric BMD measured by peripheral quantitative com­
puted tomography in patients with APS-1 with more severe 
disease (≥7 concurrent disease manifestations).
Our studies of twice-daily synthetic human parathyroid 
(PTH) 1-34 subcutaneous injections (PTH therapy) in adults 
and children with various etiologies of hypoparathyroidism 
have demonstrated that PTH therapy is more effective in 
maintaining eucalcemia with reduced urine calcium excretion 
than conventional therapy (1). We previously reported normal 
bone accrual and linear growth velocity in 9 children with 
CaR and 5 children with APS-1 aged >5 years and treated 
with PTH therapy (2). These findings, however, are limited 
by the small number of children, especially for APS-1 patients 
with severe disease.
Trabecular bone score (TBS) has been used to assess bone 
health in adults with hypoparathyroidism and is derived 
from the dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) image of 
the lumbar spine. It is a textural index related to bone micro­
architecture based on the pixel gray level variations in the 
DXA image. TBS reveals information not captured from 
standard DXA analysis and provides information on skeletal 
microarchitecture, which relates to bone strength and quality 
(12, 13). A higher TBS in adults is associated with better, 
fracture-resistant microarchitecture, whereas low TBS is asso­
ciated with higher risk for fracture and poor microarchitec­
ture (13). Silva reported normal TBS in adults with 
predominantly postsurgical hypoparathyroidism treated 
with conventional therapy (14). In a review of studies of sec­
ondary osteoporosis in adults, Ulivieri concluded that TBS 
provided a more reliable prediction of fracture risk than lum­
bar spine BMD (13). TBS reference values for children and 
adolescents provide a valuable tool to identify children with 
compromised bone health either due to chronic illness or the 
associated treatments beyond what is measured by standard 
DXA-derived BMD (15). To date, TBS has not been reported 
in children with hypoparathyroidism.
Expanding on our prior report, the objectives of this study 
were to assess age-related patterns of linear growth and bone 
density, including assessments of bone quality by TBS, in chil­
dren with CaR or APS-1. We examined whether duration of 
PTH therapy was associated with growth and bone outcomes. 
In addition, we examined the impact of disease severity on 
these outcomes in children with APS-1 given their greater vul­
nerability to diminished bone health due to chronic illness and 
multisystem organ failure. We hypothesized that the effects of 
age and PTH treatment duration on height, BMD, and TBS 
would differ between the 2 hypoparathyroidism etiologies 
and further predicted differences in bone architecture accord­
ing to disease severity within the APS-1 subgroups.
Materials and Methods
This is a secondary analysis of pooled data on patients with 
hypoparathyroidism treated with twice-daily PTH injection 
therapy from 2 studies previously described (1, 2, 6). In a long- 
term study of PTH treatment of hypoparathyroidism of various 
etiologies, height and BMD measurements by DXA were ob­
tained every 6 months beginning with the initiation of PTH 
treatment (2). Patients in the second study (6) had growth 
and BMD measurements at 1 time point at varying times after 
initiation of PTH treatment by their local physicians. All pa­
tients were enrolled into an institution review board–approved 
protocol at the NIH and signed informed consents 
(92-CH-0011; 11-I-0187). We included patients with biochem­
ically confirmed hypoparathyroidism due to APS-1 or CaR. 
Patients with APS-1 were diagnosed based on clinical manifes­
tations with confirmation by AIRE gene testing. All patients 
with CaR had a pathogenic variant of the CaSR gene. BMD 
measures in 4 children (2 CaR and 2 APS-1) measured prior 
to the year 2000 were excluded as they were performed on a 
DXA machine not compatible with current reference norms 
(16, 17). Two APS-1 patients received daily growth hormone 
injections and were excluded from the analysis. Our resultant 
analytical sample included 18 patients with APS-1 and 9 pa­
tients with CaR. We restricted bone analyses to data collected 
up to 5.98 years of PTH treatment, due to diminishing numbers 
of observations for both etiologies beyond that point.
Data abstracted from the medical chart included height, 
duration of disease, demographics, dates of PTH therapy, 
and comorbidities. Height was measured using a wall- 
mounted, Harpenden stadiometer. Height for age Z-scores 
(HAZ) were calculated based on the CDC 2000 growth charts 
(18) using the ZANTHRO procedure in Stata 16.1 (Stata 
Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). For individuals older 
than 20 years, HAZ was computed using reference values at 
age 20 years. APS-1 disease manifestations were documented 
during evaluation at the NIH Clinical Center, and the disease 
surveillance database was regularly updated (6).
Lumbar spine DXA scans were acquired with Hologic 
(Marlborough, MA, USA) bone densitometers (QDR4500A

Statistical Analysis
Descriptive statistics included means and standard deviations 
for continuous, normally distributed variables, and medians 
and interquartile ranges for other continuous measures. 
Categorical variables were described as percentages.

BMD-Z, and TBS-Z. Age was grouped as 5 to 7.9 years, 8 to

ical ages when slower vs rapid changes in linear growth or bone 
accrual were expected to occur. To test whether age trends dif­
fered by diagnostic group, we included an age group by diag­
nostic group interaction term. Results of these analyses were 
presented visually as least square means. Similar analyses

BMD and TBS According to APS-1 Disease Severity
Disease severity in the APS-1 group, defined according to 
numbers of disease manifestations at baseline, was equally 
divided between mild (3-6 disease manifestations, n = 9) and 
severe (≥7 manifestations, n = 9) disease. Two patients from 
the mild group progressed to the more severe group between 
4 and 5 years of PTH therapy. BMD-Z according to disease 
severity, duration of PTH therapy, and adjusted for age re­
vealed a higher BMD in the milder APS-1 and CaR groups 
than BMD in the APS-1 group with severe disease (interaction 
P = .0006; Fig. 3A). Similarly, the TBS-Z in the milder APS-1 
and CaR groups were higher than in the APS-1 group with se­
vere disease (interaction P = .003; Fig. 3B).
Height Adjustment of Bone Mineral Density Z-scores
Sensitivity analysis comparing BMD-Z with and without height 
Z-score adjustment revealed an approximately 0.3 SD upward 
shift with height Z-score adjustment when examining BMD 
patterns according to age or duration of PTH therapy (Fig. 4A
and 4B, unadjusted vs Figure 4C and 4D, height adjusted). 
Height adjustment eliminated the significant differences between 
diagnostic groups when comparing spine BMD-Z by diagnostic 
group and years on PTH, suggesting the differences between the 
groups were related to disparities in height (Fig. 4B, not height 
adjusted P = .006, vs Fig. 4D, height adjusted P = .1).

hypoparathyroidism, CaR and APS-1, regarding linear 
growth, BMD, and TBS, a measure of bone microarchitecture.

Our conclusions are consistent with our prior study dem­
onstrating PTH treatment was associated with normal BMD 
according to age in children for both etiologies (2). The trend 
toward BMD-Z in the high-normal or above-normal range in 
our young adult patients (age >19 years) is consistent with 
what is reported in other studies of adults with hypopara­
thyroidism (10, 14, 26). Our study, however, could not re­
veal the maximal benefit of PTH on BMD, as patients were 
not uniformly followed to peak bone mass. We further dem­
onstrate that PTH therapy was associated with an increase in 
spine TBS-Z for the CaR group and not the APS-1 group. 
Bone architecture deficits were apparent only in patients 
with more severe APS-1 whose TBS-Z were low compared 
with milder cases of APS-1. Our TBS results were consistent 
with the findings of Laakso et al, who described higher 
prevalence of fractures and low trabecular and volumetric 
bone density on peripheral quantitative computed tomog­
raphy in a cohort of mostly adults with severe APS-1 (11).
Our findings indicate that TBS appears to mirror spine 
BMD values. Compromised bone architecture may be the 
underlying cause of increased risk of vertebral fracture in 
adults with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism (26). In other 
chronic illnesses such as type 1 diabetes, osteogenesis imper­
fecta or renal failure, TBS has served as a tool to detect in­
creased fracture risk and bone pathology in adults and 
children beyond the standard DXA measurement of bone 
mass (13, 27-29). Bone disease in children with APS-1 is likely 
to emerge along a continuum of damage depending on the se­
verity of each disease manifestation and timing and duration 
of interventions. To detect subtle bone changes that may be 
present in the milder cases of APS-1, however, would require 
a larger sample size than what we had in our study.
The small number of patients limited our ability to detect 
subtle deficiencies in growth and bone health and did not per­
mit testing differences in linear growth and bone health with 
sex as a covariate. Furthermore, a larger cohort followed to 
peak bone mass would better reflect the full benefit of long- 
term PTH therapy on bone. We studied young adults in the 
APS-1 group and not in the CaR group. Therefore, our find­
ings may only be generalizable to the age groups studied in 
each etiology. Although age was considered in the analyses, 
residual confounding effects due to age may be operative. 
An assessment of the potential beneficial effects of PTH ther­
apy on bone microarchitecture compared with conventional 
therapy with calcitriol and calcium was not part of our study 
and remains a knowledge gap for future investigation.
Our study revealed that the 2 genetic forms of hypopara­
thyroidism, APS-1 and CaR, have unique phenotypes in terms 
of growth, BMD, and bone architecture. Linear growth re­
mained compromised in APS-1, regardless of age or time on 
PTH. Except for the youngest age groups, PTH-treated pa­
tients with CaR had normal growth. TBS was normal in CaR 
and in the APS-1 group with fewer disease manifestations. 
TBS, however, was compromised in the APS-1 group with 
greater disease severity. Our findings should alert providers 
who care for children with APS-1 to monitor linear growth 
and skeletal health. The standard management of APS-1, cur­
rently focused on providing therapeutic interventions as dis­
ease symptoms emerge, may lead to compromised bone 
microarchitecture in childhood or early adulthood. Recent ad­
vances have led to a greater understanding of the underlying 
pathophysiology along with an awareness of the widespread 
phenotypic diversity of disease manifestations (6). Widely 
available genetic testing allows for timely diagnosis and the im­
plementation of early preventive therapy. Future directions in­
clude innovative approaches to APS-1 therapy with immune 
modulators, such as ruxolitinib, an inhibitor of Janus kinase 
1 and 2. This immunotherapy targets the underlying pathogen­
ic mechanism, which has been identified in APS-1 as excessive 
interferon-γ production by AIRE-deficient T-cells (30). This 
approach may reduce the need for glucocorticoid therapy, 
with its adverse effects on bone and linear growth, and has 
the potential to rescue target organs, such as the gastrointes­
tinal tract, from irreparable damage (31).
