and cartilage histomorphometric parameters, and on
osteoprotegerin and RANKL mRNA expression and proteins

Abstract
We have studied the effects of the treatment
with corticosterone (CORT), parathyroid hormone (PTH),
or both (CORT + PTH), and of their withdrawal (CORT-
rec and CORT + PTH-rec), on the osteoprotegerin (OPG)
and
receptor
activator
of
nuclear
factor-kB
ligand
(RANKL) localization and expression and on histomor-
phometric parameters in primary and secondary spongiosa
of rat femur and tibia metaphyses. In the secondary
spongiosa of the CORT group, the bone remodeling and the
OPG/RANKL mRNA ratio decreased. In the PTH group,
the bone turnover and the structural and connectivity
indices increased, and the OPG/RANKL mRNA ratio fell;
this ratio rose, however, in the primary spongiosa. In the
CORT + PTH group, remodeling values intermediate be-
tween those of the CORT and PTH groups, were detected
in the secondary spongiosa, where OPG and RANKL
mRNA rose. Return towards control values was found in
the recovery groups. The Cartilage Growth Plate Width
was reduced in the CORT and CORT + PTH groups and
returned to normal values in the recovery groups, while it
was not affected by PTH. Independently of treatments,
both
OPG
and
RANKL
mRNA
and
proteins
were
co-localized in the same cartilage and bone cells and in
several bone marrow cells. In conclusion, the catabolic
effects induced by CORT treatment occur together with an
OPG fall and a RANKL rise. In the PTH group in which
the bone turnover increase, the OPG and RANKL mRNA
expressions differ in the primary and secondary spongiosa,
conﬁrming that the bone tissue in these sites can have
different metabolic trends.

Introduction
Bone is a dynamic tissue that is continually renewed by a
balanced process of bone formation and resorption, known
as bone remodeling. This process depends on the coordi-
nated action of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, whose con-
ﬂicting activities are coupled, with the resorption of bone
preceding its formation. Some mediators that govern the
cross-talk between osteoblasts/stromal cells and osteoclast
hemopoietic precursor cells have recently been identiﬁed.
Osteoprotegerin (OPG), a soluble member of the Tumor
Necrosis Factor receptor family (Simonet et al. 1997),
inhibits osteoclast differentiation by acting as a decoy
receptor for the receptor activator of nuclear factor-kB
ligand (RANKL). It is expressed by stromal cells and
osteoblasts (Lacey et al. 1998). It prevents RANKL from
binding to, or activating, the receptor activator of nuclear
factor-kB (RANK) located on the pre-osteoclastic cell
surface. RANKL and OPG also act as regulators of vas-
cular cell metabolism and calciﬁcation (Hofbauer and

Shopped 2004b). Their importance in bone metabolism is
shown by the fact that transgenic mice with excessive OPG
gene expression, and knock out mice with defective pro-
duction of RANKL, have osteopetrotic phenotypes (Sim-
onet et al. 1997; Kong et al. 1999).
A convergence hypothesis has been postulated (Hof-
bauer et al. 2000a), based on the focusing effects that
hormones seem to have on OPG and RANKL. In humans,
prolonged high-dose treatment with glucocorticoids (GCs)
induces osteoporosis as a result of impaired bone forma-
tion, and/or enhanced bone resorption, but how GCs affect
bone remodeling is still a controversial issue (Patschan
et al. 2001). GCs induce osteoblast apoptosis and seem to
increase osteoclast survival and activity (Rehman and Lane
2003a); these effects appear to be mostly mediated by the
RANKL/OPG system (Sasaki et al. 2002b; Khosla 2001).
Parathyroid hormone (PTH) is known to be a potent
anabolic factor. It has recently been conﬁrmed that inter-
mittent treatment with low doses of PTH rises the trabec-
ular and cortical bone mass (Nishida et al. 1994; Okimoto
et al. 1998; Morley et al. 2001), and markedly positive
PTH effects have been recorded in GC-induced osteopo-
rosis in humans (Robin and Bilezikian 2002; Rehman et al.
2003b). Thus, the hormone has been recognized as one of
the emerging remedies available to the pharmacotherapy of
osteoporosis (Doggrell 2003).
Both GCs and PTH affect OPG and RANKL gene
expression, but how this occurs has not yet been well de-
ﬁned. OPG falls in GC-induced osteoporosis (Aubin and
Bonnelye 2000; Sasaki et al. 2001a), PTH causes up-reg-
ulation of RANKL and down-regulation of OPG, respec-
tively, as documented in humans (Seck et al. 2001) and rats
(Yanfei et al. 2001), and in cultures of osteoblast-like or
bone marrow stromal cells (Vidal et al. 1998; Hisatomo
et al. 2002), or murine bone marrow cells (Lee and Lorenzo
1999; Locklin et al. 2003).
Apart from the results of in vivo studies on both OPG
and RANKL mRNA and/or protein in conditions of oste-
olysis induced by ovariectomy (Ikeda et al. 2001; Miyazaki
et al. 2004) or after continuous PTH administration (Yanfei
et al. 2001), no other data are available on the expression of
these factors when rats are treated with intermittent PTH
and/or GCs.
In the present study, which continues a previously re-
ported (Silvestrini et al. 2000a) experimental investigation
on GC-induced bone loss, we have investigated whether
the repeated exposure of young adult rats to a high corti-
costerone (CORT) treatment, to intermittent PTH admin-
istration, or to combined treatments with CORT and PTH,
affect OPG and RANKL protein localization and expres-
sion in the cartilage and bone cells, also in relation to the
histomorphometric indicators of bone remodeling. To dis-
tinguish between the immediate and prolonged effects of
treatments, rats were studied 24 h or 14 days after the last
hormonal doses.
Materials and methods
Animals and treatment
Experiments have been carried out on 3 month male Wistar
rats (Charles River, Calco, Italy) weighing between 225
and 250 g at the beginning of the study. They were kept on
a 12-h light/dark cycle (light on 07:00 a.m.–07:00 p.m.),
with free access to food (standard laboratory chow diet;
Mucedola, Settimo Milanese, Italy) and water. Rat body
weight was measured every day. All procedures were ap-
proved by the local Ethical Committee, and performed
according to the NIH (National Institutes of Health) Guide
for care and use of laboratory animals.
The animals were divided into six groups, each com-
prising ten animals:
The CORT group was treated with 10 mg/day of CORT
subcutaneously (s.c.), as previously described (Silvestrini
et al. 2000a) for 21 consecutive days. The PTH group was
s.c. injected three times a week with 80 (lg/kg synthetic
hPTH (1–34) (Bachem Biochemicals, Torrance, CA, USA)
dissolved in 100 mM NaCl, 25 mM citrate/phosphate
buffer (pH 5.0) containing 0.05% Tween-80 (Kobayashi
et al. 1999). The CORT + PTH group was treated with
both CORT and PTH for the same period and using the
modalities reported above. The recovery groups (CORT-
rec and CORT + PTH-rec) were allowed to recover for 2
weeks after the end of CORT and PTH + CORT treat-
ments. Control (C) group animals were treated with the
vehicles for 3 weeks.
Sample preparation

the jugular vein, and the animals were killed by decapita-
tion. The distal end of femurs and the proximal end of
tibiae were immediately removed and ﬁxed. The left fe-
murs and the right tibiae were processed differently for
immunohistochemistry (IHC) and in situ hybridization
(ISH) techniques, respectively, as previously reported
(Silvestrini et al. 2000a). Sequential sections were exam-
ined to allow comparison between OPG and RANKL
labeling localization.
The right femur distal ends were used for morphological
examination and conventional histomorphometric analysis.
They were ﬁxed with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M
phosphate buffer pH 7.2 at 4C. They were then cut lon-

Quantitative measurements
The measurement of conventional bone histomorphometric
indices in the secondary metaphyseal spongiosa and the
Growth Plate Width were carried out as previously re-
ported (Silvestrini et al. 2000a).
The count of OPG and RANKL-immunolabeled osteo-
cytes by IHC was achieved at 1:60 OPG and 1:80 RANKL
antibody dilutions, with which the maximum visibility of
positive osteocytes was obtained. At least three non-con-
secutive sections were obtained from each femur specimen.
No count of labeled osteoblasts was attempted, because of
their very low numbers after CORT treatment. Numbers of
osteocytes, both labeled (i.e., showing cytoplasmic immu-
nolabeling) and unlabeled, were counted in the secondary
spongiosa of femur methaphysis, within a zone similar to
that used for conventional histomorphometry. The results
were expressed as numbers of OPG and RANKL immu-
nolabeled osteocytes per mm2 of bone area (immuno-po-
sitive osteocyte density). Measurements were obtained
using an interactive image analyzer, consisting of a color
video-equipped computer with a dedicated software (Image
Analysis System 2000, Delta Sistemi, Rome, Italy), which
was linked to the microscope by a videocamera. Each se-
lected microscopic ﬁeld was projected by the videocamera
onto the screen of the computer where, with a mouse,
numbers of cells were obtained by individually clicking on
them, and bone area was calculated by tracing the perim-
eter of the structure. Such a semi-automatic measurement
method has the advantage of being more rapid and less
tedious than the oldest histomorphometric method, based
on simple eyepiece graticules.

Statistics
Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Dif-
ferences between groups were tested by one-way ANOVA.
After an assessment of signiﬁcant differences between
groups, these were compared using Tukey’s multiple
comparison test. Fisher’s LSD Multiple-comparison test
was carried out with reference to the body weight gain and
the CORT plasma assay results. A value of p < 0.05 was
considered signiﬁcant.

In CORT-treated rats, plasma CORT levels were signiﬁ-

2.9 lg/dL) and CORT + PTH (41.8 ± 5.6 lg/dL) groups.

3.9 ± 1.1 lg/dL and 4.5 ± 0.9 lg/dL (p < 0.05 vs CORT
and CORT + PTH mean values).

The

Changes in serum Ca and Pi values in the CORT group
conﬁrmed the efﬁcacy of CORT treatment. Serum Ca did
not increase in the PTH group, because the doses admin-
istered were insufﬁcient to induce a pathological condition
of hyperparathyroidism.
Histomorphometric analysis demonstrated the effects of
treatments on bone remodeling, and showed that the
inhibitory effect of CORT on bone was only partly coun-
tered by the anabolic effect of PTH.
Corticosterone treatment inhibited both bone formation
and resorption; despite the reduction in bone remodeling,
BV/TV and the indices of connectivity did not change sig-
niﬁcantly, probably because the length of CORT treatment
(21 days) was shorter than a single remodeling period, which
in rats lasts about 35 days (Baron et al. 1984). PTH admin-
istration induced signiﬁcant rises in bone resorption vari-
ables, and slighter, not signiﬁcant increases in OS/BS and
Ob.S/BS. Because no ﬂuorescent markers of bone formation
had been administered, no secure assessment of the degree of
bone formation could be formulated; in any case, an
enhancement of bone formation in PTH rats was conﬁrmed
by a signiﬁcant rise in Tb.Th, together with a tendency
toward a rise in BV/TV and Tb.N and toward a fall in Tb.Sp.
The CORT-mediated fall in cartilage growth width is
partly due to the previously shown (Silvestrini et al. 2000a)
increase in apoptotic terminal hypertrophic chondrocytes,
and to a probable pro-apoptotic non-genomic effect (Pats-
chan et al. 2001); moreover, the observation that, in vitro,
GCs induce a generalized fall in cell population (Maor and
Silbermann 1981) suggests that CORT impairs the coupled
mechanism of cell proliferation and death. The absence of
PTH effect on growth plate width could partly be related to
the age of the rats (12 weeks) and partly to the dose used,
as discussed above; it has been demonstrated that it is only
the cartilage of young rats (6 weeks ) that responds to PTH
(Ogawa et al. 2002).

rec groups, were presumably due to an ‘‘artifact’’ of
longitudinal bone growth attributable to CORT treatment.
In fact, under CORT administration, calciﬁed cartilage
spicules are not resorbed, so they accumulate inside the
secondary cancellous metaphyseal bone (Li et al. 1996).
This ‘‘artifact’’ was not histomorphometrically detected in
the CORT group, because the animals were sacriﬁced too
early. In the CORT + PTH-rec group, PTH could have
contributed to the above results through its anabolic effects.
In this study, the concomitant rise of OPG and
RANKL positive osteocytes, signiﬁcant in the CORT
groups and not in the PTH group, and the fact that total
numbers of osteocytes were unchanged, suggest a major
sensitivity of osteocytes to the CORT treatment and seem
to point to an extension of their lifespan or a delay in
their maturation, probably due to the inhibitory effects of
CORT on bone cells. It is well known that these cells
play an important role in osteoclastogenesis, i.e., as sen-
sors to hormonal signals; in this connection, an in vitro
experiment showed that osteocyte-like cells express OPG
and RANKL, and a surface molecule seems to control
osteocyte signaling in the osteoclast-forming process
(Zhao et al. 2002). An increase in numbers of osteocytes
expressing OPG mRNA has also been shown in aged and
ovariectomized rats (Ikeda et al. 2001). The discrepancy
between gene expression and the immuno-positive oste-
ocyte density may be due to the cellular heterogeneity in
the examined samples which, besides osteocytes, contain
variable amounts and different types of bone marrow
cells.
OPG and RANKL-positive osteocyte numbers fell in the
recovery groups vs the CORT group suggesting a return to
normal values after the treatment. The OPG-positive
osteocyte
density
was
signiﬁcantly
higher
in
the
CORT + PTH-rec than in the CORT-rec groups, as if the
effect of PTH was attenuated by CORT. In this regard, a
PTH-mediated anti-apoptotic action (Bellido et al. 2003;
Rouleau et al. 1990) and, more recently, a reduction of
sclerostin protein, an osteocyte secreted inhibitor of
osteoblastogenesis (Bellido et al 2005), have been shown.
Our ﬁndings that OPG gene expression values, contrary
to those of RANKL, rose after PTH and fell after CORT
treatment in an area rich of osteoblastic cells, such as the
primary metaphyseal spongiosa, are in agreement with the
morphological results showing a CORT inhibitory action
and a PTH anabolic effect in this bone site. Moreover, the
opposite trend of OPG and RANKL mRNA expression
induced by PTH in the secondary metaphyseal spongiosa,
appears related to different responsiveness to the hormone
of woven bone (primary spongiosa) cells with respect
to cells of lamellar bone (secondary spongiosa). The
composition of bone matrix itself varies between the
primary and secondary spongiosa (Gorski 1998).
The PTH-induced effects on RANKL and OPG mRNA,
in the secondary spongiosa, are in accordance with results
of in vivo (Onyia et al. 2000) and in vitro (Lee and Lorenzo
1999) studies. No other in vivo data, apart from those
obtained through the real-time PCR analysis of OPG and
RANKL mRNA in osteoblastic cells (Huang et al. 2004;
Okahashi et al. 2004; Cao et al. 2003), and in bone mouse
(Cao et al. 2003), are available in rats, either in controls or
after CORT and/or PTH treatments.
In the primary metaphyseal spongiosa of CORT + PTH-
treated rats, the RANKL mRNA fall in respect to the
CORT group could be due to the anabolic PTH effect. In
the secondary spongiosa of the same rat group, the ratio
between OPG mRNA and RANKL mRNA was similar to
that in controls, although their values were higher. These
data suggest a PTH-induced activation of bone turnover,
partly inhibited, however, by the catabolic action of CORT,
as shown by the signiﬁcant reduction in bone resorption
and formation variables vs the PTH group.
In conclusion, the co-localization of OPG and RANKL
expression in bone cells supports the hypothesis of their
coupled action in bone formation and resorption mecha-
nisms. The CORT catabolic effects on bone, only partly
countered by PTH, are in line with the OPG mRNA levels
lower than those of RANKL, both in the primary, and
secondary spongiosa. PTH treatment has different effects
in these sites, probably due to the major responsiveness of
woven bone cells to the anabolic effect of the hormone.
The recovery groups have clearly shown the reversibility of
hormonal effects. The increase in OPG and RANKL
immunolabeled osteocytes in CORT-treated rats and the
return towards control values in the recovery groups
underline the role of these cells in the bone metabolism and
the need for further studies on their role in bone physio-
pathology.
