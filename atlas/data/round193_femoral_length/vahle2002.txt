Skeletal Changes in Rats Given Daily Subcutaneous Injections
of Recombinant Human Parathyroid Hormone (1-34) for 2 Years
and Relevance to Human Safety
JOHN L. VAHLE,1 MASAHIKO SATO,1 GERALD G. LONG,1 JAMIE K. YOUNG,1 PAUL C. FRANCIS,1
JEFFERY A. ENGELHARDT,1 MICHAEL S. WESTMORE,1 YANFEI LINDA MA,1 AND JAMES B. NOLD2
1Lilly Research Laboratories, Greeneld, Indiana, and
2Pathology Associates—A Charles River Company, Durham, North Carolina
ABSTRACT
Fischer 344 rats (60/sex/group) were given daily subcutaneous injections of recombinant human parathyroid hormone (PTH)(1-34) for 2 years at
doses of 0, 5, 30, or 75 lg/kg. Treatment caused substantial increases in bone mass consistent with the known pharmacologic effects of once-daily
administration. As determined by quantitative computed tomography (QCT) and histomorphometry, bone mass was markedly increased. Substantial
new bone formation resulted in a large decrease in marrow space accompanied by altered bone architecture. Bone proliferative lesions were observed in
all PTH(1-34)-treated groups. Osteosarcoma occurred in 3, 21, and 31 male rats and in 4, 12, and 23 female rats in the 5-, 30-, and 75-lg/kg treatment
groups, respectively. Focal osteoblast hyperplasia, osteoma, and osteoblastoma were much less frequent. Although the specic cellular or molecular
mechanisms responsible for the rat bone tumors have not been fully elucidated, the data suggest that these lesions resulted from the long duration of
treatment and the exaggerated pharmacologic response of the rat skeleton to daily treatment with PTH(1-34). Important differences between the rat
study and clinical use in adult humans suggest that the increased incidence of bone neoplasia in rats treated for 2 years is likely not predictive of an
increased risk of bone cancer in skeletally mature adult humans being given PTH(1-34) for a limited period of time in the treatment of osteoporosis.
Keywords.
Osteosarcoma; F344 rat; osteoma; osteoblastoma; carcinogenicity; QCT; bone mass; PTH.
INTRODUCTION
Human parathyroid hormone (1-34) is a 34-amino acid
peptide identical in sequence to the N-terminal portion of
naturally secreted human parathyroid hormone (PTH). In a
variety of in vivo and in vitro assays, PTH(1-34) produced
the same spectrum of biological responses in bone as elicited
by the native 84-amino acid hormone. Once-daily subcuta-
neous administration of PTH(1-34) stimulates osteoblasts to
produce new bone matrix on trabecular, endocortical, and
periosteal surfaces, resulting in substantial increases in bone
mineral density (BMD), bone mass, and biomechanical
strength (11, 18, 46). For these reasons, recombinant human
parathyroid hormone (rhPTH(1-34)) [hereinafter referred to
as PTH(1-34)] represents a novel therapeutic option to in-
crease bone mass and decrease fracture risk in adult patients
with osteoporosis. As is common practice in developing
therapeutic agents intended for chronic use, a 2-year study
in rats was conducted to assess the effects of near-lifetime
exposure to PTH(1-34). The skeletal effects observed in
this long-term study and their relevance to clinical use in
adult humans are the focus of the present article. The high
dose used in this study (75 lg/kg) was expected to be the
maximum tolerated dose based on the results of a 6-month
rat toxicology study and was over 200 times greater than the
therapeutic human dose (0.3 lg/kg) (35).

Bone proliferative lesions,

Osteosarcoma:
Osteosarcomas arose in a variety of sites
in the appendicular and axial skeleton (Table 4). In males,
the tibia was the most common site, yet in females the ver-
tebra was the most common site. Multiple osteosarcomas at
different bone sites within a given rat were diagnosed in 13
rats, or 14% of the rats diagnosed with osteosarcoma. Of
the 94 rats with osteosarcoma, 65% had gross bone nodules
or metastatic lesions. Metastases to soft tissues occurred in
36% of the rats with osteosarcoma. A few rats had metastatic
osteosarcoma without identication of a primary bone site.
Osteosarcomas were rst detected by clinical or gross obser-
vation of bone nodules after approximately 17 months in the
75-lg/kg group and by 20 months in the 5- and 30-lg/kg
groups. However, the earliest detected occurrence of an os-
teosarcoma was in a high-dose male rat that died of other
causes at approximately 13 months. In this rat, the osteosar-
coma was observed in a histologic section of vertebra during
routine evaluation at the end of thestudy. Histologically,these
neoplasmsincluded a broad spectrum of malignant osteoblas-
tic proliferative lesions typical of those previously observed
in rats and other species including osteoblastic, broblastic,
chondroblastic, and telangiectatic subtypes (13, 29). Many

This neoplasm has not been previously

DISCUSSION
Important ndings in this study were limited to the skeleton
and occurred in all groups treated with PTH(1-34). Although
increases in bone mass were expected based on results from
animal pharmacology studies, the excessive magnitude of
the increases in bone mass, as well as the focal hyperplastic
and neoplastic bone lesions, were not anticipated. Although
the specic cellular or molecular mechanisms responsible
for the rodent bone tumors have not been fully elucidated,
we conclude that the increased incidence of bone tumors re-
sulted from the prolonged duration of exposure in these rats
in combination with an exaggerated response of the rat skele-
ton to the bone-formation-enhancing effect of daily pulsatile
PTH(1-34) treatment. Because PTH(1-34) is intended to be
used by adult humans for the treatment of osteoporosis, it is
important to address the possible clinical relevance of these
ndings in the rodent.

are not possible in osteonal remodeling species (ie, humans
and monkeys) given PTH daily for a relatively short (2–3%)
portion of the normal life span (4, 9, 35).
Relationship of Chronic Hormonal Stimulation to Devel-
opment of Bone Tumors:
In addition to the exaggerated
pharmacologic effects on bone mass, long-term treatment of
rats with PTH(1-34) caused the unexpected nding of fo-
cal bone proliferative lesions, including osteosarcoma. Al-
though the cellular and molecular mechanisms for the fo-
cal bone proliferative lesions are not presently understood,
in the current study the exaggerated effects on bone mass
and bone neoplasms resulted from chronic stimulation of os-
teoblasts by PTH(1-34). It has been established in other organ
systems in rats that chronic hormonal stimulation of a phar-
macodynamic target cell population can lead to cell prolifera-
tion, clonal expansion, and ultimately neoplasms. One well-
established example where chronic stimulation of a target
tissue with a peptide hormone leads to hyperplasia are drugs,

and other proton pump inhibitors that induce neoplasia of
enterochromafn-like cells (15). These molecules indirectly
lead to increased gastrin levels that, in the rat, cause hyperpla-
sia and neoplasia of gastric enterochromafn cells. Similar
ndings are not seen in humans receiving chronic therapy
with proton pump inhibitors (30).
These examples provide evidence that chronic hormonal
stimulation of a target tissue in the rat can cause hyperplas-
tic and proliferative lesions. Similar to TSH and gastrin in
the previous models, PTH has direct, receptor-mediated ef-
fects on osteoblasts, where PTH binds with high afnity to a
specic G-protein-coupled PTH/PTHrP receptor (39). PTH
has numerous effects on osteoblast activity, including stim-
ulating differentiation and inhibiting apoptosis (11, 12, 22,
39, 47). The available data suggest that long-term hormonal
stimulation of the osteoblast by PTH results in an exagger-
ated skeletal response in the rat and the development of bone
tumors such as those observed in this 2-year rat study.
It is reasonable to conclude that compounds that produce
a similarly profound bone anabolic response in the rodent
via receptor-mediated effects on the osteoblast, would also
result in bone proliferative lesions following near-lifetime
treatment. This hypothesis is consistent with a recent draft
guidance document from the US Food and Drug Adminis-
tration on the development of PTH for the treatment and
prevention of osteoporosis (US FDA, 2001), which indicates
that osteosarcomas have been observed in long-term studies
in two strains of rats and one strain of mice with PTH and
related peptides. This would be expected because PTH and
PTH-related peptides, including PTH(1-84), PTH(1-38),
PTH(1-34), PTH(1-31), PTHrP(1-34), and PTHrP(1-36),
have anabolic activity on the rat skeleton through interactions
with the PTH/PTHrP receptor (32, 48).
Results from Other Nonclinical Studies with PTH(1-34):
Data from other animal studies are useful in considering the
predictivity of the rat ndings for humans. A study in which
0, 1, or 5 lg/kg PTH(1-34) was given daily to 80 skeletally
mature (9–11 years old) ovariectomized cynomolgus mon-
keys for up to 18 months further emphasized the marked
differences in bone physiology between primates and rats.
Although PTH(1-34) increased bone mass, the skeletal ef-
fects were much less prominent in the monkeys than those
observed in rats and were more similar to that seen in humans
(4, 5, 21, 45). Qualitative histologic observations revealed
no neoplastic or cellular proliferation lesions (21, C. Jerome
and D. Burr, personal communication, August 2001). In addi-
tion, thePTH(1-34) used in the presentstudy was negative in a
battery of in vitro and in vivo genotoxicity tests, including an
Ames test, mouse lymphoma assay, CHO cell chromosomal
aberration assay, and an in vivo micronucleus test in the
mouse (unpublished data). These results further support the
conclusion that the bone proliferative lesions in the current
study were produced through a nongenotoxic, receptor-
mediated mechanism.
Conclusions on the Relevance of Rat Osteosarcoma to
Human Patients:
In assessing the relevance of the bone
proliferative changes observed in the 2-year rat study with
PTH(1-34) to postmenopausal women or adult men who
would receive PTH(1-34) as a therapy for osteoporosis, sev-
eral relevant factors must to be considered. Rats were treated
daily for up to 2 years, which is approximately 80 to 90%
of the normal life span. In comparison, the anticipated du-
ration of therapy for humans is approximately 24 months,
which represents only 2 to 3% of the life span. Second, in
terms of bone growth, rats were treated for about 25 to 30
bone-turnover cycles (27), as compared to the expected treat-
ment duration of 1 to 3 bone-turnover cycles in osteoporotic
women (42). Third, there are fundamental differences in bone
physiology between rats and humans, including longitudinal
growth of bones throughout much of a rat’s life and the near
absence of osteonal remodeling in rats. The prolonged du-
ration of treatment combined with the considerable sensitiv-
ity of the rat skeleton to the pharmacologic effects of PTH
resulted in chronic hormonal stimulation of the osteoblast
and highly exaggerated increases in bone mass that were in
considerable excess of the effects observed in nonhuman pri-
mates (4, 5, 45) and humans (9, 35). Therefore, it can be
concluded that in adult humans, in which such exaggerated
pharmacologic effects do not occur, it is unlikely that the risk
of bone neoplasia would be increased by daily treatment with
PTH(1-34) for a relatively small fraction of the normal life
span.
