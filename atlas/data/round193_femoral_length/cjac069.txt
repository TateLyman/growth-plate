Abaloparatide and teriparatide enhance mandibular 
growth in adolescent rats with site-specific and mechano-
related effects
Ruyi  Wang1, Hui  Qiao1, Yuran  Qian1, Gehua  Zhen2, Zhihe  Zhao1 and Yu  Li1,
1State Key Laboratory of Oral Diseases and National Clinical Research Center for Oral Diseases, Department of Orthodontics, West China 
Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University, Chengdu, China
2Department of Orthopedic Surgery, Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Baltimore, MD, USA
Correspondence to: Yu Li, State Key Laboratory of Oral Diseases and National Clinical Research Center for Oral Diseases, Department of Orthodontics, West 
China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University, Chengdu, China. E-mail: yuli@scu.edu.cn
Summary 
Objective:  Teriparatide (TPTD) and abaloparatide (ABL) are two osteoanabolic drugs targeting parathyroid hormone (PTH)1R signalling. This 
study aimed to investigate the effects of TPTD and ABL on the adolescent mandibular growth.
Method:  In total, 70 4-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly divided into 14 groups, treated with intermittent TPDT or ABL at 
various doses, accompanied by mandibular advancement (MA) or not. 3D printing was used to fabricate an innovative splint for MA. After a 
4-week treatment, morphological measurement, histological and immunohistochemical analysis were performed. Mandibular condylar chon-
drocytes (MCCs) were treated with TPTD or ABL, followed by CCK-8 assay, alcian blue staining, real time–PCR and immunofluorescent 
staining.
Result:  In vivo, TPTD or ABL alone increased the condylar length and cartilage thickness, with up-regulated SOX9 and COL II, whilst down-
regulated COL X; however, when combined with MA, the promotive effects were attenuated. TPTD or ABL alone increased the mandibular body 
height and mandibular angle width, whilst increased the mandibular body length and alveolar bone width when combined with MA. In vitro, 
TPTD or ABL enhanced the MCC proliferation, glycosaminoglycan synthesis, COL II and SOX9 expression, whilst down-regulated COL X, Ihh 
and PTH1R expression.
Conclusion:  Both ABL and TPTD enhance mandibular growth in adolescent rats with site-specific and mechano-related effects, including pro-
pelling chondrogenesis at the condylar cartilage and promoting bone apposition at other mechano-responsive sites. They behave as promising 
drugs for mandibular growth modification, and in general ABL seems more potent than TPTD in this context.
Introduction
Class II malocclusion is a common dentofacial malocclusion, 
and among the class II patients seeking for orthognathic 
surgery treatment, as high as 89% are inflicted with man-
dibular retrusion (1, 2). Mandibular hypoplasia (MH) not 
only affects the  patient’s  facial appearance and occlusal 
function, but also increases the risk of obstructive sleep 
apnea–hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) (3), which grievously 
harms health and growth. Orthognathic surgery can ameli-
orate severe MH in adulthood; however, before that, the pa-
tient has to suffer from its detriment both physically and 
psychologically. Therefore, if MH could be improved, more 
or less, during the growing period, it would be consider-
ably beneficial for the  patient’s  occlusal function, respira-
tory capacity, facial appearance and mental health, with the 
difficulty of surgery reduced as well. For adolescent patients 
with MH with growth potential, functional appliances are 
recommended by some orthodontists (4). However, a wide 
range of clinical studies so far shed light on the frustrating 
conclusion that functional orthopaedic treatment alone has 
only quite a limited effect on promoting real skeletal man-
dibular growth (5, 6).
The growth sites of the mandible involve the condyle, the 
posterior border of the mandibular body, the sigmoid notch, 
and the alveolar crest (7), among which, intramembranous 
ossification of the mandibular main body and endochondral 
ossification of the condyle act as two major growth patterns 
(8, 9). The condyle for particular plays an essential role in 
augmenting mandibular length and height (10) During 
endochondral ossification of the mandibular condylar car-
tilages, the differentiation of osteochondral progenitors into 
chondrocytes is regulated by SRY-related high mobility group-
box gene 9 (SOX9) (11). SOX9 directly triggers the expres-
sion of type II collagen (COL II) (12), which is produced by 
chondrocytes as a major collagen component of the cartilage 
matrix (13). Another cartilage matrix component, type X col-
lagen (COL X), is synthesized by hypertrophic chondrocytes 
and its expression indicates termination of chondrogenesis 
with subsequent initiation of endochondral ossification (14, 
15). The growth and endochondral ossification of the man-
dibular condyle in adolescence could be modulated by a var-
iety of external signals, including mechanical stresses (16) 
and biomolecules such as insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) 
(17), parathyroid hormone (PTH) (18), parathyroid hormone

Since mechanical stimulation with functional appliances alone 
could not greatly increase mandibular growth, it is attractive 
to attempt the strategy of combining functional orthopaedic 
treatment with medications that have pro-chondrogenic and/
or osteoanabolic potentials.
The PTH is known as a regulator of calcium homeostasis 
(21). Its paradoxical dual effects on bone metabolism have 
been widely studied, that is, continuous administration causes 
catabolic effects whilst intermittent dosing is anabolic (22). 
Based on the role of PTH in promoting bone anabolism, 
teriparatide (TPTD), a peptide that is structurally identical 
to human PTH (1–34), has been approved by FDA as the 
first osteoanabolic drug for treating osteoporosis (23). On 
the contrary, PTHrP, which is originally found in human ma-
lignant hypercalcemia, has similar osteoanabolic effects and 
shares the same type 1 PTH/PTHrP receptor (PTH1R) with 
PTH (24). However, the short half-life of PTHrP makes it 
easy to degrade and therefore difficult to take effect in sys-
temic administration (25). In order to exploit the function of 
PTHrP and overcome its unstableness, abaloparatide (ABL), 
a synthetic analogue of PTHrP (1–34), has been developed 
and also approved for the treatment of osteoporosis (26). 
Although TPTD [or PTH (1–34)] and ABL seem to have 
similar osteoanabolic effects, their osteolytic potential may 
discriminate due to different relative affinities to the RG and 
R0 conformations of PTH1R, resulting in less bone-resorptive 
effects of ABL compared to TPTD (24). In gross, ABL is be-
lieved to overcome the disadvantage of easy decomposition of 
PTHrP (1–34) and attenuate the bone metabolic window loss 
caused by TPTD.
Regarding cartilage, both PTH and PTHrP exert regula-
tory effects on mandibular condylar chondrocytes. PTH 
enhances the chondrocyte proliferation and increases the ex-
pression of COL II with a concomitant decrease in COL X 
and runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), indicating 
the promotion of chondrogenesis and suppression of terminal 
chondrocyte differentiation (18, 27). PTHrP plays an essen-
tial role in the prenatal and postnatal growth of the condyle 
(8, 28). The PTHrP-deficient mice show deformities of the 
ramus and mandibular body, indicating that PTHrP disrup-
tion may impair both intramembranous and endochondral 
osteogenesis of the mandible (8). PTHrP may regulate the 
proliferation, chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation of 
chondrocytes by up-regulating the expression of SOX9 and 
COL II, inhibiting the expression of RUNX2, COL X, and 
ALP (29–31). In the long bones of limbs, which are predom-
inant with endochondral ossification, the negative feedback 
loop formed with PTHrP and Ihh has long been recognized to 
regulate the lengthening of the growth plate. Notably, recent 
finding revealed skeletal stem cells among PTHrP-positive 
chondrocytes within the resting zone of the postnatal growth 
plate (32). PTHrP might also interplay with Ihh to maintain 
homeostasis of the mandibular condylar cartilage (33). Both 
in vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated force-induced 
expressions of Ihh and PTHrP in the mandibular condylar 
cartilage, suggesting them as mechanotransduction mediators 
(29, 34).
In brief, PTH and PTHrP are both involved in the regula-
tion of mandibular growth, and their derivative osteoanabolic 
drugs, TPTD and ABL, respectively, may well have the po-
tential to promote the growth of the mandible in adoles-
cents. Thus, this study was aimed to investigate the effects 
of intermittent TPTD or ABL on mandibular growth, with 
focus on the mandibular condylar cartilage, and furthermore, 
whether there were additive or synergistic effects when the 
drugs were combined with mandibular advancement (MA) 
mechanical stimulation.
Materials and methods
Animal model and experimental design
The study was approved by the Animal Care and Ethics 
Committee of the State Key Laboratory of Oral Disease, 
West China School of Stomatology, Sichuan University, China 
(No. WCHSIRB-D-2017-259). All the experimental proced-
ures followed the guidelines for animal experimentation of 
the university/the Animal Research N3CRs guidelines for 
Reporting of In vivo Experiments (ARRIVEs) guidelines.
In total, 70 male Sprague-Dawley rats (4-week-old, body 
weight 105 g ± 10 g) were randomly allocated into 14 groups 
(n = 5 in each), including one control group, one MA group, 
6 drug groups and 6 drug + MA groups. The control group 
received a daily subcutaneous injection of normal saline 
(NS), whilst the MA group received a functional appliance 
for MA with a daily subcutaneous injection of NS. The 6 
drug groups include the high-dose TPTD (TPTDhigh, 8mg/
kg), mid-dose TPTD (TPTDmid, 800 μg/kg), low-dose TPTD 
(TPTDlow, 80 μg/kg), high-dose ABL (ABLhigh, 8mg/kg), mid-
dose ABL (ABLmid, 800 μg/kg), and low-dose ABL (ABLlow, 80 
μg/kg) group, which received a daily subcutaneous injection 
of TPTD or ABL at the corresponding dose level. The 6 drug 
+ MA groups include the TPTDhigh + MA, TPTDmid + MA, 
TPTDlow + MA, ABLhigh + MA, ABLmid + MA, and ABLlow + 
MA group, which received both MA and daily subcutaneous 
injections of TPTD or ABL at the corresponding dose level 
(Supplementary Figure 1). All the subcutaneous injections 
were given at the back of the neck. All the rats were housed in 
a constant temperature of 25°C, a 12-hour light/12-hour dark 
environment, and fed with a standard soft diet and water ad 
libitum.
Design and fabrication of the mandibular 
advancement appliance
It is noteworthy that the appliance for MA was developed 
via digital design and 3D printing to optimize the accuracy of 
mandibular repositioning in the rat model. Firstly, the digital 
model of an inclined bite splint was designed on Tinkercad 
software (https://www.tinkercad.com), and then the solid 
male mold was made through 3D printing. The bite splint 
is an isosceles trapezoid with a length of 4 mm at the top, 
8 mm at the bottom and a height of 7 mm. From side view, 
it is a straight-angle trapezoid with a thickness of 2 mm 
(Figure 1A and 1B). Innovatively, a smooth groove was de-
signed on the occlusal surface of the guide plate to prevent 
mandibular deviation during MA (Figure 1A arrow). The 
male mold was placed in liquid silica gel and then solidified 
to obtain the template of the bite splint (Figure 1C). Then, 
the resin was filled in the template and bonded on rat upper 
incisors under anaesthesia with 70 mg/kg ketamine (Ketavet 
100, Intervet Productions Srl, Aprilia, Italy) and 5 mg/kg 
xylazine (Sedaxylan, Eurovet Animals Health B.V., Bladel, 
The Netherlands), making sure that the lingual surface of 
the upper incisor was in contact with the back wall of the 
template without left–right or forward–backward deflection

Morphological measurements
The rats in each group were euthanized using excessive anaes-
thetic injection after a 4-week treatment. The rat mandibles 
were dissected mid-sagittal and divided into left and right 
halves without muscle tissue attached to the bone surface. 
The mandibular morphology was measured referring to the 
reported method (35). Briefly, pictures of the mandibles were 
taken using a digital single lens reflex camera (Nikon D700, 
Japan), which was mounted at a 90° angle and at a fixed 
distance from the samples. The mandibular length and height 
in these photos were measured with Image-Pro plus (version 
6.0, Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA), whilst the 
width of the mandible was measured with vernier callipers. 
Measurement items included the mandibular condylar length, 
the mandibular body length, the mandibular body height, the 
mandibular incisal alveolar bone width, and the mandibular 
angle width, with the length and height standardized to the 
whole-body length. The selected landmarks and measurement 
items were shown in Table 1 and Figure 2A.
Histology and immunohistochemistry assays
The condyles were harvested after digital pictures were taken. 
The specimens of condyles were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde 
for 24 hours and subsequently decalcified in 10% ethylene di-
amine tetraacetic (EDTA) for 8 weeks at 37°C. Subsequently, 
gradient-dehydrated, dipping wax, and paraffin-embedding 
were performed before sectioning condyles at 5 μm thickness 
to obtain serial sagittal slices.
These slices were stained with safranin O/fast green, or 
haematoxylin and eosin (HE), for cartilage demarcation and 
morphological measurement. The condylar cartilage was 
divided into four layers that is, the fibrous, proliferative, 
chondrogenic, and hypertrophic layer (10, 36) (Figure 3A). 
Since the posterior region of the condylar cartilage was re-
ported to be the most sensitive to MA stimulation (11, 36), 
the posterior region of the condylar cartilage in front of the 
articular disc attachment was selected as the region of interest 
(ROI). Images of the samples were obtained using a light 
microscope (Leica DM 2500, Wetzlar, Germany), and the 
thickness of each layer and the whole cartilage were measured 
using Image-Pro plus6.0. Specifically, a straight line perpen-
dicular to the surface of condylar cartilage was made on the 
HE-stained image, which ran through the fibrous, prolifera-
tive, chondrogenic, and hypertrophic layers. The length of the 
line in each layer was calculated by the linear measurement 
tool of Image-Pro plus 6.0 software as the thickness of this 
layer. The total thickness was the sum of the thickness of each 
layer (Figure 3A).
For the immunohistochemistry assay, the slices were in-
cubated with primary antibodies (1:200) against SOX9 
(ab185966; rabbit, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), COL II 
(ab34712; rabbit, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), and COL 
X (ab260040; rabbit, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) over-
night at 4°C. Incubation with PBS instead of the primary 
antibody was used as a negative control. Following rinsing 
with PBS for three times, the samples were incubated with 
HRP-labelled goat anti-rabbit IgG polymer at 37°C for 20 
minutes according to the instruction (PV-9001, ZSJQ-Bio, 
Beijing, China). After rinsing with PBS for three times, freshly 
prepared DAB chromogen was applied and incubated for 5 
minutes at room temperature (ZLI-9018, ZSJQ-Bio, Beijing, 
China).
Similar to HE-stained sections, the ROI for immunoh­
istochemical semi-quantitative analysis was also delineated in 
the posterior region of the condylar cartilage before the ar-
ticular disc attachment point. Mean optical density (MOD) in 
the ROI of each sample, that is, the ratio of integrated optical 
density (IOD) to the ROI area, was calculated with Image-
Pro plus6.0. Given the different expression distribution of the 
three target proteins, the ROI of SOX9 was in the prolifera-
tive and chondrogenic layers, COL II in the chondrogenic and 
hypertrophic layers, and COL X in the hypertrophic layer. 
Each sample was measured three times under ×400 magnifi-
cation, and the average value was taken as the result of each 
condyle sample.

Mandibular condylar chondrocyte isolation and 
culture
Mandibular condylar cartilage was dissected from SD rats 
within 24 hours of birth. In brief, the mandibular condyles 
of new-born rats were harvested in penicillin-streptomycin 
(PS, R20016, Hyclone, Wuhan, China) and PBS solution. 
Subsequently, the condylar cartilages were dissected with 
microforceps after removing the soft tissue on their surface 
under a stereomicroscope. Then, cartilages were cut into 
small pieces followed by sequential digestion with trypsin 
(25200072, 0.25%, Gibco, USA) for 20 minutes and type 
II collagenase (YZ-1148090, 0.2%, Solarbio, Beijing, China) 
for 1 hour at 37°C. After resuspension and filtration, the 
chondrocytes were dispersed into single cells and cultured 
in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS (10100147, 
Gibco, USA), and 1% PS under a humidified atmosphere of 
5% CO2 at 37°C. Upon confluency, the primary cells were 
further subcultured to passage 1 (P1) for in vitro experi-
ments. Alcian blue and safranin O staining were used to iden-
tify the MCCs.
Cell counting Kit-8 assay
The cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (C0037, Beyotime, 
Shanghai, China) was carried out to investigate the effects of 
TPTD and ABL on MCCs proliferation. MCCs (5 × 103 cells/
well) were seeded in 96-well cell plates and treated with 100 
nmol/mL TPTD or ABL, and the media were changed every 
24 hours for 3 days. Standard curves were constructed by 
measuring the optical density (OD) value at 450 nm.
Alcian blue staining
P1 MCCs (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 24-well cell plates 
and treated with 100 nmol/mL PBS, TPTD, or ABL for 48 
hours. The glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content of MCCs was 
identified using a standard alcian blue staining kit (A8140, 
Solarbio, Beijing, China). The cells were washed three times 
with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room tem-
perature for 20 minutes, again washed three times with PBS 
(3 minutes each time), stained with alcian staining solution 
for 30 minutes, and washed three times with PBS. The im-
ages were acquired using a stereomicroscope. with consistent 
photographic parameters.
Total RNA extraction and quantitative real-time 
PCR analysis
The P1 MCCs (15 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in six-well 
cell plates and incubated with 100 nmol/mL PBS, TPTD 
or ABL for 24 hours. Total RNA was isolated from MCCs 
using RNA-Quick purification kit (ES-RN001, ES Science, 
Shanghai, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions, 
and PrimeScript RT reagent kit (RR047A, TaKaRa, Japan) 
was used for reversed transcription of RNA for cDNA. 
Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using an SYBER 
Green qPCR kit (B21703, Bimake, USA), and carried on 
in StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). 
Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GADPH) was 
used as an internal control gene to normalize the expression 
of other genes, including Col2a1, Col10a1, Sox9, Ihh, Pthlh, 
Pth1r. The results were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method 
and presented as fold increases relative to the control group. 
The primer sequences are described in the Supplementary 
Table 1.
Immunofluorescence staining
P1 MCCs (3 × 104 cells/well) were seeded on 48-well plates, 
and incubated with 100 nmol/mL PBS, TPTD or ABL for 24 
hours. Then MCCs were washed by PBS and fixed in 4% 
paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, permeabilized in 0.1% 
Triton X-100 at room temperature for 10 minutes (for COL 
II, COL X and Ihh) or 0.5% Triton X-100 on ice for 20 min-
utes (for SOX9 and PTHrP) and then blocked with 3% BSA at 
room temperature for 30 minutes, before incubated overnight 
with rabbit anti-rat COL II (1:200, ab34712, rabbit, Abcam, 
Cambridge, MA, USA), -COL X (1:200, ab260040, rabbit, 
Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), -SOX9 (1:200, ab185966, 
rabbit, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), -Ihh (1:200, DF7688; 
rabbit, Affinity, China), -PTHrP (1:200, DF6532, rabbit, 
Affinity, China), and -PTH1R (1:200, DF6589, rabbit, Affinity, 
China) antibody at 4°C. The cells were incubated with goat 
anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200, A0516, goat, Solarbio, 
Beijing, China) and phalloidin (1:400, C2201S, Solarbio, 
Beijing, China) for 1 hour, then washed twice with PBST, and 
incubated with DAPI for 10 minutes. The MCCs were finally 
washed with PBS 3 times and analyzed with Leica fluorescence 
microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Results
ABL and TPTD had synergistic effects with MA 
mechanical loading to enhance mandibular growth
Morphological measurement showed that the condylar length 
(Co-Mf) in the TPTDhigh and ABLhigh groups were significantly 
greater than that in the control group, whilst mid- or low-
dose TPTD or ABL had no effects on the condylar growth 
(Figure 2C, Supplementary Figure 2B).
TPTD and ABL also promoted growth of the mandibular 
body, including its height and length. All the doses of ABL, 
with or without MA, significantly increased the mandibular 
body height (Mf-MP) (Figure 2D). In contrast, TPTD could 
function independently only at high dose, however effective 
at mid- and low-doses only when combined with MA 
(Supplementary Figure 2C). Moreover, high-dose ABL was 
more effective than high-dose TPTD in mandibular body 
height increment (Supplementary Figure 3C). Interestingly, al-
though TPTD, ABL, or MA alone had no significant effects on 
the mandibular body length (Mf-L1), when high- or mid-dose 
TPTD or ABL was combined with MA, a significant increase 
in Mf-L1 was observed (Figure 2E, Supplementary Figure 
2D), indicating a synergistic effect of the drugs and MA mech-
anical loading on the growth of the mandibular body.
Regarding width, TPTD or MA alone did not elicit sig-
nificant changes in the lower anterior alveolar bone width 
(K–Kʹ). However, when TPTD was combined with MA, 
regardless of the drug dose, the alveolar bone width was 
significantly increased compared to that of the control or 
MA group. In contrast to TPTD, high- or mid-dose ABL 
alone significantly increased the lower anterior alveolar 
bone width, and when combined with MA, the increase be-
came significantly larger compared to the ABL alone groups 
(Figure 2F, Supplementary Figure 2E). In addition, when 
the two drugs were compared, the increase of alveolar bone 
width in the ABLhigh group was significantly larger than 
that in the TPTDhigh group (Supplementary Figure 3D). 
Furthermore, high- or mid-dose TPTD or ABL, whether 
combined with MA or not, significantly increased the man-
dibular angle width compared with the control (Figure 2G, 
Supplementary Figure 2F).
Taken together, both ABL and TPTD enhanced the man-
dibular growth with effects varying with different sites. Of 
note, synergistic effects of the drugs and MA mechanical 
loading were shown at the mandibular body and the alveolar 
bone. In general, ABL demonstrated bone-augmenting effects 
more powerful than TPTD in the present context.

HE staining showed that MA, but not TPTD or ABL, sig-
nificantly increased thickness of the fibrous layer, indicating 
that thickening of this layer was an adaptive response to the 
mechanical stimulation of MA (Figure 3D, Supplementary 
Figure 4C). As for the proliferative layer, only the TPTDhigh 
group had a significant decrease in the proliferative layer 
thickness, while the other experimental groups did not 
differ with the control group (Figure 3E, Supplementary 
Figure 4D). Moreover, the chondrogenic layer, hypertrophic 
layer and the total cartilage were all significantly increased 
in thickness only when high-dose TPTD or ABL was used 
(Figure 3F–H, Supplementary Figure 4E–G), and the increase 
was significant larger in the ABLhigh group than TPTDhigh 
group (Supplementary Figure 3F). However, when high-dose 
TPTD or ABL was combined with MA, thickness of the 
chondrogenic layer, hypertrophic layer and total cartilage 
was lower than that of the drug alone groups (still higher 
than the control group) (Figure 3F–H, Supplementary Figure 
4E–G).
Collectively, both high-dose ABL or TPTD alone could in-
crease the condylar cartilage thickness, with ABL more ef-
fective than TPTD; however, the combination of the drugs 
with MA attenuated the promotive effects.

and ABLhigh groups, but not in the TPTDhigh + MA or ABLhigh + 
MA groups (Figure 4C and 4D, Supplementary Figure 5C and 
5D). In addition, the expression of COL X was significantly 
attenuated in the TPTDhigh, TPTDmid and ABLmid groups, but 
not in the corresponding drug + MA groups (Figure 4E and 
4F, Supplementary Figure 5E and 5F).
Comparing TPTD and ABL, high-dose ABL exerted 
stronger up-regulation of SOX9 than high-dose TPTD with 
or without MA; nevertheless, they showed no significant dif-
ference in the regulation for COL II or COL X expression 
(Supplementary Figure 3H and 3I).
In summary, ABL and TPTD promoted chondrogenesis and 
inhibited hypertrophy of the mandibular condylar cartilage, 
with ABL being more effective than TPTD to up-regulating 
the SOX9 expression; however, these effects were partially 
counteracted by MA in terms of the COL II and COL X 
expression.
ABL and TPTD enhanced proliferation and 
chondrogenic differentiation whilst inhibited 
hypertrophy of MCCs in vitro
CCK-8 assay showed that both ABL and TPTD significantly 
enhanced the cell viability of MCCs compared with the con-
trol at 48 hours and 72 hours, with no significant difference 
between the two drugs (Figure 5B). After incubated with ABL 
or TPTD for 24 hours, real-time PCR (Figure 5C–H) showed 
up-regulated Sox9 and Col2a1, down-regulated Col10a1, Ihh 
and Pth1r, whilst unchanged Pthlh. When comparing the two 
drugs, ABL was stronger at up-regulating Sox9, while TPTD 
was better at down-regulating Ihh. In addition, alcian blue 
staining showed significantly increased GAG in the experi-
mental groups compared with the control at 48 hours (Figure 
5I). The immunofluorescence staining results (Supplementary 
Figure 6) were consistent with the PCR.
Together, ABL and TPTD enhanced proliferation and 
chondrogenic differentiation, whilst inhibited hypertrophy 
of MCCs in vitro, with ABL more effective than TPTD to 
up-regulating the SOX9 expression.
Discussion
For a long, functional appliance treatment seems to be the 
only clinical approach to promote mandibular growth in ado-
lescence, with limited real skeletal effects. It is of great im-
portance to pursue other biotherapies for mandibular growth 
modification. Many biomolecules and physical stimulations 
have been shown to enhance the growth of the mandibular 
condylar cartilage (37–39). In the present study, we ob-
served and compared the effects of the two FDA-approved 
osteoanabolic drugs, TPTD and ABL, on promoting man-
dibular growth in adolescent rats, combined with or without 
MA mechanical loading.
The various effects of the two drugs on different sites of 
the mandible were most interesting (Figure 6). Overall, their 
effects were similar, and ABL seemed to be more potent than 
TPTD. ABL alone increased the condylar length, the man-
dibular body height and the mandibular angle width, which 
could not be further enhanced by MA; ABL alone increased 
the anterior alveolar bone width, which could be further 
enhanced by MA; ABL could not increase the mandibular 
body length unless combined with MA. Thus, the MA splint 
seemed to elevate the mechanical stress level only at the an-
terior section of the mandible, which amplified the effects of 
ABL. Taken together, the anabolic modelling effects of ABL 
and TPTD on the mandible are associated with mechanical 
stress, both from physiologic activities and the MA splint 
(Figure 6).
Notably, both ABL and TPTD could increase the man-
dibular anterior alveolar bone width. In clinical, function ap-
pliances may labialize the lower anterior teeth, which may 
result in dehiscence (44, 45). Thus, augmentation of the an-
terior alveolar bone width with the drugs can help lower 
periodontal risks in MA. On the other hand, the increment 
of mandibular body height by ABL or TPTD (Figure 2B, 
Supplementary Figure 2A) may lead to counter clockwise 
rotation of the mandible, which has potential clinical impli-
cation for growth modification of high-angle adolescent pa-
tients with low-posterior facial height.
The mandibular angle width was increased by both TPTD 
and ABL alone, whilst TPTD had no effects on alveolar bone 
width unless combined with MA. This difference could be 
explained with differential mechanical microenvironment 
between the two sites. The mandibular angle is subject to 
strong and frequent physiological mechanical stimulation by 
the masticatory muscles; in contrast, the mandibular anterior 
alveolar bone undergoes much less physiological mechanical 
loading, partially due to excessive overjet of the rat dentition, 
which may be greatly enhanced by the MA splint. In fact, the 
combinative effects of mechanical stress and PTH or PTHrP 
have been well-established in osteoporosis studies (46–48).

The mandible is a composite bone formed via both 
endochondral ossification at the mandibular condylar car-
tilage and intramembranous ossification at other sites (40). 
As an essential growth site to increase the mandibular length 
and height, the condyle has been paid great attention to. The 
present study showed that TPTD or ABL alone significantly 
enhanced the condylar length. Accordingly, the total thick-
ness of the condylar cartilage was significantly increased 
when high-dose TPTD or ABL was used, with ABL being the 
more effective. Notwithstanding, the MA splint did not sig-
nificantly increase the cartilage thickness except that of the 
fibrous layer. The finding was largely similar to some pre-
vious studies (10), however inconsistent with others (41–43). 
Furthermore, the combination with MA even attenuated the 
effects of the two drugs on cartilage thickening. The MA 
splint used in the present study was made via digital design 
and 3D printing, which should be more accurate than the 
previous counterparts. It seemed that the effects of such MA 
splints on the rat condylar cartilage thickness might be tran-
sient and unpredictable.
A wide range of evidence indicates that PTH and PTHrP 
can enhance chondrocyte proliferation (18, 19, 27, 36); 
however, the HE staining showed unchanged or even de-
creased thickness of proliferative layers in the present study, 
which was somewhat counterintuitive. Condylar cartilage is 
a secondary cartilage, in which the differentiated chondro-
cytes no longer exhibit division ability, which means that all 
chondroblasts and hypertrophic chondrocytes derive from the 
differentiation of the upper cells rather than their own repli-
cation (49). In addition, BrdU labelling also demonstrated cell 
migration and differentiation from the proliferative layer to 
the chondrogenic and hypertrophic layers in the mandibular 
condylar cartilage (29). Therefore, although the proliferative 
layer thickness showed no increase after 4-week TPTD or ABL 
treatment in this study, significant thickening of chondrogenic 
and hypertrophic layer suggested the temporarily enhanced 
expansion of the proliferative layer, which might have faded 
at the sacrifice time point (8 weeks of age). In addition, the 
CCK-8 results in the in vitro study also supports that TPTD 
and ABL promote the proliferation of the MCCs.

the

SOX9 was up-regulated by TPTD, ABL, or MA alone, among

was combined with MA, the SOX9 up-regulation was fur-
ther enhanced. Both TPTD and ABL up-regulated COL II, 
whilst down-regulated COL X, suggesting that TPTD and 
ABL could promote chondrogenesis and inhibit chondrocyte 
premature hypertrophy.
The findings in vitro were consistent with in vivo, that 
is, CCK8, real-time PCR and immunofluorescence con-
firmed that ABL and TPTD could promote proliferation and 
chondrogenesis, while inhibit hypertrophy of MCCs. Moreover, 
the Ihh-PTHrP negative feedback loop has been reported to 
maintain homeostasis of the condylar cartilage (50, 51). In the 
present study, exogenous TPTD and ABL reduced the expres-
sion of Ihh, which could promote hypertrophy of MCCss; the 
endogenous PTHrP expression was not changed, whilst the 
PTH1R expression of MCCs was suppressed by either TPTD 
or ABL. Their effects on PTH1R were consistent with previous 
studies (52–54), and the down-regulation of PTH1R might at-
tenuate the effects of exogenous TPTD or ABL.
Although our study demonstrated that ABL and TPTD are 
efficacious in promoting mandibular growth, there are still 
some limitations to this study. Firstly, we used subcutaneous 
injection according to the clinical recommendations of ABL 
and TPTD, leading to the inevitable systemic effects. Secondly, 
this study only focused on the effects of ABL and TPTD 
on the mandible, and their impacts on other craniofacial 
structures should be taken into account in future studies. 
Additionally, since PTHrP is an autocrine/paracrine factor, 
this study was performed in a male rat model out of concern 
that females might be more susceptible to the pubertal sex 
hormones. Admittedly, gender difference has been reported in 
the rapid developmental changes of rat condylar cartilage and 
subchondral bone (55). Therefore, further investigation into 
the female rat models would be necessary.
Conclusions
Both ABL and TPTD enhance mandibular growth in adoles-
cent rats, which is site-specific and mechano-related (Figure 
6). At the endochondral ossification site, they increase the 
mandibular condylar cartilage thickness via propelling 
chondrogenesis. At the intramembranous ossification sites, 
they promote the mandibular growth most evidently at the 
mandibular angle, which is mechanically hyperactive. When 
combined with MA, they increase the mandibular body length 
and anterior alveolar bone width. In general, ABL seems more 
potent than teriparatide for these effects.
