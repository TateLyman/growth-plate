TITLE: Growth hormone regulates the stem cell population in the growth plate
======================================================================
Growth hormone (GH) remains the most effective—and essentially the only—approved treatment for children with GH deficiency–associated growth failure (1). Owing to its potent growth-promoting effects, favorable safety profile, and the absence of robust alternative therapies, GH has become a widely used intervention for short stature in children, regardless of GH status. Notably, since 2003, GH therapy has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for children with idiopathic short stature—a condition defined by height more than two SD below the mean for age and sex in the absence of GH deficiency. GH is also employed, or under consideration, in the treatment of other pediatric growth disorders, including Turner syndrome, Prader–Willi syndrome, chronic kidney disease, and children born small for gestational age without subsequent catch-up growth (1).
Despite its broad clinical application, the cellular mechanisms by which GH regulates skeletal growth—particularly within its primary target tissue, the growth plate—remain incompletely understood. This knowledge gap is especially significant with respect to growth plate stem cells, which have been proposed in the growth plate for a long time (2, 3), but have only recently been identified (4, 5).
The growth plate is a narrow cartilage disc located near the ends of long bones that drives longitudinal bone growth during childhood. This process occurs via endochondral ossification, wherein a cartilage template is progressively replaced by bone. The sequence begins in the resting zone, where the least differentiated and slowest-dividing cells reside. These cells are sequentially recruited into the proliferative zone (also referred to as the flat-cell or columnar zone), where they actively divide and align into vertical columns. Following proliferation, chondrocytes enter the hypertrophic zone, undergoing a more than 16-fold increase in volume (6), after which they either undergo apoptosis or transdifferentiate (7), leaving behind a cartilaginous extracellular matrix that serves as a scaffold for bone formation (8, 9). Sustained longitudinal bone growth depends on continuous chondrocyte production, a process now understood to be supported by a resident stem cell population localized to the resting zone (4, 5, 10).
Initial studies of GH action suggested that its effects on bone growth were mediated by a circulating secondary messenger known as somatomedin C, now recognized as insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1) (11). This led to the development of the somatomedin hypothesis, which posits that GH acts via hepatic stimulation of IGF-1, which then mediates growth effects at peripheral sites (12). However, seminal studies involving the local infusion of GH into rat growth plates revealed that treated limbs exhibited lengthening relative to vehicle-treated controls (13). These findings gave rise to the dual-effector hypothesis, which proposes that GH exerts both systemic effects via liver-derived IGF1 and direct effects at the level of the growth plate itself (14). Genetic studies have supported this model: Liver-specific deletion of IGF1 results in only mild growth defects (15), whereas global inactivation of IGF1, the GH receptor (GHR), or both leads to additive impairments in bone growth (16). Thus, the current view is that GH regulates longitudinal bone growth both directly and systemically (1, 17, 18).
While IGF1 is clearly required for chondrocyte hypertrophy and supports proliferation within the growth plate (16, 19), the cellular effects of GH are less well-defined. The dual-effector model originally proposed that GH acts on progenitor cells in the resting zone (14), based on GHR expression observed in this region. However, subsequent work revealed that GHR messenger ribonucleic acid is broadly expressed throughout the growth plate (16, 20). Notably, activation of the GHR downstream signaling cascade—specifically the JAK2/STAT5 pathway—appears to be spatially restricted to the resting zone (21). Moreover, local GH injection, but not IGF1, increased the number of slowly dividing cells in the resting zone of hypophysectomized rats (22), further supporting a direct action of GH on the stem cell compartment.
Together, these observations suggest that GH may directly regulate the activity of stem cells in the growth plate. In this study, we aimed to investigate this possibility. Given the increasing use of GH in children without GH deficiency, we utilized non-GH-deficient mice as a model system to explore the cellular effects of pharmacological GH exposure.

##  Results

##  Pharmacological Doses of GH Promote Proliferation in the Growth Plate of Normal Mice.
Given the increasingly widespread use of GH in children without GH deficiency (1), we sought to investigate the mechanisms by which GH influences the growth plate in otherwise healthy mice. To this end, wild-type C57BL/6 mice were treated with GH (5 mg/kg body weight), as previously established (23). Mice acquire GH responsiveness between 2 and 3 wk of age and are fully sexually mature at 8 wk of age. Accordingly, we chose this time window for our experiments.
To assess early activation of GH signaling within the growth plate, we first performed a short-term treatment regimen, involving daily GH injections on postnatal days 27 and 28 (P27–P28), (Fig. 1A). Levels of phosphorylated STAT5 (p-STAT5), a downstream effector of the GHR, were markedly increased in the resting zone of GH-treated mice (Fig. 1 B–D), consistent with previous findings (21). This short-term treatment did not affect the expression levels of hepatic IGF-1 or its associated components but did lead to an upregulation of SOCS2, a negative regulator of GH signaling (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A).

To evaluate the effects of GH treatment on bone growth and chondrocyte proliferation, we next performed 10-d-long treatment of mice with daily GH (5 mg/kg) injections from P28 to P38 (Fig. 1E). This 10-d regimen significantly increased longitudinal bone growth rates (Fig. 1 F–H) and cell proliferation within the growth plate (Fig. 1 I–K). Immunostaining for Ki67, a proliferative marker, confirmed a higher proportion of proliferating cells in GH-treated mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). Furthermore, 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) labeling showed enhanced proliferation in the top 50 µm of the growth plate (Fig. 1L)—an area encompassing the resting zone and corresponding to the region with the strongest STAT5 activation in response to GH (Fig. 1C). Despite the growth-promoting effect of GH, this treatment downregulated the expression of several liver-derived IGF1-related transcripts, including Igf1, Igfbp3, and Acid Labile Subunit (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D), suggesting a systemic compensatory feedback loop. To confirm it further, we measured the serum levels of IGF1 in the middle of the treatment. This analysis showed a significant decrease in serum IGF1 levels after 4 d of pharmacological GH treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E), in line with previous observations (23).
To investigate proliferative dynamics in the growth plate in more detail, we used a clonal genetic tracing approach. Col2-CreERT2 mice, which express a tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase under the control of the Col2a1 promoter, were crossed with R26R-Confetti reporter mice (24) to generate Col2-CreERT2:R26R-Confetti animals. In this model, Col2a1-expressing cells can be genetically labeled in vivo, with individual clones expressing one of four fluorescent proteins. Vehicle- and GH-treated mice were pulsed with tamoxifen 4 d prior to the end of treatment, to label cells across the growth plate (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). As labeled cells divided, they formed clonal clusters of the same color (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B–E).
Quantitative analysis revealed significantly increased clonal size throughout the growth plate in GH-treated mice compared to controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B–G), consistent with elevated proliferative activity. Stratification of clones by distance from the top of the growth plate showed that GH treatment increased clone size in all zones analyzed, including the resting zone (SI Appendix, Fig. S2H). Notably, clones observed in the distal (>125 µm) regions, presumably postmitotic zones, may reflect longitudinal cellular transit during the 4-d chasing period (SI Appendix, Fig. S2H) and reflect high growth plate dynamics.
Together, these results demonstrate that pharmacological doses of GH promote proliferation within the growth plate, including the resting zone, leading to accelerated longitudinal bone growth in mice with normal GH secretion. These observations are in line with numerous previous observations (summarized in ref. 17) and validate our model.

##  Ablation of GHR in Chondrocytes Decreases Clonal Size.
To directly assess the effect of GH within the growth plate, we ablated the GHR by crossing GHRflox/flox mice with Col2-CreERT2:R26R-Confetti mice. The resulting Col2-CreERT2:R26R-Confetti:GHRflox/flox mice (GHRcKO) and littermate controls carrying a single floxed GHR allele received a single tamoxifen pulse at postnatal day 6 (P6) and were analyzed at P28 (Fig. 2A).

Growth plates were collected at P28. (B and C) Representative maximum-intensity projections (50-µm sections) showing clonal patterns in control (B) and GHR cKO (C) mice. (G–L) Representative images of EdU+ cells (red) in growth plates of control (G–I) and GHR cKO (J–L) mice. Confetti-derived red fluorescent protein (RFP) signal was visualized using anti-RFP antibody (pseudocolored yellow). Growth plate analysis showed that cartilage-specific loss of GHR significantly reduced clonal size (Fig. 2 B–E). The number of full-length columns, defined as columns spanning the entire growth plate, was reduced by nearly 50%, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2F). Proliferation within recombined cells was also decreased in GHR-deficient chondrocytes (Fig. 2 G–M), albeit not in the resting zone, defined approximately as the upper 50 µm of the growth plate, where proliferation remained unchanged (Fig. 2N). The orientation of dyads in this zone did not differ appreciably between groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Bone lengths were not significantly affected (tibia: 18.3 ± 0.2 mm in controls, n = 3 vs. 17.9 ± 0.3 mm in GHRcKO mice, n = 4, P = 0.31; femur: 13.2 ± 0.2 mm in controls, n = 3 vs. 12.8 ± 0.3 mm in GHRcKO mice, n = 4, P = 0.35), likely reflecting low recombination rate and additional decrease of recombined columns in GHRcKO mice (Fig. 2F).
These findings indicate that GH directly promotes proliferative activity within chondrocyte columns. However, the trend toward fewer columns cannot be fully explained by reduced proliferation alone and may instead suggest an additional role for GH in regulating the stem cell compartment.

##  GH Reduces the Number of Label-Retaining Cells (LRCs).
The resting zone of the growth plate harbors a population of slow-dividing stem cells (25), and we first sought to assess how GH affects their abundance. To this end, we employed mice harboring the H2B-GFP label-retaining system, wherein cells express a GFP-tagged histone H2B protein in the presence of doxycycline (Methods).
Mice received doxycycline from postnatal day 10 (P10) to P23 to label all proliferating cells within the growth plate (Fig. 3 A and B). This was followed by a 15-d chase period (P23–P38), during which rapidly dividing cells diluted the GFP signal below the detection limit. During the final 10 d (P28–P38), mice were treated daily with either vehicle or GH (Fig. 3 A, C, and D).
GH decreases the number of slow-dividing cells in the resting zone. (A) Experimental design. H2B-GFP Tet-On mice were given doxycycline from P10–P23 to label most of the cells in the growth plate. Mice were then chased until P38, during which GH or vehicle was administered daily from P28–P38. Xylenol was injected 1 d prior to euthanasia to mark the mineralized tissue for more accurate quantification of the cells in the resting zone. (B–D) Representative confocal images of tibial growth plates at P23 (B) and P38 (C and D). Mice were treated with vehicle (C) or GH (D). GFP+ LRCs were restricted to the resting zone below the SOC. (E) Quantification of GFP+ cells. Data represent mean ± SD, n = 4 mice/group. ***P < 0.001 (unpaired t test).
As expected, doxycycline exposure led to widespread GFP labeling throughout the growth plate (Fig. 3B). After the chase, LRCs were predominantly observed in the resting zone, consistent with the presence of a slow-dividing population in this region (Fig. 3C). GH-treated mice exhibited a significant reduction in the number of LRCs compared to controls (Fig. 3 D and E).
This decline in LRCs, together with the GH-induced increase in proliferation in the resting zone (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2), indicates that GH stimulates the division of slow-cycling cells, leading to dilution of the H2B-GFP label. These findings support the idea that GH may regulate the activity of cartilaginous stem cells, which we test below.

##  Growth Plate Stem Cells Renew via Population Asymmetry.
To understand how GH influences the behavior of cartilaginous stem cells, we first sought to characterize their mode of self-renewal, which remains undefined in the growth plate. Generally, stem cells renew via either invariant asymmetry, where their number remains constant, or population asymmetry, where the stem cells behave as a population and their number can be regulated (26, 27). In the former case, the number of clones generated by stem cells and the size of the clones are expected to be constant with time, whereas in the latter case, the number of clones will decrease, but their size will increase due to the loss of some stem cells and the expansion of others.
We adopted an unbiased clonal tracing strategy previously established in hair follicles, intestines, and other renewing tissues (24, 28). All growth plate cells were labeled in Col2a1-CreERT2:R26R-Confetti mice and chased for a month, allowing transient cells to be eliminated via cell turnover but stable clones to persist (5). Thereafter, clonal dynamics was monitored over several months (Fig. 4A). The distance from the overlying secondary ossification center (SOC) to the uppermost cell in each clone did not change significantly with time (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), suggesting that mostly clones continuously renewed from the topmost cell were encountered.
Clonal dynamics in the growth plate indicate population asymmetry. (A) Experimental design. Col2-CreERT2:R26R-Confetti mice were pulsed with tamoxifen at 1 mo of age, and clonal patterns were analyzed over a 6-mo chase. (B and C) Representative images of clones at 1 (B) and 6 (C) months postlabeling. The white arrowhead in (C) marks a clone detached from the resting zone. (D and E) Quantification of clonal size (D) and density (E) at 1–6 mo postlabeling. (F and G) Clonal scaling calculated as the ratio of each clone’s size to the average at that time point (F), and log-transformed (G) to derive the correlation coefficient. SOC = secondary ossification center.
Over time, the number of clones decreased while individual clone size increased (Fig. 4 B–E). This pattern is characteristic of stochastic drift in a zero-sum system, commonly modeled as a Markov process, and is indicative of population asymmetry (24, 28). Sporadic clonal loss was observed even 6 mo postlabeling (arrowhead, Fig. 4C). When clone sizes were normalized to the average size at each time point (clonal scaling), they followed an exponential distribution (Fig. 4 F and G), consistent with neutral stem cell competition (28). A similar scaling pattern was observed in a similar experiment, but with clonal tracing initiated at postnatal day 6 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B).
These findings indicate that growth plate stem cells undergo renewal via population asymmetry. This renewal mode implies that not only the modulation of proliferative activity but also dynamic changes in stem cell number may occur in response to intrinsic or extrinsic cues.

##  GH Depletes the Number of Stem Cells in the Growth Plate.
To evaluate the effect of GH on epiphyseal stem cells, we focused on the best-characterized PTHrP-expressing population (4, 25, 29, 30). Pthrp-mCherry reporter mice were treated with GH for 10 d (Fig. 5A). Surprisingly, this revealed a significant reduction in the number of PTHrP+ stem cells in GH-treated animals (Fig. 5 B–D).

(B–D) Representative images of tibial growth plates from vehicle- (B) and GH-treated (C) mice; quantification of mCherry+ cells shown in (D). A t-PMT signal is included to highlight the border between SOC and the growth plate. (E–G) Images of EdU-labeled PTHrP+ cells in vehicle (E) and GH-treated (F) mice. Double-positive mCherry+EdU+ cells (yellow arrowheads) were quantified (G). The top 50 μm of the growth plate is demarcated by dashed lines. Confocal image panels in B, C, E, and F were obtained by stitching adjacent tile scans of high magnification.
To assess whether GH influences the proliferation of cells in the resting zone, mice were pulsed with EdU four times daily during the final treatment days (Fig. 5A). EdU incorporation in mCherry+ cells was unchanged between vehicle and GH-treated groups (Fig. 5 E–G). Similarly, immunostaining for the Ki67 marker for cycling cells showed a mild, nonsignificant decrease in proliferative PTHrP+ cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–C).
Because mCherry expression may reflect changes not only in cell number but also in PTHrP gene expression, we analyzed another established stem cell marker, CD73 (5). GH treatment similarly reduced the number of CD73+ cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 D–F). Immunostaining for phosphorylated STAT5 demonstrated activation of GH signaling in both mCherry+ and CD73+ stem cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 G and H), suggesting their direct responsiveness to GH.
Together, these data indicate that high doses of GH reduce the number of stem cells in the growth plate, and this effect is not attributable to altered proliferative activity.

##  GH Activates Stem Cells.
The reduction in stem cell number following GH treatment contrasts with our earlier observation of increased proliferation within the top 50 μm of the growth plate. To resolve this apparent contradiction and further examine stem cell behavior, we used Pthrp-CreERT2:R26R-tdTomato mice (4), in which PTHrP+ stem cells and their progeny can be genetically traced.
Tamoxifen was administered on postnatal days 25 and 26 to label stem cells, followed by a 10-d GH or vehicle treatment from P28–P38 (Fig. 6A). Due to the weak recombination efficiency of the PTHrP-CreERT2 strain at this age, only 2 to 6 stem cells were found recombined per the growth plate section of 160 μm thickness (Fig. 6B), allowing each labeled cell to be considered as an individual clone.

(B and C) Representative confocal scans of tibial growth plates showing sparse recombination at P27 (B) and clones at P38 in vehicle-treated mice (C). Maximum intensity projection of 160 µm-thick z-stack confocal scans is shown. Two weeks after labeling, clones of varying sizes were observed (Fig. 6C), indicative of diverse activity among stem cells—a hallmark of population asymmetry. GH treatment significantly increased clone size (P = 0.011, Mann–Whitney test; Fig. 6D). Clone stratification (Fig. 6E) revealed a GH-dependent decline in the proportion of single-labeled cells—likely quiescent cells, which have not undergone a single division over the 13-d-long tracing time (Fig. 6F), no change in the frequency of two-cell clones (Fig. 6G), and a greater than twofold increase in large clones (>5 cells) (Fig. 6H). Similar findings were observed in the femoral growth plate (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).
These results indicate that GH activates growth plate stem cells, promoting the generation of immediate progeny while simultaneously depleting the pool of quiescent stem cells.

##  Ablation of the GHR Impairs Clone-Forming Activity of PTHrP+ Stem Cells.
To determine whether the regulation of PTHrP+ stem cells by GH is stem cell–autonomous, we generated mice carrying one or two floxed alleles of the GHR, in combination with a Tomato reporter and PTHrP-CreERT2 transgenes (Fig. 7A). Mice with one floxed GHR allele were designated as controls, while those with two floxed alleles were classified as conditional knockouts (cKOPTHrP). To achieve efficient recombination, tamoxifen was administered at postnatal day 6 (P6), an age previously reported to yield reasonable recombination efficiency in this Cre strain (4, 30). The mice were analyzed at postnatal day 38 (P38) to maintain consistency with previous experiments (Fig. 7 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7).
Ablation of GHR in PTHrP+ Stem cells reduces their clonal expansion. (A) Experimental design: PTHrP-CreERT2:R26R-tdTomato mice carrying one or two floxed copies of the GHR were administered tamoxifen at postnatal day 6 (P6) and analyzed at postnatal day 38 (P38). (B and C) Representative confocal images of tibial growth plates showing chondrocyte column formation derived from PTHrP+ stem cells in mice with one (B, control) or two (C, GHR cKO) copies of GHR deleted. The growth plate is demarcated by dashed lines. The zone of primary spongiosa is indicated for orientation. (D) Quantification of Tomato-positive columns spanning the entire width of the tibial growth plate. A total of ten 30 µm-thick sections were quantified and averaged for each mouse. Data are presented as mean ± SD, with n = 5 mice per group. ****P < 0.0001 (unpaired t test).
Recombination efficiency was indeed enhanced when tamoxifen was administered at P6, resulting in a higher number of labeled cells and columns at P38 compared to the experiment described in Fig. 6. Notably, a marked reduction in the number of large clones was observed in GHRcKOPTHrP mice (Fig. 7 B and C). Due to the increased recombination efficiency, clonal analysis could not be performed; instead, we quantified the number of Tomato-labeled columns spanning the entire width of the growth plate. This analysis revealed a significant reduction in the number of such columns in GHRcKOPTHrP mice, indicating that PTHrP+ cells formed fewer chondrocyte columns in the absence of GHR (Fig. 7D). Similar results were obtained from the femoral growth plates (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).
Because the number of columns formed by PTHrP+ stem cells generally decreases with age (4), it was technically challenging to assess their abundance at later time points using standard tissue sectioning. To overcome this limitation, we cleared the entire tibiae from two 75-d-old GHRcKOPTHrP mice and corresponding controls, labeled Tomato-positive columns with anti-RFP antibodies, and imaged the samples by light-sheet microscopy. We then quantified all columns within each bone (Movies S1 and S2). This approach revealed an average of 31 ± 8 columns per tibia in control mice compared with 15 ± 4 columns in GHRcKOPTHrP mice. Although the small number of animals precluded formal statistical analysis, these data support the same loss of clones observed at earlier time points with either PTHrP-CreERT2 (Fig. 7) or Col2-CreERT2 (Fig. 2).
These findings demonstrate that the ability of PTHrP+ stem cells to form chondrocyte columns is directly dependent on GHR signaling, highlighting the importance of local GH receptor activity in maintaining stem cell function.

##  PTHrP+ Cells Are Present in the Human Adolescent Growth Plate.
Human growth plates differ substantially from those of mice; for example, they disappear after puberty, whereas mouse growth plates persist throughout life (31, 32). This raises the question of how directly mouse data can be translated to human physiology. To support the clinical relevance of the above mouse experiments, we examined whether PTHrP+ cells are present in human growth plates. Immunofluorescent staining revealed distinct PTHrP+ cells within the resting zone of adolescent growth plates (Fig. 8A). These findings suggest that GH may act directly on PTHrP+ cells in the human growth plate, analogous to the mechanism observed in mice, thereby strengthening the clinical relevance of our results.

##  Discussion
In this study, we investigated how GH regulates cell activity in the mouse growth plate, with a particular focus on cartilaginous stem cells. Our findings demonstrate that i) GH induces STAT5 phosphorylation within the resting zone of the growth plate; ii) GH promotes chondrocyte proliferation; iii) GH reduces the number of LRCs; iv) stem cells in the growth plate undergo self-renewal via population asymmetry; and v) GH directly regulates the activity of cartilage stem cells (Fig. 8B).
We interpret these data as follows: GH regulates a balance between stem-cell-renewing and lineage-restricted divisions of cartilaginous stem cells, and under pharmacological treatment with GH, stem cells more often undergo lineage-restricted than self-renewing cell divisions. This shift results in a depletion of the slow-dividing stem cell pool and an expansion of committed progenitors. The committed progenitors generate flat, highly proliferative chondrocytes (i.e., transit-amplifying cells), leading to an overall increase in proliferation throughout the growth plate. Additionally, GH likely promotes the proliferation of transit-amplifying flat chondrocytes directly, as we observed their reduced proliferation within columns lacking the GH receptor.
The intersection of endocrinology and stem cell biology is well established, particularly in tissues like the mammary gland epithelium (33, 34). In relation to GH, it has been implicated in the regulation of intestinal, neural, and hematopoietic stem cells (35–40). However, compelling in vivo evidence related to the ability of GH to regulate stem cells remains limited (41). In this context, our findings provide experimental support that GH can regulate stem cells, including their number and activity. Given GH’s anabolic roles in adult tissues such as muscle and adipose, these observations may also have implications spanning beyond the skeleton.
In children, GH’s primary physiological role is to promote growth. GH stimulates longitudinal growth both directly and indirectly through systemic production of IGF-1 in the liver (1). However, our pharmacological administration of GH resulted in a paradoxical decrease in serum IGF-1 levels and downregulation of expression of several IGF-1-related components in the liver. Notably, a prior study using a comparable GH dose in intact rats also reported no significant increase in serum IGF-1 (23). These findings suggest that pharmacological GH administration may trigger a homeostatic compensatory response, leading to suppression of the IGF-1 system. Consequently, the observed growth-promoting effects are likely mediated directly by GH rather than through liver-secreted IGF-1. This interpretation is further supported by the elevated levels of phosphorylated STAT5 in the resting zone of the growth plate, indicating direct GH signaling. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating direct GH effects on growth in rats (13) and human growth plate explants (42). Perhaps the strongest support for this direct action model comes from our experiments involving the genetic ablation of GH receptors in PTHrP+ stem cells, which demonstrate that PTHrP+ stem cells lacking GH receptors have a diminished capacity to form chondrocytes. Thus, the regulation of growth plate stem cells offers a logical cellular mechanism for the direct action of GH within the growth plate.
Based on the earlier experiments showing that GH increases the number of LRCs (22), we were expecting that GH would promote the number and activity of cartilaginous stem cells. Unexpectedly, GH treatment led to a decrease in stem cell number as well as in the number of LRCs. Several factors may account for this discrepancy, including differences in labeling strategy, GH dose, species, administration route, and physiological status of the animals used. Perhaps the most important difference is that the previous study used physiological GH doses in hypophysectomized rats, while in the current study, we used a pharmacological dose in GH-sufficient mice. Thus, it is plausible that GH augments both stem cell number and activity under physiological conditions but causes stem cell depletion under pharmacological exposure.
From this perspective, it is interesting to discuss the mode of stem cell renewal in the growth plate. Our clonal lineage tracing experiments indicate that renewal occurs via population asymmetry—a mode where stem cells act as a dynamic collective. This strategy, common among adult tissue stem cells (26), provides flexibility to adjust both renewal rate and stem cell number in response to systemic cues. For example, physiological doses of GH may promote symmetrical division, whereas pharmacological doses cause a shift toward lineage-restricted stem cell division. It also raises the possibility that the stem cell depletion we observed is reversible after GH withdrawal—a question that remains to be tested. Notably, ablation of GHR using either Col2-CreERT2 or PTHrP-CreERT2 resulted in fewer clones. This reduction could reflect either decreased commitment to lineage-restricted division or the competitive disadvantage of GHR-deficient stem cells relative to their wild-type counterparts, as would be predicted under a population asymmetry strategy (26). Distinguishing between these alternatives remains challenging at present.
The underlying mechanisms by which GH regulates cartilage stem cell behavior remain to be elucidated. The planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway is one of the most important regulators of symmetric vs. asymmetric stem cell division (43, 44). In the growth plate, the role of PCP signaling is well established, particularly in organizing columnar structures (45, 46) and, to a lesser extent, in regulating cartilage stem cells (25). To the best of our knowledge, no studies have directly linked GH to PCP; however, sporadic reports describe crosstalk between the STAT pathway and PCP signaling (47–49). It is therefore plausible that GH influences stem cell behavior indirectly by modulating PCP activity. Another potential mechanism involves the TGF-β pathway, which was recently reported to be regulated by GH in human cartilage-derived stem cells (42). TGF-β signaling is well recognized as a central regulator of stem cell behavior (50). Thus, these observations underscore the need for further studies to elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing cartilage stem cell maintenance and renewal, whereas our present findings suggest that GH may play an important role in shaping these mechanisms.
Our results may have clinical implications. GH therapy in children is often most effective during the first 1 to 2 y, after which growth velocity declines toward baseline levels, reducing therapeutic benefit (51, 52). If pharmacological GH similarly depletes the stem cell pool in humans, this could partially explain the reduced efficacy over time. The observed mechanism may also be related to the phenomenon of catch-up growth that often occurs when GH is discontinued (53). These possibilities warrant exploration of intermittent GH therapy strategies, especially in non-GH-deficient children.
This study has several limitations. Due to the common use of GH in non-GH-deficient children, our study was purposefully focused on non-GH-deficient mice. At the same time, the question of how GH regulates stem cells in a GH-deficient context remains to be explored. Another limitation is related to the role of IGF-1. While experiments with GHR ablation suggest the direct action of GH, the contribution of locally produced IGF-1 remains plausible. Finally, the downstream molecular mechanisms by which GH influences stem cell behavior in the growth plate remain to be elucidated.
In conclusion, our findings suggest that pharmacological GH increases the activity of stem cells in the growth plate but may also lead to their depletion. This highlights a potentially important cellular mechanism underpinning both the therapeutic action and the diminishing long-term efficacy of GH treatment.

##  Methods

##  Patient Information and Ethics.
Epiphysiodesis surgery was performed in healthy adolescent children with idiopathic tall stature according to local guidelines. The sample analyzed in this study was obtained from a 14-y-old male with no comorbidities and a body mass index within the normal range. The procedure was carried out at Karolinska University Hospital, Stockholm, Sweden. Collection of human growth plate biopsies was approved by the Karolinska Institutet Research Ethics Committee North (Karolinska University Hospital, Stockholm, Sweden). Written informed consent was obtained from both caregivers and written assent from the patient, and documentation was archived in the medical records.
Human growth plate biopsies were collected from the distal femur and proximal tibia during epiphysiodesis by an experienced orthopedic surgeon under X-ray guidance. The tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS overnight and subsequently processed for cryosectioning.

##  Animals.
The mouse strains used here were previously described (4, 24, 54) and/or commercially available: Col2a1-CreERT2, R26R-Confetti, Pthrp-mCherry (JAX #032872), Pthrp-CreERT2 (JAX #032873), R26-tdTomato (JAX #007908), and H2B-GFP:Tet-On (JAX #005104 and JAX #006965). All animal experiments were performed and animals were humanely euthanized in accordance with Swedish Board of Agriculture regulations (SJVFS 2019:9) and approved ethical permits (Dnr 5.8.18-17010/2021 from the Gothenburg Animal Ethics Board; Dnr 18160-2019 from the Stockholm North Ethical Committee).

##  Tissue Processing.
Following euthanasia with an overdose of isoflurane, mouse legs were dissected and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% formaldehyde in Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) on a roller. Samples were then decalcified in 10% Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), incubated overnight in 30% sucrose, and embedded in Optimal cutting temperature compound. Sections of 30 to 160 μm thickness were prepared at –20 °C and imaged using a Zeiss LSM700 or LSM880 confocal microscope. For immunohistochemistry or EdU detection, 30 to 50 μm sections were typically used. For clonal analysis—including rare clones, such as those in PTHrP-CreERT2:tdTomato mice—sections of 100 to 160 μm were acquired and scanned throughout the depth of the tissue using confocal microscopy.

##  GH Administration.
Mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of human recombinant GH (5 mg/kg body weight) or vehicle control at the same volume. Both the GH and the corresponding vehicle were generously provided by Pfizer Inc. Specific treatment durations and conditions are described in the corresponding figure legends.

##  Clonal Analysis.
Clonal analysis was performed as described previously (5, 55), with minor modifications. Clones in Col2a1-CreERT2:R26R-Confetti mice were defined as groups of same-color cells (more than three cells) in which the maximum distance between neighboring cells did not exceed one cell diameter (~10 μm).
PTHrP-CreERT2:tdTomato clonal analysis. To take into consideration the rarity of recombination, disorganized cell patterning in the resting zone, and the slow division rate of stem cells, the maximum distance between potentially related cells was extended to 20 μm, allowing for the possibility that an unlabeled daughter cell from an adjacent division could intercalate between two labeled progeny. To improve confidence, clones were only defined if no other labeled cells were observed within 40 μm. If another labeled cell was present between 20 and 40 μm, the cluster was excluded from analysis. Labeled cells separated by more than 40 μm were considered part of independent clones.

##  Labeling with Xylenol and Calcein.
Xylenol (90 mg/kg body weight) and calcein (10 mg/kg) (from Sigma-Aldrich Inc) were injected intraperitoneally 72 and 24 h prior to euthanasia, respectively.

##  EdU Labeling and Detection.
EdU (ThermoFisher, E10187) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 65 mg/kg body weight. The number of injections varied depending on the experiment and is indicated in the relevant figure legends. EdU detection was performed using a click-chemistry–based protocol: Sections were incubated in a solution containing 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 2 mM CuSO4, 0.1 M ascorbic acid, and 2 μM Alexa Fluor 647 azide, protected from light. Quantification was performed manually using Imaris software or automatically using ImageJ.

##  Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence.
Immunostaining was performed on 30 to 50-µm cryosections. For antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with 0.1% trypsin for 30 to 45 min at room temperature when staining with anti-mCherry (Abcam, Cat. No. ab213511), anti-Ki67 (Invitrogen, Cat. No. MA5-14520), or anti-CD73 (BioLegend, Cat. No. 127207). Alternatively, sections were boiled in DAKO antigen retrieval buffer for 1 min when staining with anti–phospho-STAT5 (Cell Signaling, Cat. No. 9359). For immunofluorescence staining with anti-tdTomato (SICGEN, Cat. No. AB1140), anti-PTHrP (Santa Cruz, sc-53936), and anti-γ-tubulin (Proteintech, Cat. Nos. 26195-1-AP and 66320-1-Ig), sections were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h. After blocking in 3% horse serum in PBS for 1 h, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following washes in PBST (PBS with 0.05% Tween-20), sections were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 60 to 90 min.
Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI before mounting.

##  Quantification of Cell Orientation Angle.
Cell orientation angle was quantified as described previously (PMID: 28994649). Chondrocyte dyads were selected from the resting zone of Col2-CreERT:GHRflox/flox:R26R-Confetti mice and heterozygous littermate controls that received a single tamoxifen pulse at P6 and were analyzed at P28. Only Confetti-positive dyads were included. The angle formed by the nuclei within each dyad was measured relative to the mediolateral axis of the growth plate using the angle tool in Fiji. A total of 100 dyads were quantified from three biological replicates per experimental group.

##  Tissue Clearing and 3D Imaging of Tomato Clones in the Entire Tibia.
Tissue clearing of immunofluorescence-labeled Pthrp-CreERT;tdTomato mouse hindlimbs was performed as described (56). Briefly, PFA-fixed hindlimbs were permeabilized using a methanol gradient (20 to 60%), followed by chemical bleaching with 3% H2O2 and 20% Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in methanol overnight. Samples were further dehydrated through 80% and 100% methanol, followed by delipidation in dichloromethane (DCM) overnight. After delipidation, samples were rehydrated through a reverse methanol gradient and decalcified with 20% formic acid in 10% sodium citrate overnight.
Decalcified hindlimbs were washed twice with 2× Saline-Sodium Citrate buffer with 0.1% Tween-20 and major muscles were carefully removed with forceps to facilitate antibody penetration. Samples were then permeabilized overnight in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100, 0.1% deoxycholate, 10% DMSO, and 25 mM EDTA. The following day, tissues were blocked in PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100, 10% DMSO, 5% normal donkey serum, and 25 mM EDTA for 8 h at 37 °C, and subsequently incubated with anti-RFP antibody (SICGEN, Cat. No. AB1140) for 3 d at 37 °C. After washing three times in PBS (including one overnight wash), samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 546 donkey anti-goat secondary antibody in blocking buffer for 3 d, followed by overnight washing in PBS.
For clearing, samples were passed through a methanol gradient (20 to 80%), dehydrated three times in 100% methanol, washed twice in DCM for 15 min each, and finally cleared in dibenzyl ether.
3D fluorescence imaging was performed using a LaVision Ultramicroscope Blaze (Miltenyi Biotec). Autofluorescence was acquired with 405 nm laser excitation. Images were processed with MACS iQ View (Miltenyi Biotec) and analyzed using Imaris v10.0.1 (Bitplane).

##  Statistical Analysis.
Data are presented as mean ± SD, unless otherwise noted. Statistical comparisons between GH-treated and control (vehicle-treated) groups were made using either unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests or the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test, depending on data distribution. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used when more than two experimental groups were compared. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.1.1.
