Tamoxifen induces permanent growth arrest through selective induction of
apoptosis in growth plate chondrocytes in cultured rat metatarsal bones

Abstract
Estrogen affects skeletal growth and promotes growth plate fusion in humans. High doses of estrogen have been used to limit growth in girls
with predicted extreme tall stature; a treatment which has been associated with severe side effects. Selective estrogen receptor modulators
(SERMs) could potentially be used as an alternative treatment. We chose to study the effects of Tamoxifen (Tam), a first generation SERM that has
been used in the treatment of pubertal gynecomastia or McCune–Albright syndrome.
Cultured fetal rat metatarsal bones were used to study the effects of Tam on longitudinal bone growth. In sectioned bones, chondrocyte
apoptosis and proliferation were analyzed by TUNEL assay and BrdU incorporation, respectively. We also used a human chondrocytic cell line,
HSC-2/8, to study the effects of Tam on apoptosis (FACS analysis and Cell Death detection ELISA) and caspase activation (caspase substrate
cleavage and Western immunoblotting).
Tam caused a dose-dependent growth retardation of cultured metatarsal bones. No catch-up growth was observed after Tam was removed from
the culture medium. Detailed analysis of sectioned growth plate cartilage revealed increased apoptosis of chondrocytes within the resting and
hypertrophic zones. HCS-2/8 cells also underwent apoptosis upon Tam treatment. Tam-induced apoptosis was caspase-dependent and completely
abrogated by either caspase-8 or -9 inhibitors. A substrate assay revealed that caspase-8 is first activated followed by caspase-9 and -3. Finally,
FasL secretion was stimulated by Tam and blocking of either FasL or Fas decreased Tam-induced apoptosis in chondrocytes.
We here describe a novel mechanism of tamoxifen-induced apoptosis in chondrocytes, involving the activation of caspases and the FasL/Fas
pathway, which diminishes the potential for bone growth.

Introduction
The importance of estrogens in the regulation of longitudinal
bone growth is unequivocal. The discovery of a man with an
inactivating mutation in the estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) gene
and patients with aromatase deficiency have demonstrated the
importance of estrogen signaling for growth cessation and
closure of the growth plate [1,29,37]. Moreover, the demonstra-
tion of estrogen receptors within growth plate chondrocytes in
different species including humans, suggests that estrogens
affect longitudinal bone growth through direct actions within
the growth plate [21,30,31].
High dose estrogen therapy has been used to induce
premature growth plate fusion in order to prevent extreme tall
stature in girls [16]. However, the treatment is associated with
potential severe side effects, such as decreased fertility [40] and
a fear of increased risk of breast and uterus cancer [14].
Therefore, alternative treatment options need to be developed.
Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) have tissue
specific estrogenic actions and could therefore potentially be
used to prevent extreme tall stature and avoid at least some of
the side effects of estrogen therapy. For example, raloxifene,
previously shown to increase bone mineral density in

SERMs display selective tissue-specific agonistic and
antagonistic effects due to different binding affinities to ERα
and/or estrogen receptor beta (ERβ). Moreover, tissue-selective
actions of SERMs are modulated by ligand-dependent con-
formational changes of ERα and/or ERβ followed by the
recruitment of tissue-specific co-activators or co-repressors
[27]. Tamoxifen, the first SERM that became available, is
therapeutically used in breast cancer due to its anti-ERα
activity. However, also ERα-negative breast cancer cells often
respond to tamoxifen therapy through known non-genomic
actions of tamoxifen [11,15,34].
Tamoxifen has pro-estrogenic effects inhibiting bone mass
loss in bone tissue [24]. However, any effects of tamoxifen on
longitudinal bone growth are largely unknown. Tamoxifen
therapy in boys with pubertal gynecomastia has been reported to
decrease [22] or not affect longitudinal bone growth [8]. In girls
with McCune–Albright syndrome, tamoxifen treatment has
been associated with decreased growth velocity [9]. Based on
these findings, it could be speculated that tamoxifen has the
potential to be used therapeutically to limit growth in children
with extreme tall stature. Supporting this is the narrowing of the
growth plate of gonadoectomized rats treated with tamoxifen
[12,13,23]. However, a recent report suggests rather a
stimulatory effect of tamoxifen on bone growth in intact mice
[33].
The present study was designed to investigate any local
effects of tamoxifen in growth plate cartilage. To address this
question, the effects of tamoxifen on organ cultures of fetal rat
metatarsal bones and on a human chondrocytic cell line were
examined.
Materials and methods
Materials
Recombinant human IGF-1 was kindly supplied by Pharmacia Corp.
(Stockholm, Sweden). Dexamethasone, Tamoxifen, BSA and β-glyceropho-
sphate were from Sigma-Aldrich, Inc. (Steinheim, Germany). Phosphate buffer
saline (PBS), DMEM/F12 medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), Trypsin–EDTA
solution and gentamycin were all purchased from Invitrogen, Inc. (Paisley,
UK). Charcoal treated fetal bovine serum (CTS) was from HyClone (Logan,
Utah). Ascorbic acid was from ICN Corp. (Aurora, Ohio). Inhibitors for
caspase-8 (Z-IETD-FMK) and caspase-9 (Z-LEHD-FMK) were purchased
from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN), dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide
(DMSO) and diluted (1:1000) to reach a final concentration of 50 μM in the
medium. Protein molecular standards for Western immunoblots were purchased
from Bio-Rad Laboratories (Hercules, CA).
Cell culture
The human chondrocyte-like cell line HCS-2/8, established by Dr. M.
Takigawa [38], is very well characterized. HCS-2/8 cells express several
markers of primary chondrocytes such as collagen type II, aggrecans and have
rounded cell morphology. Furthermore, the cells have the ability to differentiate
and form cartilage nodules expressing collagen type X. HCS-2/8 cells were
cultured in DMEM/F12 mixture medium supplemented with 20% FBS exactly
as recently described [2]. In all experiments performed, phenol red free DMEM/
F12 medium supplemented with 1% CTS was used.
Organ culture
Fetal rat metatarsal bones were collected from 20-day-old embryos [7]. The
three middle metatarsals were dissected from each hind leg and cultured as
previously described [3]. The length of each metatarsal bone was measured at
days 0, 2, 5, 7, 12, 19, and 26 of culture using the Image-Pro® Plus software
(Media Cybernetics Inc., Berkshire, UK). For each time point, the length is
expressed as a percent of the length on the day when the experiment was started
(day 0). All isolated bones (usually 30 to 40 per experiment) were pooled and
then randomly divided into the different experimental groups (3 to 5 bones per
group). Each experiment was repeated at least 3 times. The study was approved
by the Northern Stockholm Ethical Committee on Animal care at Karolinska
Institutet.
Collagen type X immunohistochemistry
Fetal rat metatarsal bones were fixed in 4% formaldehyde, paraffin
embedded and 5-μm-thick sections were obtained. Immunohistochemistry
was performed as previously described [31] with the following modification.
After antigen retrieval in citrate buffer at 95 °C and peroxidase quench in 3%
H2O2, the sections were additionally treated with 5 mg/ml hyaluronidase
(Sigma-Aldrich, Inc, Steinheim, Germany) for 30 min at 37 °C. Anti-collagen
type X primary antibody was purchased from Quartett (Quartett GmbH, Berlin,
Germany). Secondary anti-mouse biotinilated antibody was purchased from
DakoCytomation A/S (Glostrup, Denmark).
Cell proliferation assay; bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) labeling
For BrdU labeling, 2.5 h prior to fixation fetal rat metatarsal bones were
washed once in PBS and the DMEM/F12 medium was changed to MEM
medium supplemented with BrdU labeling reagent (1:200 dilution of labeling
reagent, Cell Proliferation Kit RPN20, Amersham Biosciences, Buckingham-
shire, UK). The bones were then fixed in 4% formaldehyde, decalcified in 10%
EDTA and 5-μm-thick paraffin-embedded sections were obtained. Detection of
BrdU-positive cells was performed according to the manufacture's instructions
with the following modifications. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate
buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min at 94 °C–96 °C. Detection of bound mouse anti-BrdU
antibody was achieved by using an anti-mouse FITC conjugated antibody (Santa
Cruz Biotech, Inc. Santa Cruz, CA). Sections were then washed and embedded
in DAPI containing mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, Inc. Burlingame,
CA). The level of proliferation is expressed as the percent FITC-positive cells of
the total cell number (DAPI positive cells) in the same area (see Digital
Automatic Cell Counting protocol).
Apoptosis assay (TUNEL)
Apoptotic cells were identified by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase
(TdT)-mediated deoxy-UTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) immunohistochemistry
according to instructions for the TdT-FragEL™DNA fragmentation kit
(Oncogene Research, Boston, MA) with the following modifications. The
sections were treated with 5 μg/ml proteinase K for 10 min [26]. DNA
incorporation of biotin-labeling nucleotides was detected by streptavidin
conjugated to Alexa Fluor® 546 (Invitrogen Inc.). Slides were embedded in
DAPI-containing mounting medium (Vector Laboratories Inc.). The level of
apoptosis is expressed as the percent Alexa-546 positive cells of the total cell
number (DAPI positive cells) in the same area (see Digital Automatic Cell
Counting protocol). Control experiments showed that when pre-treating sections
with distilled water instead of TdT, all cells were negative, whereas pre-
treatment with DNase enabled labeling of all cells.
Digital automatic cell counting
To achieve automatic quantitation of the numbers of proliferating and
apoptotic cells, we developed a special protocol. After detection of BrdU-
positive cells (green color originated from FITC) or TUNEL-positive cells (red
color produced by Alexa-546), images were recorded with a Nikon DP70 digital
camera. For counting cell numbers, 100× magnification was used. From each

Cell viability assay (WST-1)
We used a cell proliferation reagent 4-[3-(4-iodophenyl)-2(4-nitrophenyl)-
2H-5-tetrazolio]-1,3-benzene disulfonate (WST-1; Roche Diagnostics GmbH,
Mannheim, Germany) which is a colorimetric assay for the quantification of cell
proliferation and cell viability based on activity of mitochondrial enzymes. The
assay was performed according to manufacturer's instructions. HCS-2/8 cells
were cultured in 96-well plates and treated in phenol red free DMEM/F12
medium supplemented with 1% CTS. After treatment, the WST-1 reagent was
added, 20 μl/well (final dilution 1:10), and the reaction mixture was incubated
for 1 h at 37 °C before reading absorbance at 450 nm. Each experiment included
three wells and was repeated at least four times.
Cell death detection ELISA
Apoptosis of HSC-2/8 cells was studied through the detection and
quantification of cytoplasmic histone-associated DNA fragments (mono- and
oligonucleasomes) using a photometric enzyme immunoassay (Cell Death
Detection ELISAPLUS, Roche Diagnostics) as recently described [4]. Three
samples were analyzed for each experiment, which was repeated four times.
Western immunoblotting
Western blot analysis of protein expression was performed as recently
described [4]. The primary antibodies against caspase-3 (1:2000 dilution),
PARP (1:1000), Bax (1:1000) and FasL (1:1000) were purchased from Santa
Cruz Biotechnology Inc., and Bcl-xS (1:500) from Abcam plc (Cambridge,
UK). The primary antibodies against Bid (1:500), caspase-8 (1:1000) and
caspase-9 (1:500) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly,
MA).
Annexin-V/propidium iodide double labeling
HCS-2/8 cells were treated with 10 μM Tam for 24, 48 or 72 h in phenol red
free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 1% CTS. Double labeling with
annexin V (detection of phosphatidylserine exposure) and propidium iodide
(detection of membrane integrity) was performed using Annexin-V-FLUOS
Staining kit (Roche Diagnostic) according to manufacturer's instructions. The
stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.
Fluorometric caspase-3, -8 and -9 activity assay
The HCS-2/8 chondrocytes were treated with 10 μM Tam for 6, 12, 24, 48 or
72 h in DMEM/F12 phenol red free medium supplemented with 1% CTS. The
cells were collected, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, counted, transferred to a
caspase-assay plate and kept at −80 °C for further analysis (1×106 cells/well).
The fluorogenic peptide substrates IETD-AMC, LEHD-AMC and DEVD-AMC
(50 μM for each) (Biomol, Plymouth Meeting, PA) were used to measure
activity of caspase-8, -9 and -3, respectively. Caspase activation analysis was
performed as previously described [10] using a Fluoroskan II plate reader
(Labsystems, Stockholm, Sweden). Fluorescence units were converted to
picomoles and displayed as AMC release per minute.
Detection of soluble Fas ligand
HCS-2/8 cells were treated with 10 μM Tam for 24, 48 or 72 h in phenol red
free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 1% CTS. The conditioned
medium was collected, floating cells were eliminated by centrifugation and the
conditioned medium was concentrated 10 times using vacuum centrifugation for
3 h (Millipore AB, Sundbyberg, Sweden). The resulting samples were separated
on a 12.5% SDS gel and analyzed by Western immunoblotting as described
above.
Neutralizing of Fas and Fas ligand
The Fas-blocking mouse monoclonal antibody ZB4 was purchased from
MBL (Nagoya, Japan) and the FasL-specific neutralizing mouse monoclonal
antibody NOK-2 was purchased from PharMingen (San Diego, CA). In order to
block the Fas-signaling pathway, HCS-2/8 cells were pretreated either with ZB4
or NOK-2 antibodies for 1 h before Tam treatment. The level of apoptosis was
measured after 48 or 72 h of treatment with Cell Death Detection ELISA as
described above.
Statistics
Statistical differences were calculated by one-way ANOVA followed by the
Holm–Sidak method if not otherwise stated.
Results
Tamoxifen induced bone growth retardation
To validate our model, initial experiments showed that
insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I), a well known stimulator
of longitudinal bone growth, promoted growth while
dexamethasone, a well known inhibitor of bone growth,
decreased growth of fetal rat metatarsals cultured in vitro
(Fig. 1A). Tamoxifen (Tam) caused a significant growth
retardation (p<0.001 vs control at day 12) comparable to that
observed in dexamethasone treated bones (Fig. 1A). The
higher the dose of Tam used, the sooner growth cessation
occurred (Fig. 1B). Bone growth was almost completely
blocked after 2 days of culture with 10 μM Tam (Fig. 1B),
after 7 days with 5 μM Tam (Fig. 1B), after 12 days with
1 μM Tam (Fig. 1B) and after 19 days with 0.1 μM Tam
(data not shown). The difference in bone length between
control and Tam-treated bones was markedly increased with
time (Figs. 1B and C).
There were no significant differences in Tam (0.1–10 μM)
induced growth retardation between male and female bones
(163±4% vs 156±6% of bone length at day 0, respectively;
5 μM Tam, 19 days culture; p=0.43, t-test data not shown for
other concentrations). Co-treatment of Tam (1 μM) with 17β-
estradiol (1 μM) resulted in a growth retardation not signifi-
cantly different from bones treated with Tam only (178±5% vs
175±1% of bone length at day 0, respectively; 19 days culture;
p=0.64, t-test).
To study if short-term treatment with Tam may cause
permanent growth arrest, metatarsal bones were treated with 1
or 5 μM Tam for the first 5 days only and subsequently
cultured without Tam until day 26 (Fig. 2A). Bone length was
not affected during Tam treatment. However, growth arrested
after Tam had been removed from the organ cultures. Growth

Mechanisms of tamoxifen induced apoptosis
Analysis of the expression of Bcl-xL/S, Bax and Bid by
Western immunoblotting revealed that the pro-apoptotic protein
Bcl-xS was significantly up-regulated after 24 h exposure to
10 μM Tam (28±4% above control; p<0.01). The levels of the
anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-xL and the pro-apoptotic proteins
Bax and Bid were not affected by Tam (data not shown).
To investigate any involvement of caspases in Tam-induced
apoptosis, HCS-2/8 cells were treated with 10 μM Tam and the
activities of caspases-8, -9 and -3 were analyzed using specific
fluorogenic substrates. The activity of caspase-8 was increased
by 78% after 6 h of Tam treatment, whereas the activity of
caspase-9 was increased only after 12 h of Tam exposure (Fig.
6A). Caspase-3 activity was increased by 26% within 6 h and
doubled within 12 h of Tam exposure (Fig. 6A). To further
elucidate the role of caspases-8 and -9 in Tam-induced
apoptosis, HCS-2/8 cells were treated with the combination of
Tam (5 μM) and an inhibitor of either caspase-8 or -9. Both
inhibitors completely prevented Tam-induced apoptosis
(p<0.001 for caspase-8 inhibition and p<0.01 for caspase-9
inhibition, Fig. 6B). Moreover, cleavage of caspases-8, -9, -3
and caspase-3 substrate protein-PARP (poly ADP-ribose
polymerase) was observed within 24 h upon Tam (10 μM)
exposure (Fig. 6C). However when combining 1 μM Tam and
50 μM pan-caspase inhibitor treatment to fetal rat metatarsal
bones cultured for 19 days, we were not able to prevent Tam-
induced growth retardation (127±3% increase of length in
control, 86±5% in Tam, 125±2% in pan-caspase and 91±4%
in Tam plus pan-caspase treated bones).
The observed early activation of caspase-8 in HCS-2/8 cells
suggests involvement of the Fas/FasL pathway. Both the FasL-
neutralizing antibody NOK-2 and the Fas neutralizing antibody
ZB4 were able to inhibit Tam-induced apoptosis. In the presence
of the NOK-2 antibody, we observed a significant decrease in the
level of Tam-induced apoptosis after 48 h (p<0.01, Fig. 7A),
whereas in the presence of the ZB4 antibody apoptosis was
decreased after 72 h (199±15% in 5 μM Tam vs 148±4% in Tam
plus ZB4, p<0.01). Analysis of FasL expression revealed
increased secretion of FasL upon Tam treatment (Fig. 7B). The
level of secreted FasL was increased by 22% after 24 h and by
53% after 48 h of Tam treatment (p<0.01, Fig. 7C).

To understand the mechanisms of Tam-induced growth
retardation, we chose to study chondrocyte proliferation and
apoptosis in fetal rat metatarsal bones treated with Tam. Our
data clearly show that Tam-induced growth retardation is
associated with massive induction of apoptosis in both resting
and hypertrophic chondrocytes in the growth plate. Moreover,
the pattern of bone growth, including initial normal growth
velocity followed by complete cessation of growth and the
absence of catch up growth after short term (5 days) treatment,
strongly suggests that Tam induces growth arrest through
apoptotic elimination of stem-like chondrocytes within the
growth plate. We observed a high level of apoptosis also in
perichondrial cells, mainly at the sites of bone bending,
suggesting involvement of the perichondrium in bone remodel-
ing. Interestingly, Tam did not affect the level of apoptosis in the
perichondrium.
Induction of apoptosis by Tam has previously not been
reported in chondrocytes, although in several other cell types,
including glioma cells [28], osteoclasts [41], estrogen receptor
(ER)-positive and ER-negative breast cancers cell lines [34].
Thus, loss of stem-like chondrocytes by apoptosis upon Tam
treatment can cause early cessation of bone growth and
therefore diminish the growth potential. To our best knowl-
edge there are no longitudinal studies in animals exposed to
high doses of Tam and further studies are needed to
investigate the ability of Tam to eliminate bone growth
potential in vivo.
Induction of apoptosis by Tam is well known and can either
involve estrogen receptors (ERs) or occur through non-genomic
mechanisms [25]. We were not able to prevent Tam-induced
apoptosis, with the ER agonist 17β-estradiol or with the non-
selective ER antagonist ICI 182,780. These experiments
suggest that estrogen receptors are not involved in Tam-
mediated apoptosis in growth plate chondrocytes. Supporting
this, we found no differences between female and male bones
with regards to their responses to Tam. ER-independent
apoptosis induced by Tam has been reported for other cell
types, including osteoclasts [41] and ER-negative breast cancer
cells [34], effects that have been found to be mediated through
mitochondrial stress, activation of p38 MAP kinase, or FasL
[25]. We made the novel finding that Tam induces secretion of
FasL which in turn activates Fas-mediated apoptosis. Our
findings are supported by recent reports showing that similar
concentrations of Tam induces activation of the Fas-pathway
also in osteoclasts [41] and glioma cells [28]. Generally, binding
FasL to CD95/Fas initiates formation of the DISC complex and
thereby activates initiator caspase-8 which triggers the caspase
cascade [6]. Indeed, we found activation of caspase-8 as early as
6 h after Tam treatment whereas activation of caspase-9
occurred after 12 h. Early activation of caspase-8 in
chondrocytes supports that Tam-induced apoptosis occurs via
the Fas/FasL pathway. Moreover, inhibition of both caspase-8
and caspase-9 abrogated Tam-induced apoptosis but was not
able to prevent Tam-induced growth retardation. Due to high
divergence of apoptotic pathways, caspase inhibition is well
known often only to delay cell death [39]. Our data are in line
with a previous report suggesting that alternative cell death
pathways are activated when caspases are blocked with z-VAD-
fmk [39].

Always remembering to be cautious when attempting to
draw definitive conclusions from in vitro findings and to then
extrapolate these to human disorders, our observations none-
theless suggest that Tam may diminish growth potential in
patients. Tam has been reported to decrease growth velocity in
patients with McCune–Albright syndrome [9] and pubertal
gynecomastia [22]. However, another study reported no effect
of Tam on growth velocity in late pubertal boys treated for
pubertal gynecomastia [8]. Moreover, it has recently been
reported that administration of Tam to short boys also receiving
growth hormone delays maturation of their bones [20]. This
report suggests that in humans, Tam acts solely as an estrogen
antagonist and does not exert any direct effects on chondro-
cytes. However, the retrospective nature of this study, the low
number of patients involved, concurrent GH therapy and the
lack of data concerning final heights limit the reliability of the
conclusions drawn. Further follow-up is required in order to
determine the exact effect of Tam on longitudinal growth in
patients. We propose that a more careful analysis of growth
velocity and especially final height be performed in patients
treated with Tam.
To summarize, our data show that tamoxifen induces
permanent growth arrest of cultured rat metatarsal bones. We
demonstrate that the growth arrest occurs due to specific
apoptotic elimination of chondrocytes mainly in the resting
zone of the growth plate. Finally, we describe a new mechanism
of tamoxifen induced apoptosis in chondrocytes which includes
the activation of caspases and the Fas–FasL pathway.
