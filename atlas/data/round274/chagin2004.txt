Estrogen Receptor- Inhibits Skeletal Growth and Has the Capacity to
Mediate Growth Plate Fusion in Female Mice

ABSTRACT
To determine the long-term role of ER in the regulation of longitudinal bone growth, appendicular and axial
skeletal growth was followed and compared in female ER/, ER/, and ER// mice. Our results
show that ER inhibits appendicular and axial skeletal growth and has the capacity to induce fusion of the
growth plates.
Introduction: Estrogen affects skeletal growth and promotes growth plate fusion in humans. In rodents, the growth
plates do not fuse after sexual maturation, but prolonged treatment with supraphysiological levels of estradiol has the
capacity to fuse the growth plates. It should be emphasized that the estrogen receptor (ER)/ and the
ER//, but not the ER/, mouse models have clearly increased serum levels of estradiol.
Materials and Methods: The skeletal growth was monitored by X-ray and dynamic histomorphometry, and the
growth plates were analyzed by quantitative histology, calcein double labeling, bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) incor-
poration, and TUNEL assay in 4- and 18-month-old female ER/, ER/, and ER// mice.
Results: Young adult (4-month-old) ER/ mice demonstrated an increased axial- and appendicular-skeletal
growth, supporting the notion that ER inhibits skeletal growth in young adult female mice. Interestingly, the growth
plates were consistently fused in the appendicular skeleton of 18-month-old female ER/ mice. This fusion of
growth plates, caused by a prolonged exposure to supraphysiological levels of estradiol in female ER/ mice, must
be mediated through ER because old ER// mice displayed unchanged, unfused growth plates.
Conclusions: Our results conﬁrm that ER is a physiological inhibitor of appendicular- and axial-skeletal growth in
young adult female mice. Furthermore, we made the novel observation that ER, after prolonged supraphysiological
estradiol exposure, has the capacity to mediate growth plate fusion in old female mice.

INTRODUCTION
T
HE KEY ROLE OF estrogen in the regulation of the human
growth plate is supported by the ﬁnding that both males
and females with estrogen deﬁciency, caused by a mutation
in the aromatase gene, have nonfused growth plates and
continue to grow after sexual maturation.(1,2) One male
patient with a point mutation in exon 2 of the estrogen
receptor- (ER) had a similar phenotype, indicating that
ER mediates the effect of estrogen on human growth plate
fusion.(3) In rodents, the growth plates do not fuse after
sexual maturation, but prolonged supraphysiological expo-
sure to estradiol results in growth plate fusion.(4) Ovariec-
tomy of rodents results in an increase in longitudinal bone
growth,(5,6) which is reversed by treatment with estrogen.(7)
Both ER and ER are expressed in the human(8,9) and
rodent(10,11) growth plate. We have previously demonstrated
that ER inhibits skeletal growth in young adult female
mice.(12,13) In contrast, ER, but not ER, is involved in the
regulation of skeletal growth in male mice.(14) The aim of
this study was to determine the long-term role of ER in the

Genotyping of tail DNA was performed at 3 weeks of age.(14)
The mice were killed at 4 or 18 months of age.
DXA
DXA measurement was performed in 2-, 4-, 9-, and
18-month-old female mice with the Norland pDEXA Sabre
(Norland) and the Sabre Research software (version 3.6) as
previously described.(12)
Quantitative histology
Tail, right distal femora, and right proximal tibia were
ﬁxed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalciﬁed in 10% EDTA,
and embedded in parafﬁn. Sections (5 m thick) were
stained with Alcian blue/van Gieson. Images were captured
using a Nikon Eclipse E800 light microscope connected to
a Hamamatsu digital camera C4742-95 and a computer. All
histological measurements were performed in the central
two-thirds of the growth plate sections using Olympus Mi-
croImage software (version 4.0; Olympus Optical Co.,
Hamburg, Germany). The height of the growth plate was
calculated as an average of 20 measurements. For determi-
nation of column and cell densities, measurements from
four (tibia) or ﬁve (femur and vertebra) different areas of
each growth plate were averaged and normalized per milli-
meter or square millimeter. The number of proliferative and
hypertrophic cells were counted in 10 columns per growth
plate and averaged for each growth plate. Hypertrophic
chondrocytes were deﬁned by a height of more than 6 m.
The height of the terminal hypertrophic chondrocyte, the
cell in the last intact lacuna, was measured in 24 different
columns per growth plate and averaged.
Proliferation index
The DNA-replicating cells were investigated using the
immunohistochemical
detection
of
bromodeoxyuridine
(BrdU)-labeled cells. Animals were injected with BrdU
solution (RPN 20; Amersham Biosciences UK, Bucking-
hamshire, UK) 18 and 4 h before death. BrdU-labeled cells
were visualized in the femoral, tibial, and vertebral growth
plates by immunohistochemistry, using a BrdU staining kit
(RPN 20; Amersham Biosciences UK). The number of
positively stained cells was counted per section, normalized
per length of the section, and averaged for three sections for
each growth plate.
Apoptosis assay (TUNEL)
DNA fragmentation was evaluated by terminal deoxynu-
cleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated biotinylated UTP nick
end labeling (TUNEL). Detection of apoptotic cells was
performed using the TdT FragEL DNA Fragmentation De-
tection Kit (QIA33; Oncogene, Boston, MA, USA) accord-
ing to manufacturer’s protocol. The number of positively
stained cells was counted per section, normalized per length
of the section, and averaged for three sections for each
growth plate.
Calcein double labeling
To determine longitudinal growth rate, ER/, ER/,
and WT mice were injected subcutaneously twice with 25
mg/kg calcein (C-0875; Sigma Chemical Co., St Louis,
MO, USA) 6 days apart on days 7 and 1 before death.
Images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse E800 ultravi-
olet microscope connected to a Hamamatsu digital camera
C4742-95 and a computer. The inter-label distance was
calculated as an average of 20 measurements for each
growth plate using the Olympus MicroImage software (ver-
sion 4; Olympus Optical Co.).
Statistics
All data are expressed as mean  SE when appropriate.
The differences between groups (ER/ mice versus WT
mice and ER/ mice versus ER// mice) were
evaluated using an unpaired t-test.
RESULTS
It should be emphasized that the ER/ and the
ER//, but not the ER/, mouse models have
clearly increased serum levels of estradiol. (13,15) The phe-
notype in female ER/ mice might either be caused by
the disturbed sex steroid levels or the inactivation of ER.
Thus, the speciﬁc physiological role of ER cannot be
investigated using gonadally intact ER/ mice. There-
fore, in this study, we focused on the effect of ER. The
effect of ER, in the presence of physiological levels of
estradiol, was elucidated by comparing the phenotype of
ER/ and WT mice, whereas the effect of ER, in the
presence of supraphysiological levels of estradiol (and ab-
sence of ER), was elucidated by comparing the phenotypes
of ER/ and ER// mice.
ER inhibits axial skeletal growth
The crown-rump (CR) length of female WT, ER/,
ER/, and ER// mice was monitored at 2, 4, 9,
and 18 months of age. The CR length was increased in
ER/ mice at 4 months but not at 18 months of age
compared with WT mice, showing that ER inhibits axial
skeletal growth in young adult female mice (Fig. 1). The CR
length was increased in 9- and 18-month-old ER//
mice compared with ER/ mice, suggesting that ER
inhibits the axial skeletal growth in the presence of supra-
physiological levels of estradiol in old ER/ female mice

DISCUSSION
ER is not involved in the physiological regulation of
skeletal growth in male mice.(12,14,17) In contrast, this study
conﬁrms previous studies showing a role of ER in the
regulation of the female skeleton.(12,13,17,18) The reason for
this sex difference regarding the role of ER for skeletal
growth is not obvious. However, one may speculate that
inactivation of ER results in a loss of feminization of the
GH-secretory pattern, resulting in increased male-like skel-
etal growth in female ER/ mice, whereas no effect is
seen in male ER/ mice. Interestingly, the enhanced
growth of the young adult female ER/ mice, in this
study, was associated with an increased ratio of proliferative
to hypertrophic chondrocytes in the proximal tibia. This
ﬁnding might indicate that ER inhibits the number of cell
divisions in the proliferative layer. However, no signiﬁcant

In humans, estrogen induces growth plate fusion after
sexual maturation as a result of the prolonged increased
serum estrogen level.(19,20) The timing of growth plate fu-
sion can be manipulated by different pharmacologic treat-
ments, which all alter the timing of the peak of estrogenic
activity.(21,22) In rodents, it seems as if this threshold level
for estrogen-induced growth plate fusion is not normally
reached, but it is clear that it can be reached by supraphysi-
ological estradiol treatment.(4)
We searched for an animal model to test if ER has the
capacity to mediate estrogen-induced growth plate fusion.
This animal model should have supraphysiological serum
levels of estradiol and no functional ER. We and others
have previously demonstrated that both ER/ and
ER// mice, because of disturbed feedback regula-
tion, have supraphysiological serum levels of estradi-
ol.(12,13,15,17) They are both ER inactivated, and the differ-
ence between ER// and ER/ mice is that ER is
expressed in ER/ but not in ER// mice. By
comparing the growth plates of ER// and ER/
mice, we made the novel observation that ER, after pro-
longed and supraphysiological estradiol exposure, has the
capacity to mediate growth plate fusion in old female mice.
In contrast, one previous clinical observation suggested that
it is ER and not ER that is of importance for the medi-
ation of estrogen-induced growth plate closure, supported
by the absence of growth plate fusion in a male with a point
mutation in exon 2 of ER (HERKO).(3) The different
results regarding the ER speciﬁcity for growth plate fusion
might very well be because of the well-known species
difference in the regulation of growth plate fusion between
humans, with growth plate fusion directly after sexual mat-
uration, and mice, with growth plate fusion very late in life.
However, when looking critically at the results from the
HERKO human, it is of interest to note that it has not yet
been demonstrated that he expresses a functional ER.
Furthermore, the point mutation in exon 2 of ER (157
argenine) is believed to produce a 46-kDa alternative
spliced form without functional A/B domain (starts at 174
methionine) and with dominant negative properties.(23) This
short form of ER might result in an inhibition of ER
activity. Thus, although a species difference is the most
probable explanation for the different ER speciﬁcity in the
regulation of growth plate fusion in mice compared with
humans, we do not think that it can be excluded that the
HERKO human has a functional ER/ double inactiva-
tion.
In vitro experiments have indicated that, at low concen-
trations of estrogen, ER has the capacity to repress ER-
activated transcription from estrogen response elements. In
contrast, at high concentrations of ligand, ER does not
inhibit ER action, and moreover, it induces transcription
on its own.(24,25) Thus, at different concentrations of estro-
gen, ER might have the capacity to promote different
effects, and the growth plate’s fusion of ER/ mice might
be mediated through the ER at high serum levels of
estrogens.
It is interesting to note that growth plate fusion must be
regulated in different manners in the axial compared with
the appendicular skeleton, because the growth plates of tibia
and femur but not of vertebra were fused in the old female
ER/ mice. We do not have a good explanation for this
difference between the axial and appendicular skeleton.

In conclusion, our results conﬁrm that ER is a physio-
logical inhibitor of appendicular- and axial-skeletal growth
in young adult female mice. Furthermore, we made the
novel observation that ER, after prolonged and supra-
physiological estradiol exposure, has the capacity to medi-
ate growth plate fusion in old female mice. It remains to be
investigated if ER has the capacity to mediate growth plate
fusion in primates.
