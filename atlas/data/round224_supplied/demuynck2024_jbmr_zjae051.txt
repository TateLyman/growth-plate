Low–dose inﬁgratinib increases bone growth and corrects
growth plate abnormalities in an achondroplasia mouse
model

Abstract
Achondroplasia (ACH), the most common form of disproportionate short stature, is caused by gain–of–function point mutations in ﬁbroblast
growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3). Abnormally elevated activation of FGFR3 modulates chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation via multiple
signaling pathways, such as the MAPK pathway. Using a mouse model mimicking ACH (Fgfr3Y367C/+), we have previously shown that daily
treatment with inﬁgratinib (BGJ398), a selective and orally bioavailable FGFR1-3 inhibitor, at a dose of 2 mg/kg, signiﬁcantly increased bone
growth. In this study, we investigated the activity of inﬁgratinib administered at substantially lower doses (0.2 and 0.5 mg/kg, given once daily)
and using an intermittent dosing regimen (1 mg/kg every 3 days). Following a 15–day treatment period, these low dosages were sufﬁcient to
observe signiﬁcant improvement of clinical hallmarks of ACH such as growth of the axial and appendicular skeleton and skull development.
Immunohistological labeling demonstrated the positive impact of inﬁgratinib on chondrocyte differentiation in the cartilage growth plate and
the cartilage end plate of the vertebrae. Macroscopic and microcomputed analyses showed enlargement of the foramen magnum area at the
skull base, thus improving foramen magnum stenosis, a well–recognized complication in ACH. No changes in FGF23 or phosphorus levels were
observed, indicating that the treatment did not modify phosphate homeostasis. This proof–of–concept study demonstrates that inﬁgratinib
administered at low doses has the potential to be a safe and effective therapeutic option for children with ACH.
Keywords: FGFR3, achondroplasia, inﬁgratinib low dose, preclinical study, mouse model
Introduction
The fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) family consists
of four conserved transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinases,
FGFR1-4. FGFR is activated by fibroblast growth factor
(FGF) ligands, which induce dimerization of the receptor.
Once dimerized, FGFR is released from autoinhibition1 and
becomes tyrosine-phosphorylated, resulting in mobilization
of intracellular signaling, including the MAPK, PI3K/AKT,
PLCγ , and STAT pathways.2 These regulate a variety of phys-
iological and pathological processes, including cell prolifera-
tion, differentiation, morphogenesis, metabolic homeostasis,
and patterning.1,3
FGFR signaling pathways are directly involved in the regu-
lation of chondrogenesis, osteogenesis, and bone and mineral
homeostasis.4 Germline mutations in genes encoding FGFRs
are responsible for chondrodysplasias (the most frequent is
caused by FGFR3 gain–of–function mutations), craniosynos-
toses (mainly caused by FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR3 muta-
tions), and rare skeletal overgrowth syndromes (eg, camp-
todactyly, tall stature, scoliosis, and hearing loss [CATSHL]
syndrome because of an FGFR3 loss–of–function mutation)
demonstrating the critical role of FGFR signaling in osteo-
chondrodysplasia.4 In FGFR3–related chondrodysplasia, acti-
vated FGFR3 abnormally affects cartilage growth plate and
bone, resulting in disproportionate short stature, craniofacial
malformation, and other features of varying severity.5,6 These
conditions include achondroplasia (ACH), hypochondropla-
sia, severe ACH with developmental delay, and acanthosis
nigricans and thanatophoric dysplasia.
ACH is the most common nonlethal form of skeletal
dysplasia, affecting between one in 15 000 and one in 30 000
individuals.7 People with ACH have disproportionately short
stature with rhizomelia, and grow to an average final adult
height of ∼4 feet or 125 cm. Affected individuals are prone
to significant comorbidities, including sleep apnea, chronic
otitis media with conductive hearing loss, spinal stenosis, and
obesity. Particularly significantly, narrowing of the foramen
magnum contributes to an increase in sudden infant death
syndrome and may require prompt surgical intervention dur-
ing early infancy.8,9ACH is an autosomal dominant disorder
characterized by gain–of–function mutations in the FGFR3
gene, 99% of which are p.Gly380Arg substitution in the
transmembrane domain of FGFR3.10 Most cases (80%) are
sporadic and increased paternal age has been observed to have
an association with de novo ACH.11 The extended activation
of FGFR3 because of ACH gain–of–function mutation results
in a further increase in the activity of downstream signaling
pathways.12-15 Increased downstream signaling of FGFR3
in ACH disrupts normal chondrocyte proliferation and

Infigratinib (BGJ398) is an orally bioavailable, selective,
ATP–competitive inhibitor of FGFR1-3.18 In vitro, infigra-

that are involved in the regulation of proliferation and dif-

disproportionate short stature, and hearing loss, as well as

appendicular and axial skeletons and increased the length
and width of the skull and foramen magnum.19 Given the
causal role of FGFR3 gain–of–function mutation in ACH and
the initial demonstration of the efficacy of infigratinib, we
carried out systematic studies to investigate lower doses and
various dosing schedules (continuous vs intermittent dosing)
as a means to provide further insight into the mechanism of
action of infigratinib and a rationale for low–dose selection
to support the clinical development of infigratinib in children
with ACH.
Materials and methods
Animals
The ACH Fgfr3Y367C/+ mouse model has been described
previously.20,26 All mice were on a C57BL/6 background.
Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice were generated by crossing CMV-Cre mice
(C57BL/6J) with mice exhibiting germline transmission of
the Y367C mutation corresponding to the human Y373C
mutation. All experimental procedures and protocols were
approved by the French National Animal Care and Use Com-
mittee (APAFIS#24826-2018080216094268 v5), in compli-
ance with the EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animals.
Animal treatment
Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice were tattooed and assigned to treatment
groups (wild–type mice, Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice treated with vehi-
cle, Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice treated with infigratinib) within litters
at 1 day old. Treatment started 1 day after birth. Infigratinib
was administered subcutaneously once daily at 0.2 mg/kg
(n = 10) or 0.5 mg/kg (n = 10), or once every 3 days at 1 mg/kg
per dose (intermittent dosing) (n = 13). As an internal control,
vehicle was administered to wild–type (n = 6) or Fgfr3Y367C/+
mice (n = 11). The vehicle was HCl 3.5 mM containing 5%
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Infigratinib phosphate (BGJ398-
AZA), the phosphate salt of Infigratinib, was formulated in
DMSO for subcutaneous administration, rather than oral
gavage, because of the size of the mice. It is important to note
that Infigratinib is dosed orally in the clinical studies, hence,
uses a different formulation. Clinical observations of potential
treatment side effects were performed twice a week (hindlimb
movement, posture, tail, paws). Detailed clinical observations
were made at the time of scoring. Animals were sacrificed
1 day after the last day of dosing (day 15).

Morphometric analyses
Micro–computed tomography imaging of the skull was per-
formed using a Skyscan 1172 (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium).
Samples were fixed in 70% ethanol and scanned with settings
of 80 kV, 100 μA, 19.98 μm pixel resolution, and 0.5◦rota-
tion steps without filter. Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruc-
tions were generated using NRecon software (Skyscan). The
3D surface mesh for each specimen was obtained using OsiriX
software and subsequently used for landmark positioning.
Pharmacokinetics analyses
Pharmacokinetics of Infigratinib were determined in male
C57BL/6 mice following once daily subcutaneous administra-
tion of 0.5, 1, and 2 mg/kg Infigratinib for 10 consecutive days
of dosing. Blood samples (0.5 ml) were collected K2EDTA
tubes via terminal cardiac puncture at seven different time-
points (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24 h) post last dose. Total of
three mice were used for each timepoint in all dose groups.
The blood tubes were placed in chill bucket, centrifuged

Austin, TX). Plasma samples were analyzed for Infigratinib
using validated LC–MS–MS assay (Liquid Chromatography–
Mass Spectrometry). Data were stored in Watson laboratory
information management system (Version 7.5; Thermo Fisher
Scientific). Plasma concentration–time data for Infigratinib

Results
Daily infigratinib doses increase skeletal growth in a mouse
model mimicking ACH.
To investigate the efficacy of low–dose infigratinib in revers-
ing bone growth defects, Fgfr3Y367C/+ pup mice received sub-
cutaneously administered infigratinib (0.2 or 0.5 mg/kg) or
vehicle, once daily for 15 days. During the study, infigratinib
treatment led to a progressive dose–dependent increase in
naso–anal length (Figure 1A) and weight (Figure 1B) of the
Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice compared with the vehicle–treated con-
trol group (Table S1). X–ray imaging showed the beneficial
effect of treatment on skeleton growth in Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice
treated with either infigratinib 0.2 or 0.5 mg/kg versus control
(Figure 1C). At the end of the 15–day treatment period, mea-
surements of selected appendicular bones were taken. Com-
pared with vehicle–treated mice, infigratinib–treated animals
showed a statistically significant dose–dependent increase in
the length of lower limbs (femur and tibia; Figure 1D) and
upper limbs (humerus and ulna; Figure 1E). For example, as
shown in Table 1, the percentage increase in femur length
after 15 days of infigratinib 0.2 and 0.5 mg/kg was 4.89%
and 10.35%, respectively. For the tibia, humerus and ulna,
statistically significant increases were observed in animals in
the 0.5 mg/kg treatment group (Table 1).
Intermittent inﬁgratinib treatment also increases
skeletal growth
To test whether an alternative dosing regimen, such as
intermittent administration, could generate similar beneficial
results as the daily regimen, we treated Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice
with infigratinib 1 mg/kg every 3 days, corresponding to
five injections over 15 days. We observed progressive and
significant increases in naso–anal length (Figure 2A) and
weight (Figure 2B) of these Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice over the course
of the study (Table 1). At the end of the 15–day treatment
period, naso–anal and lumbar vertebral lengths and animal
weights showed significant increases compared with the
control group (Figure 2C). The gain of growth in infigratinib–
versus vehicle–treated mice was statistically significantly
different for all long bones measured (femur 8.7%, P < .001;
tibia 6.4%, P < .001; humerus 5.0%, P < .001; ulna 5.6%,
P < .001; L4-L6 6.4%, P < .001 Table 1; Figure 2D and E).
Low–dose inﬁgratinib improved the endochondral
ossiﬁcation process
To characterize the in vivo mode of action of infigratinib,
we analyzed distal and proximal growth plates of the femur
in Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice. We observed that the size of the
femoral distal epiphysis was increased in both daily (0.2
and 0.5 mg/kg) and intermittent dosing (1 mg/kg every
3 days) infigratinib–treated groups, and that the treatment
promoted formation of a secondary ossification center (SOC;
Figure 3A). Quantification of the secondary ossification area
of the epiphysis showed a statistically significant increase
in size of the center area compared with vehicle–treated
control mice for all treatment groups (Figure 3B), and the
modification correlated with Col X, a marker of late–stage
hypertrophic chondrocyte differentiation (Figure 3A).
To investigate the effect of infigratinib on chondrocyte
differentiation, we analyzed changes in cell morphology and
expression of Col X in the hypertrophic zone (HY) of the
growth plate. An increase in the size of hypertrophic cells
was visible in the infigratinib–treated Fgfr3Y367C/+ growth
plate compared with the vehicle–treated plate (Figure 3C). A
larger Col X–positive area in the proximal femur was also
noticeable as result of infigratinib treatment (0.5 and 1 mg/kg
every 3 days). This modification of the HY of the growth plate
is consistent with elongation of long bones in infigratinib–
treated animals (Figure 3D). These results indicate that the
endochondral ossification process was enhanced by treatment
with infigratinib.
Anomalies in IVDs and lumbar vertebrae were
ameliorated by low–dose inﬁgratinib
As the spines of many individuals with ACH show some
degree of lumbar lordosis, we then wished to determine if
infigratinib treatment also modified the axial skeleton, in
particular the vertebrae and IVDs. The length of the L4-L6
segment increased by 18.4% in Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice treated with
infigratinib 0.5 mg/kg daily, 4.5% with 0.2 mg/kg daily, and
6.4% with the intermittent injection (1 mg/kg every 3 days)
versus vehicle–treated animals (Table 1).
To assess the extent of modification of the lumbar vertebrae
and IVD caused by different treatments, histological and
immunohistological analyses were conducted. Anomalies in
the shape and size of the IVD, such as the reduced size of the
ossified part of the vertebrae, were visible with HES staining
(Figure 3E). Interestingly, treatment with infigratinib 0.5 and
1 mg/kg increased the IVD area (Figure 3F). Immunological
analysis of the fibrocartilage tissues, for example, annulus
fibrosus, showed a statistically significant increase in the Col
I–positive outer annulus fibrosus area upon treatment with
infigratinib 0.5 mg/kg (+ 63.89%) and 0.2 mg/kg (+ 28.61%;
Figure 3G and H). Finally, we studied the cartilage endplate
organization of the vertebrae using Col X immunolabeling
(Figure S1A). Higher magnification of the cartilage endplate
showed the presence of Col X–positive chondrocytes (arrows)
in Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice treated with infigratinib 0.5 mg/kg that

Low–dose inﬁgratinib treatment alter FGFR3 and
Erk1/2 phosphorylation
Komla-Ebri et al.19 have described a reduction in FGFR3
phosphorylation in transiently transfected human control
chondrocyte lines and HEK293-Vnr cell lines. Interestingly,
using immunostaining in HY of the cartilage of the femur, we
assessed FGFR phosphorylation in FGFR3 positive cells. As
expected, FGFR3 expression in Fgfr3+/+ is restricted to the
proliferative zone and seems to slightly phosphorylated. As
previously reported,19 we observed an elevated expression
of FGFR3 and phosphorylated FGFR3 in the HY of the
cartilage in untreated Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice, interestingly a
reduction in FGFR3 and phosphorylated FGFR3 is observed
in Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice treated with infigratinib (Figure S2A).
To confirm the reduced activity of FGFR3, we analyzed the
downstream MAPK pathway. We performed immunolabeling
of phospho-ERK1/2 on growth plate cartilage from infigra-
tinib–treated and untreated Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice. Quantification
of the phosphorylated ERK1/2–positive chondrocyte showed
a statistically significant reduction in phosphorylated Erk1/2
proteins in mice treated with intermittent administration
of infigratinib compared with untreated Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice
(Figure S2B). All these data seem to show that treatment
with infigratinib reduces the FGFR3 protein turnover and the
phosphorylation of FGFRs and ERK1/2. Finally, we evaluated
the benefit of infigratinib treatment on survival. FgfrY367C/+
mice treated with infigratinib had better survival over the
course of the 15–day study compared with vehicle–treated
mice (P < .001 at all doses vs vehicle; Figure 5D).
Low–dose inﬁgratinib treatment did not alter
serum FGF23 and phosphorus levels
Hyperphosphatemia and accompanying FGF23 elevation
have been observed in preclinical and clinical studies when
FGFR inhibitors were given at or near their maximum
tolerated doses,28,29 effects that are believed to result from
FGFR1 inhibition. To assess whether systemic FGF23 and
phosphate levels were elevated in Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice following
the full course of infigratinib treatment, serum was collected
at the end of the study and subjected to ELISA (FGF23)
and colorimetric (phosphate) tests. No statistically significant
differences in FGF23 levels were observed in infigratinib–
treated mice versus controls (P > .05; Figure S3A). Similarly,
no statistically significant differences in phosphate levels
were observed in infigratinib–treated Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice

Discussion
It is well known that FGFR3 is a negative regulator of long
bone development, that it is highly expressed in growth
plate chondrocytes, and that it plays a critical role in
regulating chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation.30
In ACH, a well–described p.Gly380Arg substitution in the
transmembrane domain of FGFR310 leads to increased
activation of downstream signaling, including the MAPK,
PI3K/AKT, PLCγ , and STAT pathways,2 causing disordered
bone growth and disproportionately short stature (Figure S4).

FGFR3 gain–of–function mutations increase the activity of
the receptor and the level of tyrosine phosphorylation.
To counteract this abnormal activity, a valuable strategy is
to target the overactive FGFR3 receptor. Inhibition of FGFR3
tyrosine kinase activity by the small–molecule tyrosine kinase
inhibitor infigratinib at a dose of 2 mg/kg/d has been previ-
ously shown.19 We have now performed preclinical studies
using very low doses of infigratinib and an intermittent dosing
regimen to provide further preclinical proof of concept and
rationale for dose selection to support the clinical develop-
ment of infigratinib in children with ACH. We hypothesized
that rescue of the ACH phenotype because of direct inhibition
of FGFR3 by infigratinib would be robust, given the impact
of infigratinib on FGFR3 and Erk1/2 phosphorylation.19 Our
results support this hypothesis and show that direct inhibition
of ACH–related FGFR3 overactivity by low–dose infigra-
tinib appears to ameliorate many of the phenotypic charac-
teristics associated with the condition. The present in vivo
data demonstrate that low and intermittent doses of infi-
gratinib promote growth in this ACH mouse model in a
dose–dependent manner. Treatment of FgfrY367C/+ mice with
low–dose infigratinib over 15 days enhanced endochondral
ossification and membranous ossification processes. It is note-
worthy to mention that mouse pharmacokinetics data support
the efficacy of infigratinib to promote growth at low doses.
These data expand on previous work in Fgfr3Y367C/+ mice,
which showed that daily treatment with infigratinib 2 mg/kg
significantly increased growth in long bones, vertebrae, IVD,
craniofacial morphology, and foramen magnum.19 Thorough
analyses identified beneficial changes in several clinical fea-
tures of ACH, such as the SOC delay, IVD anomalies, and the
reduced size of the foramen magnum and skull fontanelles.
These results confirm the high specificity of infigratinib for
improving the key features of ACH.
These data suggest that inhibition of FGFR3 overactivity
is critical to phenotype rescue in ACH. Previously, it was
described that phosphorous levels, driven by increases in
FGF23 via FGFR1 inhibition, could be a side effect of high–
dose infigratinib therapy.31,32 Here, in the mouse model,
infigratinib was well tolerated, as demonstrated by stable
FGF23 and phosphorus levels, as well as by better survival
in infigratinib– versus vehicle–treated mice. Based on pre-
liminary preclinical and clinical data, we hypothesized that
changes in FGF23 and phosphorus levels would not be seen
with low–dose infigratinib. Results from the doses of infi-
gratinib examined in these experiments showed that FGF23
and phosphate levels did not differ from levels in untreated
wild–type mice, demonstrating that low–dose infigratinib can
inhibit dysfunctional FGFR3 signaling in an ACH mouse
model while having no measurable impact on phosphate
homeostasis. This proof–of–concept investigation studying
most of the clinical hallmarks of ACH (long bones, foramen
magnum, IVD) suggests that long–term, low–dose treatment
may be an effective strategy.
Many other therapeutic approaches for the treatment of
ACH have been developed and evaluated in preclinical studies.
These include targeting the FGF ligand, for example, the
soluble FGFR3 decoy Recifercept and the anti-FGF2 aptamer
RBM-007 were generated to prevent FGF fixation, a therapeu-
tic approach that relies on the idea that the ACH mutation
is primarily modulated by FGF binding.33,34 Results of an
interim analysis of soluble FGFR3 decoy data from a phase
II study have indicated, however, that the study would not
reach its primary endpoint and the clinical development of
Recifercept has been discontinued.35 At present, only vosori-
tide (VOXZOGO), a daily injectable type C natriuretic pep-
tide analog that blocks downstream signaling pathways, is
approved in Europe36 and the United States, having received
an accelerated approval by the US FDA.37.
In the present study, we evaluated a different therapeutic
approach. Infigratinib is the only agent in clinical development
for ACH that directly targets FGFR3; it is also the only
orally administered medicine in clinical development for this
indication. The oral administration route is preferred over
various other modes of drug delivery, such as the subcuta-
neous injection route used for the administration of vosoritide
and TransCon CNP. Recently announced results of a phase 2
study of infigratinib in children with ACH demonstrated a
