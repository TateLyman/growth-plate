A B S T R A C T  
Objective: Hypoparathyroidism has heterogeneous genetic and acquired etiologies with a broad spectrum of 
severity. Herein we describe the clinical outcomes of the largest cohort of hypoparathyroid patients reported to 
date, who were followed over 27-years. 
Design: Pooled analysis of current and past studies describing the differential responses to PTH 1–34 injections vs 
conventional therapy among the varied hypoPT etiologies. 
Methods: 192 participants (ages 2–74 years) with hypoparathyroidism who received either calcitriol and calcium 
or PTH 1–34 by subcutaneous injection. 
Results: Among the 4 main etiologic categories of hypoparathyroidism (autoimmune polyglandular failure type 1, 
activating mutation of the calcium receptor, surgical, and idiopathic hypoparathyroidism), we reveal significant 
differences in PTH 1–34 dose requirements, prevalence of nephrocalcinosis, biomarkers of mineral homeostasis, 
and pharmacodynamic profiles. Serum 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 increased significantly (P < 0.001) and 25- 
hydroxyvitamin D levels decreased during PTH 1–34 injections compared to calcitriol therapy (P < 0.01). 
Post-surgical patients achieved consistently lower urine calcium excretion over long-term PTH 1–34 therapy 
compared to conventional therapy (p < 0.001), but this was not achieved in the other etiologies. At study entry, 
patients had a high prevalence of renal insufficiency and nephrocalcinosis which were directly related to the 
duration of hypoparathyroidism (P < 0.03). Renal function remained stable during participation in our studies 
for both PTH 1–34 and conventional therapies. 
Conclusions: We conclude that the effects and dose-response of PTH 1–34 treatment differ according to the eti­
ology of hypoparathyroidism. Postsurgical hypoPT maintained mean serum calcium levels in the mid- to low- 
normal range while concurrently maintaining normal mean urine calcium during long-term twice-daily PTH 
1–34 therapy.   
1. Introduction 
Hypoparathyroidism (hypoPT) is a rare hormonal insufficiency dis­
order characterized by inappropriately low or undetectable circulating 
concentrations of parathyroid hormone (PTH) and consequent chronic 
hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, and hypomagnesemia. Symptoms 
include neuromuscular irritability, tetany, muscle cramps, lar­
yngospasm, and seizures. The condition is characterized by decreased 
PTH-dependent calcium reabsorption from the renal tubule, decreased 
PTH-mediated phosphaturia, and decreased PTH-stimulated renal 1,α- 
hydroxylase activity causing reduced endogenous 1,25-dihydroxyvita­
min D3 production and reduced dietary calcium absorption. Hypopara­
thyroidism remains a hormonal insufficiency state for which 
replacement hormone therapy remains a largely unmet clinical need [1]. 
Adult-onset hypoPT is usually caused by damage or removal of the 
parathyroid glands during surgery or following radiation treatment. 
This represents the most common cause of hypoPT, therefore most 
studies in the literature focus on adults with post-surgical hypoPT. In

secretion of PTH, profound hypocalcemia, and excessive urinary cal­

hypoPT include 22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge) syndrome with para­

congenital defects in parathyroid gland development or PTH secretion 
[2]. Genetic forms of hypoPT usually present early in childhood and 
exhibit greater management challenges than post-surgical hypoPT in 
adults. 
In 1992, we initiated the first controlled studies of PTH 1–34 
replacement therapy ([8,15]; Table 1). These studies followed prior at­
tempts at replacement therapy, first in 1928, by Albright and Ellsworth 
who administered bovine PTH in an adolescent with post-surgical 
hypoPT and achieved a marked calcemic and phosphaturic response 
with a reduction in symptoms [16]. Years later, in 1990, the successful 
short-term use (2–8 months) of replacement with synthetic human PTH 
1–38 in two German APS-1/APECED adolescents paved the way for our 
studies [17]. 
Our studies began with a randomized crossover study of the 
pharmacodynamic responses to a single daily PTH 1–34 injection 
compared to conventional therapy with calcitriol and calcium [8]. This 
was followed by controlled studies comparing twice-daily to once-daily 
subcutaneous PTH 1–34 injections and, subsequently, compared to 
conventional therapy [9–12]. These studies demonstrated classic phys­
iologic responses to PTH 1–34 in the kidney and bone. We noted, 
however, sub-optimal responses in some patients with congenital 
hypoPT whose urine calcium levels remained intermittently elevated 
during PTH 1–34 injection therapy. The use of the insulin pump to 
deliver PTH 1–34 proved the most physiologic approach to therapy in 
both adults with acquired disease and children with genetic hypoPT 
[13,14,18,19]. 
The decades-long search for a replacement therapy for treatment of 
hypoPT has been fueled by evidence that conventional therapy with the 
hormonally active form of vitamin D, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 (cal­
citriol) and calcium supplements leads to adverse effects on the kidney 
and diminished quality of life [20–26]. Conventional therapy raises 
serum calcium through its direct effects on the intestinal absorption of 
calcium but, without the PTH-dependent calcium reabsorption by the 
kidney, elevated urine calcium levels may cause renal calcifications and 
impair renal function. Furthermore, combined hypoPT and malabsorp­
tion in APS-1/APECED presents a special challenge for therapy with 
calcitriol and supplemental calcium, which depends on gastrointestinal 
absorption to maintain normal serum calcium [3]. Prior publications 
have reported increased risk of renal complications in conventionally 
treated hypoPT [27–30]. Mitchell and colleagues found an increased 
risk of chronic kidney disease (40%) in a cohort of mostly adult post­
surgical hypoPT treated with conventional therapy. Underbjerg's retro­
spective review of adults with post-surgical hypoPT in Denmark over a 
14-year period reported a 5-fold increased risk of renal complications 
compared to age-matched controls. The same group reported an increase 
in renal complications in nonsurgical hypoPT patients compared with 
population-based controls in Denmark [29,30]. 
Here we describe the clinical characteristics and management of the 
largest cohort of hypoparathyroid patients reported to date, followed at 
a single center over a 27-year period. This pooled analysis across past 
and ongoing studies enabled us to compare response to therapy ac­
cording to hypoPT etiology, which has been limited in previous studies 
because of the small numbers within diagnostic sub-groups. Our results 
provide a broad overview of the main hypoPT etiology groups and their 
unique responses to both conventional and PTH 1–34 therapy. 
2. Methods 
We pooled data from ten studies (Table 1) which enabled a com­
parison of conventional therapy (calcitriol and calcium) with synthetic 
human PTH 1–34 and an evaluation of the responses to PTH 1–34 ac­
cording to diagnostic etiology (APS-1/APECED, CaR, post-surgical, 
idiopathic/familial). All studies were approved by the NIH IRB (#1–9 
at NICHD and #10 at NIAID). The diagnosis of hypoPT was confirmed in 
all patients by a subnormal or undetectable intact PTH level during 
hypocalcemia. 
Study participants in long-term studies were evaluated in the NIH 
Clinical Center outpatient clinic every 6 months (Table 1, Studies 
3,5,8,9). During these semi-annual visits, we collected urine and blood 
samples over a 3-day period to inform dose adjustments and other 
changes to treatment regimens. All studies began with a 1–2-week 
inpatient evaluation. Blood and urine minerals were measured daily, or 
more frequently, as appropriate. 
Some patients entered into multiple studies and were switched be­
tween calcitriol and PTH therapy with no titration period between the 
two treatment regimens. Fourteen patients (5 children) had been treated 
with PTH 1–34 prior to entry into long-term treatment with calcitriol 
and calcium (studies 1–3, Table 1). A subset of patients received 
exclusively PTH 1–34 or conventional therapy. In the final pooled 
analysis, the exclusively conventional treatment group did not include

Calcitriol and calcium supplements were not used as an adjunct to 
PTH 1–34 therapy, except as short-term treatment for acute symptom­

Studies #8, 9 (Table 1) were natural history studies of patients with 
severe refractory hypoPT who received twice-daily PTH 1–34, and study 
#10 is an ongoing multidisciplinary prospective natural history study of 
APS-1/APECED at the NIAID which includes, for most patients, multiple 
inpatient evaluations at various time intervals. 
For all PTH 1–34 treatment studies (# 1–9), synthetic human N- 
terminal fragment of PTH (PTH 1–34), was formulated (1992–2014) at 
the NIH pharmacy. Patients used an insulin syringe to inject PTH in the 
abdomen, upper leg, or arms. PTH 1–34 was vialed in two strengths, 50 
and 200 μg/mL, in a 5% mannitol solution without preservative [8]. The 
two vial strengths permitted dose changes in increments of 0.5 μg or 2 μg 
for the 50 and 200 μg vials, respectively. The intended level for serum 
calcium (1.9 mmol/L) was just below the normal range, and for urine 
calcium within the normal range—for both treatments and for all etio­
logic groups. The initiation of PTH 1–34 therapy was achieved during an 
inpatient admission lasting one week. For Studies 1–9, mean ± SD dose 
adjustments were 23 ± 18.4% (median:16.7%, range: 3.5–100%) up or 
down, were open label, and were made by a single investigator (KKW) 
throughout the time patients were participating in studies at NIH. For 
Study #10, patients received conventional therapy with few exceptions, 
and the outpatient management of hypocalcemia was returned to their 
local endocrinologists after each NIH evaluation. 
We performed a pooled analysis of pharmacodynamic responses to 
twice-daily PTH 1–34 injections (Table 1, studies 2,4,6,7) over a 24-h 
period in 58 hypoparathyroid patients. We implemented the same 
study procedures in each study including a standardized calcium diet of 
800–1000 mg during the testing period and measured serum minerals 
every 2-h and urine mineral excretion every 4-h. The pharmacodynamic 
studies were performed after three phases of PTH 1–34 dose titration– 
during an initial 3-day inpatient evaluation, 3 months of outpatient 
monitoring, and a second 3-day inpatient evaluation, just prior to the 24 
h test. All patients were biochemically stable at the time of pharmaco­
dynamic study. 
3. Statistical analysis 
24-h urine excretion measures were corrected in all patients to a 
body surface area of 1.73 m2. Data were mostly presented as mean ± SD. 
For patients followed long-term (>6 months), overall mean (SD) values 
of blood and urine analytes from baseline to the patient's last observa­
tion up to 3 years (PTH vs calcitriol, N = 89 and N = 48, respectively) 
were determined by treatment group and compared by a 2-sample t-test 
for all patients within each treatment group and separately by etiology. 
The blood and urine calcium values were further stratified by semi- 
annual timepoint which represent the average of multiple values ob­
tained during the 3 months before and after the timepoint. We omitted 
from the current analyses all biochemical data obtained during the short 
periods when patients were receiving PTH through an insulin pump 
[13,14]. 
In 58 patients, we performed a pooled analysis of the 24-h phar­
macodynamic responses to twice-daily PTH 1–34 injections (Table 1, 
studies 2,4,6,7) to compare responses among the different hypoPT eti­
ologies. Mean ± SD age of these 58 patients was 29 ± 18.5 years (32 
adults, ages 20–69 y; 26 children, ages 4–18 y) and the numbers of 
patients in each etiology group were: 13 APS-1, 16 CaR, 22 post-surgical, 
and 7 idiopathic/familial. We analyzed the results by harmonic regres­
sion modeling with random effects to compare the circadian response 
pattern among etiologies. This model assumes that the effect of cova­
riates and random effects may alter the amplitude and phase shift but 
not the shape of the circadian pattern [31]. Additionally, we compared 
the mean (SD) blood and urine mineral levels for each etiology to all 
other etiologies by a two-sample t-test. 
4. Results 
4.1. Subjects 
192 patients (ages 2–74 years; 123 female; 97 children, ages 2–18 y) 
with hypoPT (90 APS-1/APECED, 49 post-surgical, 21 CaR, 24 Idio­
pathic/familial, 8 DiGeorge) were recruited over 27-years (1992–2018) 
representing 612 patient-years (median 1.2; interquartile range 
IQR:0.01–4.04 years; range: 0.003–25.7 years; Table 2). The mean ± SD 
patient age at initial baseline evaluation was 26 ± 18 years. 
The median duration of hypoPT at study baseline, was 7.0 years (IQR 
3.14–14.6 years; range 0.04–56 years). Median age at diagnosis of 
hypoPT was 7.0 years (range: 0.02–74 years). HypoPT associated with 
CaR was diagnosed between 6 days-40 years with 11 patients diagnosed 
before age 1 year. Seizures were the presenting symptom in 6 CaR pa­
tients (ages 14 days −7 year). APS-1/APECED was diagnosed between 
ages 1–29 years with seizures as the presenting symptom in 30%, 
occurring between ages 1–12 years. Post-surgical hypoPT patients were 
diagnosed between ages 7–74 years, with no patient presenting with 
seizures. Patients with hypoPT were referred to the NIH by their primary 
care physicians to participate in treatment or natural history studies or 
to seek guidance on the management of their disease. Most patients were 
from the US with 12 children from Canada, Sweden, Great Britain, 
Ukraine, Iceland, Brazil, and Uruguay. The idiopathic/familial group 
included 7 patients with affected family members. These cases of fa­
milial hypoPT either had no genetic testing or were not tested for genetic 
causes of hypoPT that were discovered after their evaluation [32–35]. 
When comparing the different etiologies of hypoPT, we combined the 
mixed group of idiopathic/familial (N = 24) etiologies with a group of 8 
patients with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, 5 of whom from one family 
had a heterozygous TBX1 mutation [34]. We refer to this as the idio­
pathic/familial group. 
We followed patients receiving PTH 1–34 or calcitriol and calcium 
(referred to as “calcitriol” in Results) for up to 19 and 26 years, 
respectively. Some patients (N = 25) participated in more than one 
treatment protocol and received both PTH 1–34 and calcitriol therapies 
for extended periods. Eighty-seven patients (56 APS-1/APECED, 14 
post-surgical, and 17 idiopathic/familial) received calcitriol therapy 
exclusively during the study for a mean of 1.8 ± 4.9 (range: 0.002–25.7) 
years. Seventy-four patients (25 APS-1/APECED, 14 CaR, 21 post- 
surgical, 14 idiopathic/familial) received PTH 1–34 injections exclu­
sively for a mean of 4.3 ± 4.6 (range: 0.35–19.4) years. 
4.2. Medication doses 
The PTH dose was adjusted in increments and decrements of 
approximately 20% to maintain urine calcium in the normal range and 
serum calcium in the low-normal or just below the normal range. The 
size and frequency of dose adjustments depended on clinical status and 
biochemical profile. The normalization of urine calcium was difficult to 
attain during both treatment regimens—PTH 1–34 or calcitriol—in pa­
tients with CaR, because of their underlying molecular defect, and in 
patients with APS-1/APECED, due to concurrent steroid treatment and 
other co-morbidities, especially intestinal malabsorption. 
Ninety-nine patients were treated with synthetic human PTH 1–34. 
The mean ± SD PTH 1–34 dose was 48 ± 34.9 μg/day (0.79 ± 0.52 μg/ 
kg/day; range 0.065–3.5 μg/kg/day. The median daily PTH 1–34 dose 
per kg body weight was 0.66 μg/kg/day. The average daily PTH 1–34 
dose (mcg/kg/day) required to maintain normal calcium homeostasis 
differed according to etiology (P < 0.006). Patients with APS-1/APECED 
and CaR (0.94 ± 0.64 and 0.99 ± 0.54 μg/kg/day, respectively; median 
0.9 μg/kg/day for both etiologies) required higher mean ± SD PTH 1–34 
doses compared to patients with post-surgical or idiopathic hypoPT

4.3. Mineral homeostasis 
We examined the distribution of serum and urine calcium levels 
among low, normal, and above normal (high) values according to 
treatment (PTH 1–34 vs calcitriol) and etiology for all 192 patients. 
Blood calcium levels were lower during PTH 1–34 compared to con­
ventional (calcitriol) therapy and this was significant for all etiologies 
(P < 0.02). High blood calcium levels were rare, accounting for <5% of 
all calcium levels on either therapy. APS-1/APECED patients had the 
highest number of elevated calcium levels (4.4%) during both conven­
tional and PTH 1–34 therapies.

4.6. Renal function 
182 patients had renal creatinine clearance measures at their initial 
evaluation, while receiving conventional therapy. Ninety-three of those 
patients (51%) had some degree of renal insufficiency (stages 2–5) 
whereas 15% had moderate to severe kidney disease (stages 3–5). Three 
adult patients (2 CaR;1 post-surgical;creatinine clearance range:19-25 
mL/min/1.73 m2) had severe chronic kidney disease and one patient, 
an 8 yo child with idiopathic hypoPT, had renal failure (creatinine 
clearance = 10.6 mL/min). 
Creatinine clearance correlated negatively with patient age at initial 
NIH evaluation (Pearson's correlation coefficient, −0.39; 95% confi­
dence interval: −0.42 to −0.35, p < 0.0001) and also correlated nega­
tively with duration of hypoPT at baseline when adjusting for age (P <
0.001). Mean ± SD creatinine clearances by etiology (per 1.73 m2 BSA) 
were: APS-1/APECED, 105 ± 34.2 mL/min (range 32.5–179 mL/min); 
CaR, 80 ± 33.2 mL/min (19–136.2 mL/min); post-surgical, 82 ± 24 mL/ 
min (20.7–170.1 mL/min); and idiopathic/familial, 96 ± 34.5 mL/min 
(10.6–141 mL/min). The APS-1/APECED patients had significantly 
higher mean corrected creatinine clearance values compared to post- 
surgical and CaR (both P < 0.001) and idiopathic hypoPT (P = 0.02). 
The CaR patients had significantly lower creatinine clearance compared 
to both APS-1/APECED and idiopathic groups, which 3 groups all had 
similar mean ages (P < 0.004), but no difference compared to the post- 
surgical hypoPT, whose mean age was 29 years greater than the CaR 
patients (P = 0.6). The decrease in renal function for the CaR group was 
likely related to the relatively longer duration of hypoPT and adverse 
effects of conventional therapy which were worsened by the renal effects 
of their molecular defect, whereas age is likely a factor in the reduced 
renal function in post-surgical patients (mean age: 47 years old at 
baseline, Table 2). 
Patients who received exclusively conventional vs exclusively PTH 
1–34 therapy during study participation had significantly greater 
creatinine clearance at initial NIH evaluation (107 ± 32 vs 94 ± 30 mL/ 
min/1.73 m2 BSA, respectively, P < 0.001). By linear mixed-effects 
model, no significant change in renal function was observed during 
1.8 ± 4.9 years (range 0.002–25.7 y) in the calcitriol and calcium only 
group (N = 87) or during 4.3 ± 4.6 years (range 0.35–19.4 y) in the 
PTH1–34 only group (N = 74). The total duration of hypoPT (duration at 
baseline plus protocol duration) was lower, but not to a statistically 
significant degree (P = 0.12), in the calcitriol group (median: 7.62 y; 
IQR:3.8–18; range: 0.33–55.7y) compared to the PTH group (median: 
12.7y, IQR 6–19; range: 1.4–60.5 y). 
The difference in baseline renal creatinine clearance should be 
interpreted in light of disproportionate number of CaR patients in the 
group who exclusively received PTH 1–34 versus conventional therapy 
(14 vs 0, respectively; see Subjects and Methods). Additionally, the PTH 
1–34 group included patients with deteriorating renal function who 
were referred to our studies specifically to receive PTH 1–34 therapy. 
This group included a family of four with CaR, all of whom had creati­
nine clearance values between 19 and 40 mL/min/1.73 m2 at time of 
referral. This group also included a young adult with APS-1/APECED 
who had received a renal transplant within the year prior to study 
entry and an 8-year-old boy with idiopathic hypoPT who received a 
renal transplant shortly after his initial NIH evaluation. Renal disease 
was generally due to the largely unavoidable iatrogenic effects of long- 
term conventional treatment of hypoPT. 
4.7. Renal calcifications 
The presence of baseline nephrocalcinosis was directly related to the 
duration of hypoPT (P < 0.03) and not related to patient's age (P =
0.16). The duration of hypoPT was significantly associated with neph­
rocalcinosis in APS-1/APECED (P = 0.026) and idiopathic/familial (P <
0.04) but not in the CaR or surgical patients. The mean duration of 
hypoPT at baseline in patients with and without nephrocalcinosis was 
12.8 ± 10.7 and 8.77 ± 11.1 years, respectively. Seventy-seven of the 
145 patients (53% [42 APS-1/APECED, 21 CaR, 7 post-surgical, and 7 
idiopathic/familial], Table 2) who had renal imaging at baseline had 
nephrocalcinosis. HypoPT patients without nephrocalcinosis at baseline 
renal imaging included 28 APS-1/APECED, 28 post-surgical, and 12 
idiopathic/familial. All patients with CaR had baseline nephrocalci­
nosis. There was no baseline difference between the creatinine clear­
ance/BSA of the 77 patients with and the 68 patients without 
nephrocalcinosis (92 ± 39 vs 102 ± 21 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively, 
P = 0.57). 
4.8. Brain calcifications 
Twenty-nine of the 55 patients (53% [17 APS-1/APECED, 8 CaR, 4 
idiopathic/familial]) who had brain imaging (computed tomography 
[CT]) had calcifications which were located primarily in the basal 
ganglia but also found in the frontal and parietal lobes [15]. HypoPT 
patients without brain calcifications included 17 APS-1/APECED, 2 CaR, 
6 post-surgical, and 1 idiopathic/familial. None of the patients with 
post-surgical hypoPT and 80% of the CaR patients who had brain im­
aging had calcifications (Table 2). All patients were receiving calcitriol 
and calcium therapy at the time of the brain CT.

4.9. Calcium-phosphorus product 
Mean ± SD calcium × phosphorus product (Ca × P) was 40.1 ± 9.0 
mg2/dL2 for the combined cohort of both PTH 1–34- and calcitriol- 
treated patients, and was slightly but significantly lower during PTH 
1–34 vs calcitriol therapy (39.8 ± 8.8 vs 41.2 ± 9.3 mg2/dL2, respec­
tively, P < 0.001). The Ca × P values in each patient correlated signif­
icantly but weakly with the corresponding urine calcium excretion 
(Pearson's correlation coefficient, 0.076 [95% CI: 0.045–0.107], Sup­
plementary Table 2). Thus, high Ca × P product was not a strong pre­
dictor of high urine calcium levels and therefore not a good marker for 
renal toxicity risk. The CaR group, for example, carries a well- 
established greater risk for nephrocalcinosis and kidney damage. This 
group, however, had a majority (74%) of Ca × P values <45 mg2/dL2 
(<3.6 mmol2/L2), yet the corresponding urine calcium excretion values 
were above normal (>7.5 mmol/24 h) in nearly half of those values 
(46%). 
4.10. Transition to post-PTH 1–34 conventional therapy 
When PTH 1–34 treatment ended per protocol, patients often 
resumed their usual dose of calcitriol as received at study baseline. To 
investigate calcium homeostasis directly after switching from PTH 1–34 
to calcitriol therapy we compared the biochemical responses to calcitriol 
at two timepoints in 21 patients who had received PTH 1–34 for 8 
months to 4.5 years: [1] at study baseline while receiving calcitriol and 
calcium supplements (Baseline) and [2] up to two months after 
resuming calcitriol and calcium after PTH 1–34 treatment (Post-PTH 
VitD). Comparing baseline vs Post-PTH VitD, there were no differences 
in the Ca × P product (44.6 ± 10.2 vs 42.9 ± 11.5 mg2/dL2, respectively, 
P = 0.34) or the dose of calcitriol (0.60 ± 0.32 vs 0.69 ± 0.223 μg/day, 
respectively, P = 0.17). The calcium supplementation dosage, however, 
was significantly lower during the Post-PTH VitD period (1697 ± 1175 
vs 1071 ± 389 mg/day, respectively, P < 0.02). Mean ± SD serum and 
24-h urine calcium were also significantly lower at the Post-PTH VitD 
timepoint (2.12 ± 0.19 mmol/L vs 1.92 ± 0.14 mmol/L; 7.75 ± 4.05 vs 
5.27 ± 2.94 mmol/24 h/1.73 m2, respectively, P < 0.02 for both). 
The differences in blood and urine calcium levels should be inter­
preted in light of our treatment goals for a slightly subnormal serum Ca 
of 1.9 mmol/L, whereas the primary care physicians' treatments that 
determined calcium levels at baseline often aimed at maintaining serum 
calcium in the mid-normal range. Additionally, the initial urine calcium 
measured during the post-PTH VitD timepoint may reflect lingering 
long-term PTH 1–34 effects. These data indicate that abrupt change from 
PTH 1–34 to calcitriol is safe with careful monitoring. 
5. Discussion 
HypoPT has heterogeneous genetic and acquired etiologies. Most 
clinical studies to date, however, include homogeneous study pop­
ulations comprised mainly of adult women with post-surgical hypoPT. 
To our knowledge, there are no published studies to date describing the 
differential responses to PTH 1–34 injections among the varied hypoPT 
etiologies. The current study revealed significant differences according 
to hypoPT etiology in PTH 1–34 dose requirements, prevalence of 
nephrocalcinosis, biomarkers of mineral homeostasis, and PTH 1–34 
pharmacodynamic profiles. Understanding such differences is an 
important step toward effective individualized management of hypoPT. 
Previous pharmacodynamic studies demonstrated different responses 
according to frequency of PTH 1–34 injections [9] and by twice-daily 
injections versus an insulin pump [13]. We show here twice-daily PTH 
1–34 injections produced overall normal serum and 24-h urine mineral 
levels, with significantly different circadian patterns according to 
hypoPT etiology. The fluctuations in serum and urine minerals produced 
by a PTH 1–34 injection are far greater for CaR patients versus post- 
surgical patients in whom the fluctuations may be minimal and there­
fore associated with less risk of long-term adverse renal consequences. 
These observations help explain the greater vigilance and number of 
dose adjustments that may be needed in achieving an optimal balance 
between hypocalcemia and hypercalciuria in the CaR patients. We have 
also shown that magnesium homeostasis is disordered to a differing 
extent among the different etiologies, evident in the high urine mag­
nesium excretion in APS-1/APECED and CaR patients, which was not 
corrected with PTH 1–34 injections. 
Half of our cohort had renal insufficiency at baseline evaluation. We 
attributed this largely to the adverse effects of conventional therapy on 
the kidney. Preservation of normal renal function and avoidance of 
extraosseous calcifications, especially in kidney, are critical goals in 
managing hypoPT. Our analyses showed that, at initial evaluation, 
duration of hypoPT correlated positively with the presence of neph­
rocalcinosis and negatively with renal function. Furthermore, we did not 
observe diminishing renal function over the duration of our studies for 
either PTH 1–34 or conventional therapies. Tay et al. recently reported 
similar stability in renal function in their 8-year observational study of 
PTH 1–84 therapy in adults with hypoPT [36]. However, we observed at 
baseline a considerably higher rate of nephrocalcinosis (53%) in our 
diverse patient population compared to previous studies of predomi­
nantly post-surgical cases, which reported a 20–30% prevalence of 
nephrocalcinosis [28–30]. Similar to prior reports, 20% of our post- 
surgical group had nephrocalcinosis, with much higher rates of renal 
calcifications in the non-surgical patients with CaR (100%) and APS-1/ 
APECED (60%). 
Although monitoring of Ca × P product has been recommended to 
reduce the risk of calcifications [2,28,37], we found a quite weak cor­
relation between this product and concurrent urine calcium excretion 
values. Therefore, we conclude that the Ca × P product does not reliably 
predict hypercalciuria, a precursor to renal damage, and suggests that 
this tool seems unlikely to be a useful surrogate marker for risk of kidney 
damage. Additionally, mean Ca × P product was similar comparing 
patients who had brain calcification to those who had normal brain 
imaging. Skepticism regarding the clinical usefulness of the Ca × P 
product emerged years ago in the nephrology literature [38,39], yet 
some physicians still use the Ca × P product as a tool for risk assessment 
and dose adjustment in hypoPT. 
Calcifications in the brain were evident in half the patients who had 
imaging at baseline. Up to 80% of the non-surgical hypoPT patients had 
brain calcifications compared with none of the six post-surgical patients 
who had brain imaging. Hyperphosphatemia, the most pervasive min­
eral disorder in treated and untreated hypoPT, is considered the un­
derlying abnormality leading to brain calcifications [28,37,40]. Our 
results, however, did not support this hypothesis as we did not find a 
difference in blood phosphorus levels comparing patients with and 
without brain calcifications. Our results also did not confirm the cal­
cium/phosphorus ratio or the Ca × P product helpful as risk markers for 
brain calcifications as proposed by Goswami et al. [41]. 
Additionally, we show that switching between calcitriol and PTH 
1–34 therapy can be done rapidly and without the need for prolonged 
high calcitriol doses after PTH 1–34 is discontinued [2,37].

5.1. Optimizing therapy 
When the current studies began in 1992, most of the genetic forms of 
hypoPT had not yet been identified. Management guidelines then, and 
still today, were focused on post-surgical hypoparathyroidism, with 
limited guidance for the different categories of genetic etiology. Physi­
cians caring for patients with hypoPT are lacking evidence-based man­
agement recommendations for the varied etiologies and the technology 
to self-monitor calcium in real time, which has proven so transformative 
in the treatment of diabetes. Additionally, children and adolescents with 
hypoPT, who are the most susceptible to the long-term risks associated 
with conventional therapy, are lacking FDA-approved hormonal 
replacement therapy. Thus, there remains much to be done to fulfill the 
goal of effective individualized therapy, with clinically relevant end­
points and target goals, for all patients with hypoPT. 
To reduce urine calcium excretion, off-label use of thiazides has been 
recommended [2,28,37,40] based upon an uncontrolled pilot study of 
six post-surgical hypoPT patients managed with sodium restriction, 
chlorthalidone, and calcium supplement [42]. More recent studies, 
however, have revealed higher levels of urine calcium excretion among 
thiazide-treated patients compared to patients not receiving thiazides 
[28]. Other potential harm from thiazides includes decreased magne­
sium reabsorption, increased urine magnesium excretion, and decreased 
serum magnesium. Increased magnesium excretion can enhance the risk 
of nephrocalcinosis, and worsened magnesium deficiency compounds 
the already destabilized mineral homeostasis in hypoPT patients [43]. 
Recombinant hPTH 1–34 (Forteo®), approved for use in osteoporosis 
in the elderly, has not been approved in children and its use in osteo­
porosis has until recently been limited to 2 years. These restrictions 
notwithstanding, Forteo is used worldwide as an off-label alternative to 
conventional therapy in patients with hypoPT who are refractory to 
conventional therapy [18,19,27,44]. Natpara (rhPTH 1–84), approved 
for treatment of hypoPT in 2015, was removed from the market in 2019 
to address a pen-injector defect. It had been approved as a single-daily 
dose along with conventional therapy for refractory hypoPT. Howev­
er, Natpara was not tested or approved for children or in patients with 
CaR [45]. 
Based on our findings, we conclude that otherwise healthy patients 
with postsurgical hypoPT can maintain serum calcium levels in the mid- 
to low-normal range while concurrently maintaining urine calcium in 
the normal range with twice daily PTH 1–34 injections. Patients with 
CaR, by contrast, must maintain blood calcium levels in the low-normal 
or below-normal range because their defect includes a tendency for 
hypercalciuria with a high risk of renal and brain calcifications. The 
pharmacodynamic profile of blood and urine minerals (Fig. 2) con­
firms—as we have previously concluded—that twice-daily PTH 1–34 
injection therapy may also produce undesirably wide fluctuations in 
blood and urine calcium in the CaR and APS-1/APECED cases, which 
would be predicted to place these patients at greater risk of renal cal­
cifications than that of post-surgical patients [15]. 
APS-1/APECED patients with Addison's disease and intestinal 
malabsorption have decreased intestinal calcium absorption and 
increased urinary calcium losses. Moreover, the disease components that 
comprise this syndrome emerge over years and sometimes decades with 
hypoPT typically presenting as the first endocrinopathy [3,46,47]. From 
limited observation, we have found that APS-1/APECED patients with 
isolated hypoPT present less challenge to simultaneously normalizing 
both urine and serum calcium than do the APS-1/APECED patients with 
multiple endocrinopathies and co-morbidities. 
The current study has strengths and limitations. Strengths include its 
systematic collection of data, with consistent laboratory monitoring, 
from an exceptionally large number of hypoPT patients of varying eti­
ology, managed by a single endocrinologist (KKW), and with, at any 
point in time, consistent target goals for serum and urine calcium. 
Except as indicated in the Methods, all data from patients enrolled in the 
indicated individual studies have been included. Limitations include the 
lack of an initial prospective plan to compare therapeutic responses by 
etiology, and the open-label design of the underlying studies. Indeed, 
knowledge of the diverse hypoPT etiologies has itself evolved substan­
tially over the 27-year period of these studies. Also, therapeutic targets 
for serum and urine calcium became gradually individualized by etiol­
ogy as new knowledge evolved. Thus, to a differing extent by etiology, 
the results reflect the average from therapeutic targets that were 
evolving over time —especially the need to keep serum calcium below 
normal in the CaR patients, which resulted in a somewhat lower mean 
serum calcium compared to the other etiologies. Lastly, many statistical 
analyses have been included as an aid to judgment, without correction 
for multiple comparisons, and should not be interpreted as formal hy­
pothesis testing. 
5.2. Future directions 
A greater understanding of the spectrum of severity within the 
various hypoPT etiologies is essential to optimizing therapy in this dis­
order. Ignoring such differences or excluding entire patient populations, 
such as CaR, from investigative studies leads to insufficient management 
guidelines that serve only a subset of hypoPT patients. 
Our previous study of PTH 1–34 delivered by an insulin pump pro­
duced a pharmacodynamic profile that closely mimicked normal phys­
iology. In this previous study, pump delivery of PTH 1–34 normalized 
serum and urine calcium and markers of bone turnover and reduced the 
need for magnesium supplements. We hypothesize that the key to this 
improved calcium homeostasis was the delivery of PTH 1–34 in small 
discrete bursts of 0.5 μL (0.1 μg PTH 1–34) given approximately every 
10 min, thus simulating endogenous secretion and minimizing the 
amplitude of calcium fluctuations. We concluded that this modulation of 
the pharmacodynamic profile could serve as a model for optimal PTH 
1–34 delivery [13,14]. 
Long-acting PTH analogs and PTH receptor allosteric modulators are 
promising and may reduce or eliminate the post-injection calcium ex­
cursions in blood and urine that were observed with once- or twice-daily 
PTH injections [48,49]. To test this hypothesis, future studies should 
include all etiology groups to determine appropriate dosing recom­
mendations for the wide array of hypoPT patients. Furthermore, new 
therapies should provide the option to titrate doses in real time as 
symptoms or biochemical instabilities occur.

Abstract
Context: Our study explores the impact of human parathyroid hormone (PTH) 1-34 injections (PTH therapy) on growth, areal bone mineral 
density (BMD), and bone quality (measured by trabecular bone score, TBS) in hypoparathyroidism due to autoimmune polyendocrine 
syndrome type 1 (APS-1) or an activating variant of the calcium sensing receptor (CaR).
Objective: To assess associations of (1) age and PTH therapy duration with age-standardized Z-scores for height (HAZ), BMD (BMD-Z), and TBS 
(TBS-Z) in CaR or APS-1, and (2) APS-1 disease severity with BMD-Z and TBS-Z.
Methods: This secondary analysis pooled linear growth and lumbar spine (LS) dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry data from studies of 
hypoparathyroidism with mean baseline age of 13.7 ± 5.5 years. Comparing the 2 diagnostic etiologies (18 APS-1 and 9 CaR), we examined 
the impact of age and PTH duration on HAZ, LS-BMD-Z, and LS-TBS-Z using longitudinal mixed-effects modeling.
Results: During PTH therapy, mean HAZ remained below 0 in the APS-1 group at all ages, whereas HAZ increased in the CaR group (age by group 
interaction P < .0001). Mean LS-BMD-Z were normal (BMD-Z: 0 ± 1) for both groups. Mean LS-TBS-Z were near or above 0 and differed by group; 
CaR showed an upward trajectory according to time on PTH whereas the APS-1 group maintained a LS-TBS-Z of approximately 0 (time by group 
interaction P = .02). The APS-1 group with greater disease severity (≥7 manifestations) had lower LS-BMD-Z and LS-TBS-Z than the less severe 
APS-1 or CaR groups.
Conclusion: Our study highlights distinct growth and BMD patterns in APS-1 and CaR and underscores the need for careful monitoring and 
tailored treatment strategies to optimize growth and bone health.

Hypoparathyroidism is a rare disease of mineral metabolism 
of heterogeneous etiologies. Over 3 decades, we studied 
both adults and children with a wide array of etiologies and 
severity of disease (1). Our study of hypoparathyroidism in 
children has focused mainly on 2 genetic forms of hypopara­
thyroidism: autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1 
(APS-1), also known as autoimmune polyendocrinopathy, 
candidiasis, and ectodermal dystrophy (APECED), and hypo­
parathyroidism resulting from an activating pathogenic vari­
ant of the calcium sensing receptor, referred to here as CaR. 
The pathophysiology of both diseases was identified 3 decades 
ago with the discovery of variants in the AIRE and CaSR

genes, the underlying etiology of APS-1 and CaR, respectively. 
Conventional therapy of these conditions with oral calcitriol 
and calcium supplements (conventional therapy) can lead to 
excessive urine calcium excretion, renal damage, and de­
creased renal function (1, 2). Estimates of hypoparathyroid­
ism prevalence in the United States, Denmark, and Italy 
include predominantly adults with surgically induced hypo­
parathyroidism are all in the range of 23 to 37 per 100 000 
(3). The estimated prevalence of APS-1 in the United States 
is 2.7 per 100 000 and is higher in Persian Jews (1:9000), 
Sardinians (1:14 400), and Finns (1:25 000) (4). Based on a 
US cohort predominantly comprising individuals of northern 
European descent, the prevalence of CaR is estimated at 3.9 
per 100 000 (5).
Patients with CaR have hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, 
hyperphosphatemia, and hypercalciuria with a high risk of 
nephrocalcinosis and compromised renal function but are 
otherwise generally healthy. By contrast, APS-1 is a multisys­
tem autoimmune disease potentially affecting every organ in 
the body. By late adolescence, parathyroid insufficiency 
(86% of patients), adrenal insufficiency (70%), and gastro­
intestinal tract dysfunction (77%) are evident in most patients 
with APS-1 (6). Several APS-1 disease manifestations or their 
therapies, including hypogonadism, chronic malabsorption, 
and adrenal insufficiency requiring long-term glucocorticoid 
therapy, may adversely affect areal bone mineral density 
(BMD). Isolated intestinal malabsorption may alter mineral 
metabolism leading to osteopenia in adults or insufficient 
bone accrual in children (7, 8). Chronic glucocorticoids 
needed to treat chronic illness in childhood may have adverse 
effects on the growth plate and bone formation leading to 
growth failure and secondary osteoporosis (9). Although post­
surgical hypoparathyroidism in adults is typically associated 
with increased bone mass, Mäkitie and colleagues reported 
low BMD in one-third of their adult APS-1 cohort (10). 
Subsequently, the same group reported (11) low trabecular 
volumetric BMD measured by peripheral quantitative com­
puted tomography in patients with APS-1 with more severe 
disease (≥7 concurrent disease manifestations).
Our studies of twice-daily synthetic human parathyroid 
(PTH) 1-34 subcutaneous injections (PTH therapy) in adults 
and children with various etiologies of hypoparathyroidism 
have demonstrated that PTH therapy is more effective in 
maintaining eucalcemia with reduced urine calcium excretion 
than conventional therapy (1). We previously reported normal 
bone accrual and linear growth velocity in 9 children with 
CaR and 5 children with APS-1 aged >5 years and treated 
with PTH therapy (2). These findings, however, are limited 
by the small number of children, especially for APS-1 patients 
with severe disease.
Trabecular bone score (TBS) has been used to assess bone 
health in adults with hypoparathyroidism and is derived 
from the dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) image of 
the lumbar spine. It is a textural index related to bone micro­
architecture based on the pixel gray level variations in the 
DXA image. TBS reveals information not captured from 
standard DXA analysis and provides information on skeletal 
microarchitecture, which relates to bone strength and quality 
(12, 13). A higher TBS in adults is associated with better, 
fracture-resistant microarchitecture, whereas low TBS is asso­
ciated with higher risk for fracture and poor microarchitec­
ture (13). Silva reported normal TBS in adults with 
predominantly postsurgical hypoparathyroidism treated 
with conventional therapy (14). In a review of studies of sec­
ondary osteoporosis in adults, Ulivieri concluded that TBS 
provided a more reliable prediction of fracture risk than lum­
bar spine BMD (13). TBS reference values for children and 
adolescents provide a valuable tool to identify children with 
compromised bone health either due to chronic illness or the 
associated treatments beyond what is measured by standard 
DXA-derived BMD (15). To date, TBS has not been reported 
in children with hypoparathyroidism.
Expanding on our prior report, the objectives of this study 
were to assess age-related patterns of linear growth and bone 
density, including assessments of bone quality by TBS, in chil­
dren with CaR or APS-1. We examined whether duration of 
PTH therapy was associated with growth and bone outcomes. 
In addition, we examined the impact of disease severity on 
these outcomes in children with APS-1 given their greater vul­
nerability to diminished bone health due to chronic illness and 
multisystem organ failure. We hypothesized that the effects of 
age and PTH treatment duration on height, BMD, and TBS 
would differ between the 2 hypoparathyroidism etiologies 
and further predicted differences in bone architecture accord­
ing to disease severity within the APS-1 subgroups.
Materials and Methods
This is a secondary analysis of pooled data on patients with 
hypoparathyroidism treated with twice-daily PTH injection 
therapy from 2 studies previously described (1, 2, 6). In a long- 
term study of PTH treatment of hypoparathyroidism of various 
etiologies, height and BMD measurements by DXA were ob­
tained every 6 months beginning with the initiation of PTH 
treatment (2). Patients in the second study (6) had growth 
and BMD measurements at 1 time point at varying times after 
initiation of PTH treatment by their local physicians. All pa­
tients were enrolled into an institution review board–approved 
protocol at the NIH and signed informed consents 
(92-CH-0011; 11-I-0187). We included patients with biochem­
ically confirmed hypoparathyroidism due to APS-1 or CaR. 
Patients with APS-1 were diagnosed based on clinical manifes­
tations with confirmation by AIRE gene testing. All patients 
with CaR had a pathogenic variant of the CaSR gene. BMD 
measures in 4 children (2 CaR and 2 APS-1) measured prior 
to the year 2000 were excluded as they were performed on a 
DXA machine not compatible with current reference norms 
(16, 17). Two APS-1 patients received daily growth hormone 
injections and were excluded from the analysis. Our resultant 
analytical sample included 18 patients with APS-1 and 9 pa­
tients with CaR. We restricted bone analyses to data collected 
up to 5.98 years of PTH treatment, due to diminishing numbers 
of observations for both etiologies beyond that point.
Data abstracted from the medical chart included height, 
duration of disease, demographics, dates of PTH therapy, 
and comorbidities. Height was measured using a wall- 
mounted, Harpenden stadiometer. Height for age Z-scores 
(HAZ) were calculated based on the CDC 2000 growth charts 
(18) using the ZANTHRO procedure in Stata 16.1 (Stata 
Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). For individuals older 
than 20 years, HAZ was computed using reference values at 
age 20 years. APS-1 disease manifestations were documented 
during evaluation at the NIH Clinical Center, and the disease 
surveillance database was regularly updated (6).
Lumbar spine DXA scans were acquired with Hologic 
(Marlborough, MA, USA) bone densitometers (QDR4500A

BMD and TBS According to APS-1 Disease Severity
Disease severity in the APS-1 group, defined according to 
numbers of disease manifestations at baseline, was equally 
divided between mild (3-6 disease manifestations, n = 9) and 
severe (≥7 manifestations, n = 9) disease. Two patients from 
the mild group progressed to the more severe group between 
4 and 5 years of PTH therapy. BMD-Z according to disease 
severity, duration of PTH therapy, and adjusted for age re­
vealed a higher BMD in the milder APS-1 and CaR groups 
than BMD in the APS-1 group with severe disease (interaction 
P = .0006; Fig. 3A). Similarly, the TBS-Z in the milder APS-1 
and CaR groups were higher than in the APS-1 group with se­
vere disease (interaction P = .003; Fig. 3B).

Sensitivity analysis comparing BMD-Z with and without height 
Z-score adjustment revealed an approximately 0.3 SD upward 
shift with height Z-score adjustment when examining BMD 
patterns according to age or duration of PTH therapy (Fig. 4A
and 4B, unadjusted vs Figure 4C and 4D, height adjusted). 
Height adjustment eliminated the significant differences between 
diagnostic groups when comparing spine BMD-Z by diagnostic 
group and years on PTH, suggesting the differences between the 
groups were related to disparities in height (Fig. 4B, not height 
adjusted P = .006, vs Fig. 4D, height adjusted P = .1).

Our study explored the differences between 2 etiologies of 
hypoparathyroidism, CaR and APS-1, regarding linear 
growth, BMD, and TBS, a measure of bone microarchitecture.

Our conclusions are consistent with our prior study dem­
onstrating PTH treatment was associated with normal BMD 
according to age in children for both etiologies (2). The trend 
toward BMD-Z in the high-normal or above-normal range in 
our young adult patients (age >19 years) is consistent with 
what is reported in other studies of adults with hypopara­
thyroidism (10, 14, 26). Our study, however, could not re­
veal the maximal benefit of PTH on BMD, as patients were 
not uniformly followed to peak bone mass. We further dem­
onstrate that PTH therapy was associated with an increase in 
spine TBS-Z for the CaR group and not the APS-1 group. 
Bone architecture deficits were apparent only in patients 
with more severe APS-1 whose TBS-Z were low compared 
with milder cases of APS-1. Our TBS results were consistent 
with the findings of Laakso et al, who described higher 
prevalence of fractures and low trabecular and volumetric 
bone density on peripheral quantitative computed tomog­
raphy in a cohort of mostly adults with severe APS-1 (11).
Our findings indicate that TBS appears to mirror spine 
BMD values. Compromised bone architecture may be the 
underlying cause of increased risk of vertebral fracture in 
adults with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism (26). In other 
chronic illnesses such as type 1 diabetes, osteogenesis imper­
fecta or renal failure, TBS has served as a tool to detect in­
creased fracture risk and bone pathology in adults and 
children beyond the standard DXA measurement of bone 
mass (13, 27-29). Bone disease in children with APS-1 is likely 
to emerge along a continuum of damage depending on the se­
verity of each disease manifestation and timing and duration 
of interventions. To detect subtle bone changes that may be 
present in the milder cases of APS-1, however, would require 
a larger sample size than what we had in our study.
The small number of patients limited our ability to detect 
subtle deficiencies in growth and bone health and did not per­
mit testing differences in linear growth and bone health with 
sex as a covariate. Furthermore, a larger cohort followed to 
peak bone mass would better reflect the full benefit of long- 
term PTH therapy on bone. We studied young adults in the 
APS-1 group and not in the CaR group. Therefore, our find­
ings may only be generalizable to the age groups studied in 
each etiology. Although age was considered in the analyses, 
residual confounding effects due to age may be operative. 
An assessment of the potential beneficial effects of PTH ther­
apy on bone microarchitecture compared with conventional 
therapy with calcitriol and calcium was not part of our study 
and remains a knowledge gap for future investigation.
Our study revealed that the 2 genetic forms of hypopara­
thyroidism, APS-1 and CaR, have unique phenotypes in terms 
of growth, BMD, and bone architecture. Linear growth re­
mained compromised in APS-1, regardless of age or time on 
PTH. Except for the youngest age groups, PTH-treated pa­
tients with CaR had normal growth. TBS was normal in CaR 
and in the APS-1 group with fewer disease manifestations. 
TBS, however, was compromised in the APS-1 group with 
greater disease severity. Our findings should alert providers 
who care for children with APS-1 to monitor linear growth 
and skeletal health. The standard management of APS-1, cur­
rently focused on providing therapeutic interventions as dis­
ease symptoms emerge, may lead to compromised bone 
microarchitecture in childhood or early adulthood. Recent ad­
vances have led to a greater understanding of the underlying 
pathophysiology along with an awareness of the widespread 
phenotypic diversity of disease manifestations (6). Widely 
available genetic testing allows for timely diagnosis and the im­
plementation of early preventive therapy. Future directions in­
clude innovative approaches to APS-1 therapy with immune 
modulators, such as ruxolitinib, an inhibitor of Janus kinase 
1 and 2. This immunotherapy targets the underlying pathogen­
ic mechanism, which has been identified in APS-1 as excessive 
interferon-γ production by AIRE-deficient T-cells (30). This 
approach may reduce the need for glucocorticoid therapy, 
with its adverse effects on bone and linear growth, and has 
the potential to rescue target organs, such as the gastrointes­
tinal tract, from irreparable damage (31).
