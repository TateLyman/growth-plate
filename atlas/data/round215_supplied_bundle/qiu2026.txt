Introduction
Osimertinib serves as first-line therapy for patients with 
EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer and is also 
an effective second-line option for those with the EGFR 
T790M resistance mutation [1]. The FLAURA trial 
demonstrated that osimertinib significantly extended 
progression-free survival (PFS) compared to first-gener­
ation EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKI), with 
a median PFS of 18.9 months for patients treated with 
osimertinib versus 10.2 months for the control group [2].

Abstract
Osimertinib is a first-line treatment for advanced non-small cell lung cancer with epidermal growth factor receptor 
(EGFR)-sensitive mutations; however, the severe hepatotoxicity of osimertinib is a challenge that has attracted 
attention. In this study, we found that osimertinib directly caused hepatocyte damage, with cells showing 
morphological features of vacuolization after treatment. Using transmission electron microscopy and other 
analytical techniques, we found that excessive activation of the autophagy-lysosome pathway was the main cause 
of cell vacuolation. Mechanistically, osimertinib causes dephosphorylation of transcription factor EB (TFEB), leading 
to its translocation into the nucleus to exert transcriptional regulatory functions. TFEB in the nucleus induces 
autophagy and lysosome biogenesis, thereby causing excessive activation of the autophagy-lysosome pathway 
subsequently leading to vacuolation-associated cell death. Through this study, we have identified S-adenosyl-
L-methionine as a clinical hepatoprotective agent that could mitigate osimertinib-induced hepatocyte damage 
by inhibiting TFEB activation. Collectively, our findings not only lay the groundwork for addressing the clinical 
challenges posed by osimertinib hepatotoxicity but also broaden our understanding of drug-induced hepatocyte 
injury, thereby offering a novel research pathway for the study of drug-induced liver injury.

Cytoplasmic vacuolation, characterized by the pres­
ence of numerous large vacuoles within mammalian 
cell cytoplasm, is a morphological feature frequently 
observed in cancer cells following exposure to pharma­
ceutical agents and various chemicals, such as gambogic 
acid and cyclosporine A [5]. Cytoplasmic vacuoles can 
originate from diverse cellular compartments, including 
macropinosomes, lysosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, 
endolysosomes, and autophagosomes, and are frequently 
associated with cell death [6]. However, cell death that 
occurs through vacuolation is a unique process that does 
not conform to the traditional classifications of lytic and 
apoptotic cell death. Cytoplasmic vacuolation is linked 
to four distinct forms of cell death: methuosis, autosis, 
paraptosis, and lysosome-associated vacuolar cell death 
[7–12]. These distinctions are based on specific mor­
phological features and origins of the vacuoles involved. 
However, the mechanisms underlying cytoplasmic vacu­
ole formation are complicated, and the contribution of 
these forms of cytoplasmic vacuolation-associated cell 
death to drug-induced hepatotoxicity remains unclear.
Transcription factor EB (TFEB) plays a central role 
in orchestrating autophagy and lysosomal function 
[13]. Under nutrient-rich conditions, TFEB is predomi­
nantly cytoplasmic; however, it shifts to the nucleus in 
response to nutrient deprivation. Within the nucleus, 
TFEB acts as a transcription factor that robustly upregu­
lates the expression of genes associated with autophagy 
and lysosomal biogenesis. This promotes the formation 
of autophagosomes, their fusion with lysosomes, and 
their subsequent autophagic degradation [14]. Evidence 
suggests that disruptions in TFEB-mediated lysosomal 
biogenesis and autophagy contribute to alcohol-induced 
chronic liver injury and steatosis, whereas TFEB activa­
tion protects against acetaminophen-induced liver injury 
in mice [15]. Trehalose activates hepatic TFEB but fails to 
ameliorate alcohol-induced TFEB and liver injury in mice 
[16]. Moreover, TFEB-mediated autophagy activation 
facilitates gefitinib-induced hepatotoxicity by selectively 
degrading an important anti-apoptosis factor COX6A1 
(cytochrome c oxidase subunit 6A1) [17]. Thus, the 
roles of TFEB and TFEB-mediated autophagy-lysosome 
activation in liver injury are complex and not yet clearly 
understood and require further investigation.
In this study, we developed both in vivo and in vitro 
models to investigate the hepatotoxic effects of osimer­
tinib and found that it induced cytoplasmic vacu­
olation–associated hepatocyte death and liver injury. 
Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy revealed 
that the activation of the autophagy-lysosome pathway is 
a major contributor to osimertinib-induced cytoplasmic 
vacuolation-associated hepatocyte death. Mechanisti­
cally, osimertinib suppressed TFEB phosphorylation, 
facilitating its translocation to the nucleus and the con­
sequent activation of the autophagy-lysosome pathway. 
Moreover, inhibitors of the autophagy-lysosome path­
way were shown to reduce cytoplasmic vacuolation and 
mitigate osimertinib-induced liver injury. Most notably, 
through a drug screening process, we identified S-ade­
nosyl-L-methionine (SAM) as a protective agent against 
osimertinib-induced hepatotoxicity. Collectively, our 
findings not only shed light on the molecular underpin­
nings of osimertinib-induced hepatotoxicity but also 
present a viable target and therapeutic strategy for clini­
cal management.
Results
Osimertinib induces hepatotoxicity in vivo
To assess osimertinib hepatotoxicity, we administered 
C57BL/6J mice with CMC-Na or osimertinib (50 mg/
kg) daily via gavage (Fig. 1A). After 1 month, body weight 
was significantly lower in the osimertinib group than in 
the vehicle group (Fig. 1B). Serum and livers were col­
lected for further analysis. Consistent with systemic tox­
icity, a decrease in the liver-to-body weight ratio was also 
observed (Fig. 1C). As expected, elevated levels of glu­
tamic-pyruvic transaminase (GPT/ALT) and glutamic-
oxaloacetic transaminase 1 (GOPT1/AST) were found 
in the bloodstream of mice of the osimertinib group 
compared with that of the vehicle group (Fig. 1D-E). 
Histological analysis also revealed larger necrotic areas 
with typical bridging necrosis within the centrilobular 
region (black arrow), immune cell infiltration (yellow 
arrow), and cytoplasmic vacuoles (green arrow) in the 
livers of the osimertinib group, indicating that osimer­
tinib induced hepatotoxicity (Fig. 1F, H). However, Sirius 
Red staining revealed no significant difference in colla­
gen deposition between the two groups (Fig. 1G, I), sug­
gesting that a one-month osimertinib treatment did not 
induce apparent liver fibrosis. To further validate the cell 
death of hepatocytes, liver slices were analyzed using 
TdT-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) stain­
ing, and the results showed that TUNEL-positive hepato­
cytes significantly increased after osimertinib treatment 
(Fig. 1J-K). These data indicate that osimertinib induces 
significant, non-fibrotic liver injury in mice.
Osimertinib induces cytoplasmic vacuolation–associated 
hepatocyte death
Having established that osimertinib induces hepatotox­
icity in vivo, we investigated whether it directly causes 
hepatocyte death in vitro and determined the underlying 
mode of cell death. We then validated the toxic effects of 
osimertinib in human and mouse hepatocyte cell lines,

MIHA and AML12, to develop a cell model of osimer­
tinib-induced hepatotoxicity. According to a study of 
small-molecule kinase inhibitors, the clinically relevant 
Cmax of osimertinib is approximately 0.5 µM [18]. Based 
on the Cmax, MIHA and AML12 cells were treated with 
different concentrations of osimertinib for 48 h. Increas­
ing osimertinib dose reduced the survival rate of both cell 
lines (Fig. 2A, B).
Subsequently, we investigated the primary mechanism 
by which osimertinib induces hepatocyte death. As hepa­
tocyte apoptosis was detected in liver tissue (Fig. 1J-K), 
we performed PI/Annexin V staining of MIHA cells 
at various concentrations for 48 h. We did not detect 
any significant increase in apoptotic cells upon osimer­
tinib treatment (Fig. 2C). Moreover, immunoblotting 
analysis of lysates of cells treated with 4 µM osimer­
tinib for up to 48 h revealed no significant change in the 
levels of caspase-3 processing and poly (ADP-ribose) 
polymerase (PARP) cleavage, which are two classical 
apoptosis markers (Fig. 3D). As a positive control, cis­
platin (DDP), a known apoptosis inducer [19], caused 
marked cleavage of caspase-3 and PARP in MIHA cells 
(Fig. 2D). These results indicate that osimertinib did not 
directly induce caspase-mediated apoptosis in hepato­
cytes. Furthermore, various cell death inhibitors, such 
as Z-VAD-FMK (an apoptosis inhibitor), necrostatin-1 
(a necroptosis inhibitor), cycloheximide (a paraptosis 
inhibitor) and rapamycin (an autophagy activator), were 
unable to reverse hepatocyte death induced by osimer­
tinib (Fig. 2E).
Given that the cell death was independent of these 
classical pathways, we therefore turned to examine its 
morphological characteristics. Interestingly, cellular 
morphology analysis revealed that osimertinib treat­
ment led to a significant accumulation of cytoplasmic 
vacuoles, with the volume and quantity being time- and

Osimertinib-induced cytoplasmic vacuolation–associated 
cell death is related to autolysosome and lysosome
As cytoplasmic vacuolation was a prominent morpholog­
ical feature observed in osimertinib-treated hepatocytes 
(Fig. 2F-G), we next investigated the origin and nature of 
these vacuoles to understand their role in cell death. We 
used electron microscopy to elucidate the structure of 
these cytoplasmic vacuoles and discovered that they were 
autolysosome-like structures containing a substantial 
number of substrates (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we applied 
GFP-MAP1LC3/LC3 (microtubule-associated protein 1 
light chain 3) and BFP-LAMP1 ((lysosomal-associated 
membrane protein 1) to target autophagosomes and lyso­
somes, respectively, and observed colocalization of most 
of the autophagosomes marked by LC3 and lysosomes 
marked by LAMP1. Moreover, they coincided with the 
vacuoles (Fig. 3B). These results confirmed that the cyto­
plasmic vacuoles were autolysosomes. Osimertinib was 
found to induce autophagy in non-small cell lung cancer 
cells. Thus, we used the mCherry-GFP-LC3 autophagy 
reporter to monitor autophagic flux and observed the 
accumulation of autophagosomes and autolysosomes 
following osimertinib treatment (Fig. 3C). In addition, 
the expression levels of SQSTM1/p62 (sequestosome 
1), ATG7, BECN1 and LC3-II gradually increased in a 
dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3D, S1A), indicating an 
accumulation of autophagosomes. To determine whether 
autophagy is directly involved in osimertinib-induced 
increases in p62 and LC3-II, we employed the autoph­
agy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA) and found 3-MA 
treatment effectively reduced the protein levels of p62 
and LC3-II (Fig. S1B-C). LysoSencer and LysoTracker 
analyses further indicated that the lysosome size and 
quantity were substantially increased (Fig. 3E). Taken 
together, these findings suggest that osimertinib triggers 
autophagosome formation and promotes lysosomal bio­
genesis or expansion. However, the observed accumula­
tion of autophagic substrates (p62 and LC3-II) alongside 
enlarged lysosomes raises the possibility that lysosomal 
clearance capacity may be impaired or that autophagic 
flux is overloaded.
Lysosome inhibition protects against osimertinib-induced 
liver injury
Considering the accumulation of both autophago­
somes and lysosomes induced by osimertinib (Fig. 3), 
we hypothesized that lysosomal function contributes to 
osimertinib-induced cell death. To distinguish whether 
the accumulation of SQSTM1/p62 and LC3-II resulted 
from enhanced autophagic flux or from a blockage at 
the degradative stage due to lysosomal dysfunction, we 
assessed their protein levels with or without bafilomy­
cin A1 (BafA1), an inhibitor of autolysosome maturation 
and lysosomal acidification. The osimertinib-induced 
accumulation of SQSTM1/p62 and LC3-II was more 
significant in the presence of BafA1 (Fig. 4A), indicating 
that autophagic flux was enhanced rather than impaired. 
Strikingly, analysis of cellular morphology revealed that 
BafA1 treatment significantly reduced the number of 
osimertinib-induced cytoplasmic vacuoles (Fig. 4B). In 
addition, BafA1 reversed osimertinib-induced hepato­
cyte death by inhibiting lysosomal acidification (Fig. 4C, 
D). To translate this finding into a more clinically rel­
evant context, we employed hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), 
a clinically used lysosome inhibitor [21], to inhibit lyso­
somal function in vivo (Fig. 4E). As expected, HCQ treat­
ment significantly decreased the necrotic areas in the 
livers of mice with osimertinib-induced liver injury (Fig. 
4F), suggesting that lysosomes are a promising target for 
the treatment of osimertinib-induced liver injury.

rapidly applied in patients with osimertinib-induced 
hepatotoxicity, we screened a library of approved drugs

TFEB mediates activation of the osimertinib-induced 
autophagy-lysosome pathway
To further elucidate the transcriptional regulation under­
lying the observed autophagy-lysosome activation, we 
performed transcriptomic analysis of the livers from 
osimertinib-treated mice and hepatocytes. Liver tissues 
were collected from the control and osimertinib-treated 
mice and subjected to transcriptome profiling. Genes 
that were significantly upregulated or downregulated 
(fold change > 1.5, P < 0.05) in osimertinib-treated livers 
compared with the control were identified (Fig. S2A). 
Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated 
that significantly downregulated genes in the osimer­
tinib-treated livers were enriched in fatty acid metabo­
lism, cell morphogenesis, and cytoskeleton organization 
(Fig. S2B), and the significantly upregulated genes in 
osimertinib-treated livers were enriched in the autoph­
agy pathway, vacuolar acidification, activation of immune 
response, lysosome, and wound healing (Fig. S2C). Addi­
tionally, heatmap analysis revealed that the expression of 
lysosome- and autophagy-related genes, such as Lamp1, 
Ctsb, Atg14, Atg2b were significantly increased in the 
osimertinib-treated group (Fig. S2D). Taken together, 
these data are consistent with the autophagy-lysosome 
activation that was observed upon osimertinib treatment 
in vitro and with osimertinib-induced liver injury accom­
panied by immune response activation.
To further investigate the mechanism of osimertinib-
induced autophagy-lysosome pathway activation in hepa­
tocytes, we collected osimertinib-treated AML12 cells 
and subjected them to transcriptome profiling. Genes 
that were significantly upregulated or downregulated 
(fold change > 1.5, P < 0.05) in osimertinib-treated cells 
compared with the control were identified (Fig. 6A). GO 
enrichment analysis indicated that significantly upregu­
lated genes in osimertinib-treated cells were enriched 
in vacuolar acidification, lysosome and autophagy (Fig. 
6B). Further, GO analyses revealed that genes from DNA 
replication and cell cycle process items were significantly 
downregulated in osimertinib-treated cells compared 
with the control (Fig S2E). Gene Set Enrichment Analy­
sis (GSEA) also revealed that the genes upregulated in 
osimertinib treated cells were enriched in gene signatures 
associated with lysosome (Fig. 6C). Additionally, heat­
map analysis revealed that the expression of lysosome- 
and autophagy-related genes, such as Lamp1, Ctsd, 
Map1lc3b, and Sqstm1, were significantly increased in 
the osimertinib-treated group (Fig. 6D).
Given the protective role of SAM in osimertinib-
induced hepatotoxicity, we conducted a network phar­
macology analysis of SAM-related genes and performed 
a Venn analysis of these genes with those upregulated 
or downregulated by osimertinib (osimertinib-regulated 
genes). Venn analysis showed that 48 genes overlapped 
with SAM-related genes (Fig. S2F). Among these, 37 
genes were associated with lysosomal functions, includ­
ing 18 genes that were highly correlated with TFEB regu­
lation (highlighted in Fig. 6A).

SAM inhibits the nuclear translocation of TFEB to inhibit 
osimersitib-induced lysosome activation
Given that the phosphorylation status and subcellular 
localization of TFEB are critical determinants of its tran­
scriptional activity, we investigated whether osimertinib 
affects TEFB phosphorylation and cellular localization. 
Western blot analysis revealed that TFEB phosphoryla­
tion was significantly inhibited by osimertinib in a time-
dependent manner (Fig. 7A). Immunofluorescence assays 
showed that nuclear TFEB levels significantly increased 
after osimertinib treatment (Fig. 7B). TFEB binds to the 
promoter regions of many autophagy and lysosome genes 
and enhances their transcription under stress conditions. 
Consistently, heatmap and qRT-PCR analyses confirmed 
the upregulation of lysosomal and autophagy-related 
genes such as Lamp1, Ctsd, Map1lc3b, and Sqstm1 (Figs. 
6D and 7C). These results further confirm that TFEB is 
a target of osimertinib-induced cytoplasmic vacuola­
tion-associated hepatocyte death by inhibiting the phos­
phorylation of TFEB to activate the autophagy-lysosome 
pathway.
We then considered whether SAM protects against 
osimertinib-induced hepatotoxicity by inhibiting the 
TFEB-mediated autophagy-lysosome pathway. Inter­
estingly, SAM could not reverse osimertinib-induced 
TFEB dephosphorylation but partly inhibited the 
nuclear translocation of TFEB and the upregulation of 
lysosome-related gene mRNA levels (Fig. 7D-F). Fur­
thermore, western blot analysis revealed that SAM sig­
nificantly reduced LAMP1 protein levels, indicating that 
lysosome quantity was significantly reduced (Fig. 7G). In 
addition, LC3-II expression was reduced by SAM treat­
ment, whereas SQSTM1/p62 expression was not (Fig. 
7G). In addition, LAMP1 immunofluorescence showed 
a sharp increase in LAMP1 expression in osimertinib-
treated mouse liver sections, indicating enlarged lyso­
somes induced by osimertinib (Fig. 7H). SAM effectively 
suppressed the enlargement of lysosomes induced by 
osimertinib, as the LAMP1 expression area was reduced 
after SAM combination treatment (Fig. 7H). Altogether, 
these results indicate that SAM suppresses the nuclear 
translocation of TFEB to inhibit lysosomal enlargement 
and mitigate osimertinib-induced hepatotoxicity.
Clinical potential of SAM as a therapeutic agent for 
osimertinib-induced lysosome enlargement–related 
hepatotoxicity
Critically, the analysis of liver biopsies from patients 
experiencing osimertinib-induced hepatotoxicity reca­
pitulated our experimental findings, showing signifi­
cant TFEB nuclear translocation and increased LAMP1 
expression compared with those from healthy controls 
(Fig. 8A-C), strongly supporting the clinical relevance 
of the TFEB-lysosomal pathway in osimertinib-induced 
toxicity.
Based on the significant protective effect of SAM on 
the autophagy-lysosome pathway activation–mediated 
hepatotoxicity of osimertinib, we investigated whether 
SAM affects the anti-tumor effect of osimertinib in the 
osimertinib-sensitive lung cancer cell line NCI-H1975. 
First, we evaluated the cytotoxic effect of osimertinib 
on NCI-H1975 and identified that the IC50 was less than 
0.039 µM (Fig. 8D), which is consistent with previously 
reported results [25]. Subsequently, we used 0.039 µM 
osimertinib in combination with SAM to examine the 
impact of SAM on the osimertinib-induced cytotox­
icity in NCI-H1975 cells. Cellular morphology analy­
sis revealed that osimertinib induced NCI-H1975 cell 
death and apoptosis, whereas SAM had no impact on 
the induction of cell death and apoptosis triggered by 
osimertinib (Fig. 8E). CCK8 results showed that SAM 
alone had no cytotoxic effect on NCI-H1975 cells, and 
its co-administration with osimertinib did not affect 
the antitumor efficacy of osimertinib (Fig. 8F). More­
over, immunoblot analysis of cell lysates revealed that 
osimertinib significantly increased the levels of cleaved 
PARP and cleaved caspase-3, indicating the induction of 
apoptosis, whereas SAM had no impact on osimertinib-
induced apoptosis (Fig. 8G, H). These results suggest that 
SAM is a clinically rapidly available drug that can allevi­
ate the hepatotoxicity of osimertinib without affecting its 
anti-tumor effect.

Osimertinib induces a distinct, TFEB-driven form of 
cytoplasmic vacuolation-associated cell death in 
hepatocytes
Cytoplasmic vacuolization induced by exposure to vari­
ous chemicals and bioactive substances has been exten­
sively documented [5]. Cytoplasmic vacuolation–linked 
cell death encompasses methuosis, autosis, paraptosis, 
and lysosome-associated vacuolar death, distinguished 
by vacuolar origin and morphology [26–28]. Interest­
ingly, osimertinib triggers the formation of extensive 
vacuoles to induce paraptosis-like cell death in glioblas­
toma cells [20]. Paraptosis is an alternative cell death 
pathway characterized by vacuolation and damage to the 
endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria [29]. However, 
in the present study, we demonstrated that osimertinib 
triggers hepatocyte death associated with cytoplasmic 
vacuolation, which cannot be rescued by paraptosis 
inhibitors. Further studies revealed that autophagy and 
lysosome activation occur in osimertinib-treated hepa­
tocytes, and the inhibition of autolysosomes alleviates 
hepatocyte death and liver injury. Therefore, the above­
mentioned evidence indicates that osimertinib-induced 
vacuolation is distinct from that observed in previous 
studies. Osimertinib-induced vacuolation is caused by 
the co-activation of autophagy and lysosomes mediated 
by TFEB, manifesting as autolysosomes or lysosomes, 
and is reversible by autolysosome/lysosome inhibitors.
Notably, cytoplasmic vacuolization–associated cell 
death involves crosstalk among multiple cellular stress 
pathways (encompassing ER stress, heightened mem­
brane permeability, ion imbalance, and mitochondrial 
dysfunction), and is morphologically defined by the 
presence of vacuoles preceding death [30]. In this study, 
we observed that osimertinib caused necrosis, apopto­
sis, and cytoplasmic vacuolation in liver tissue in vivo, 
whereas it induced cytoplasmic vacuolation and death 
without apoptosis in vitro. This suggests that apoptosis 
observed in the liver in vivo is not solely owing to events 
within hepatocytes. H&E staining and RNA-seq analy­
sis revealed immune cell infiltration and an increased 
immune response in the liver (Fig. 1E, S2B), indicating 
that this apoptosis may be secondary, caused by infiltrat­
ing immune cells attacking hepatocytes.
Thus, osimertinib not only induces various types of 
cytoplasmic vacuolation–associated cell death in glio­
blastoma cells and hepatocytes but also elicits a novel 
form of vacuolar cell death in hepatocytes, characterized 
by a unique set of features that have not been previously 
reported.
Autophagy- and lysosome-dependent cell death in drug-
induced liver injury
Elevated aminotransferase levels are commonly observed 
during TKI treatment [31]. TKIs are associated with 
several risk factors for developing drug-induced liver 
injury. These include their high daily dose requirements, 
properties as P450 enzyme substrates, and significant 
involvement in hepatic metabolism [32]. In this study, 
we demonstrated that osimertinib induces vacuolation-
like hepatocyte death, which is related to the activation 
of lysosomes and autophagy. Although autophagy acti­
vation–related hepatotoxicity has been reported to be 
involved in gefitinib-induced hepatocyte apoptosis by 
selectively degrading the important anti-apoptotic fac­
tor COX6A1 [17], osimertinib mainly induces TFEB 
activation to activate autophagy and lysosomes to form 
oversized lysosomes/autolysosomes. Therefore, osimer­

autolysosome-dependent cytoplasmic-associated vacu­

“cytoplasmic vacuolation–associated cell death” because: 
(1) vacuolization is an exceptionally striking and defining 
morphological feature, and (2) it distinguishes this pro­
cess from previously reported forms of autophagy-depen­
dent cell death, where cytoplasmic vacuolization has not 
been consistently documented as a hallmark characteris­
tic. In addition, our study underscores the importance of

Notably, the autophagic response to osimertinib pres­
ents a unique molecular signature: the upregulation of 
autophagy is accompanied by an increase in both LC3-II 
and the autophagic receptor p62/SQSTM1. This differs 
from the typical expectation where autophagy activation 
enhances p62 degradation. This phenomenon likely stems 
from a particularly robust transcriptional activation of 
autophagy, where the synthesis of autophagic compo­
nents, including p62, outpaces the degradative capacity 
of lysosomes. As noted in established autophagy guide­
lines, “there is not always a clear correlation between 
increases in LC3-II and decreases in SQSTM1” [33]. In 
our model, qRT-PCR analysis confirms that SQSTM1 
mRNA is significantly upregulated upon osimertinib 
treatment (Fig. 7C), indicating transcriptional induc­
tion. Thus, the observed p62 protein accumulation likely 
reflects a dynamic balance between increased synthe­
sis and ongoing lysosomal turnover. To confirm this 
accumulation is consistent with functional autophagy 
activation rather than a blockade, we employed pharma­
cological inhibitors. Co-treatment with the early-stage 
inhibitor 3-MA suppressed the osimertinib-induced p62 
upregulation (Fig. S1B-C), indicating its accumulation 
is dependent on early autophagic signaling. Further­
more, the late-stage inhibitor BafA1, which blocks lyso­
somal degradation, further enhanced p62 accumulation 
in osimertinib-treated cells (Fig. 4A). This result con­
firms that autophagic flux is functional and that p62 is 
being actively delivered to lysosomes. Collectively, these 
data demonstrate that osimertinib activates a functional 
autophagic process, with the concurrent p62 accumula­
tion representing a state of high transcriptional output 
coupled with active degradation.
Pathologically, the liver injury induced by osimertinib 
in our model manifested primarily as an acute inflamma­
tory insult, featuring hepatocyte necrosis, vacuolization, 
and immune infiltration, without progression to signifi­
cant fibrosis within the 30-day treatment period. This 
acute, non-fibrotic pathological profile aligns well with 
clinical case reports of osimertinib-related hepatotoxic­
ity, where liver biopsies predominantly show hepatocel­
lular injury features like steatosis and cholestasis without 
reported fibrosis [34]. Our experimental model thus reca­
pitulates this key aspect of the clinical presentation.
Interestingly, our previous study revealed that autoph­
agy activation could alleviate acetaminophen-, isonia­
zid-, or toosendanin-induced liver injury [35]; however, 
autophagy activation is the reason for gefitinib-induced 
liver injury [17]. In this study, we found that osimer­
tinib-induced hepatocyte death could not be rescued 
by the autophagy activator rapamycin but was rescued 
by the autophagy inhibitors BafA1 and HCQ, indicating 
different roles of autophagy activation in TKIs and other 
drugs.
However, these studies have certain limitations. 
Although HCQ is an alkalinizing lysosomatropic drug 
that accumulates in lysosomes, where it inhibits impor­
tant functions by increasing the pH [36], HCQ is one 
of the most common drugs used to relieve acute and 
chronic inflammatory diseases. Future studies should 
investigate the role of immunosuppressive mechanisms 
in the therapeutic management of osimertinib-induced 
hepatotoxicity.
In conclusion, our study underscores the critical role 
of TFEB-mediated transcriptional programs in osimer­
tinib-induced hepatotoxicity, presenting a significant 
consideration for the design and risk assessment of future 
tyrosine kinase inhibitors. The identified autolysosome-
dependent cell death pathway, with its unique molecular 
and pathological features, adds a novel dimension to our 
understanding of TKI-associated liver injury.
SAM stands out from other hepatoprotective drugs for 
osimertinib-induced liver injury by inhibiting lysosome 
enlargement
SAM is an important bioactive substance formed in the 
human body by the combination of methionine and ade­
nosine triphosphate (ATP) and is widely present in all tis­
sues and body fluids [37]. SAM plays a central role in a 
variety of biochemical processes, including methylation, 
transsulfuration, and polyamine synthesis and is primar­
ily used to support recovery from liver damage caused by 
various factors [38, 39]. SAM acts as a precursor for glu­
tathione synthesis, which is the main protective mecha­
nism of SAM against liver injury [40]. In this study, we 
identified SAM as a protective agent against osimertinib-
induced hepatotoxicity through a drug-screening process 
that distinguishes it from other hepatoprotective drugs 
in clinical use. Mechanistically, SAM protected against 
osimertinib-induced 
hepatotoxicity 
by 
suppressing 
TFEB translocation to the nucleus and inhibiting lyso­
some-related gene transcription induced by osimertinib. 
Moreover, SAM did not alter the anti-tumor effect of 
osimertinib in NCI-H1975 cells, indicating that SAM is a 
potential hepatoprotective drug in patients with osimer­
tinib-induced hepatotoxicity. However, further studies 
are required to investigate the effects of SAM on osimer­
tinib treatment for lung cancer in tumor-bearing mice.
Notably, autophagy is potently suppressed by SAM 
[41]. It has been reported that SAM inhibits autophagy in 
hepatocytes via PP2A methylation to regulate the patho­
genesis of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [42]. However, 
our findings highlight the possibility that SAM modu­
lates the cellular localization of TFEB to inhibit lysosomal 
biogenesis. TFEB is the master regulator of lysosomal 
biogenesis. However, the mechanism by which SAM

In conclusion, our study not only advances our under­
standing of drug-induced liver injury, but also provides 
a potential therapeutic strategy for the management of 
osimertinib-induced hepatotoxicity. Future research 
should focus on exploring the detailed mechanisms by 
which osimertinib or SAM regulate TFEB and its down­

cacy and safety of SAM in clinical settings for patients 
receiving osimertinib treatment.
Limitations and future perspectives
While our study delineates a novel pathway of osimer­
tinib hepatotoxicity, several limitations should be 
acknowledged. First, the 30-day in vivo treatment 
period was sufficient to establish acute injury but may 
not capture potential long-term fibrotic consequences 
of chronic, low-grade damage. Second, the discrepancy 
between in vitro (pure vacuolar death) and in vivo (mixed 
vacuolation, necrosis, and apoptosis) phenotypes war­
rants further investigation into the precise contribution 
of immune cells to the final pathological outcome. Third, 
the exact molecular mechanism by which SAM impedes 
TFEB nuclear translocation without affecting its phos­
phorylation status remains unclear and merits future 
exploration. Finally, while SAM did not affect osimer­
tinib’s cytotoxicity in 2D tumor cell culture, its impact 
on tumor dynamics and drug efficacy in more complex in 
vivo tumor models requires validation.
In conclusion, our work establishes TFEB-mediated 
transcriptional hyperactivation of the autophagy-lys­
osome pathway as a central mechanism underlying 
osimertinib-induced hepatocyte vacuolation and death. 
This pathway, with its unique molecular signature and 
acute pathological presentation, adds a critical dimension 
to our understanding of TKI-associated liver injury. The 
identification of SAM as a targeted therapeutic agent that 
decouples hepatotoxicity from antitumor efficacy offers 
a promising strategy for improving the safety profile of 
osimertinib in clinical practice.
Materials and methods
Animal
C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Weitong Lihua 
Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Male C57BL/6J mice, 
aged 6–8 weeks, were used for drug treatment. The ani­
mals were provided a standard laboratory diet, had free 
access to water, and were maintained at a temperature of 
22 ± 1 °C with a 12-h light–dark cycle and humidity levels 
of 65 ± 5%. Animal experiments were performed in accor­
dance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal 
Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Zhejiang Univer­
sity (IACUC-23–232).
In vivo experiment
Mice were randomly divided into different groups. 
Osimertinib (Aladdin, A302849) was provided as a pow­
der and was reconstituted to form a suspension in 0.1% 
sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na) in a phos­
phate-buffered saline (PBS). A dose of 50 mg/kg osimer­
tinib or vehicle was orally administered daily for 30 days 
or 3 weeks before all mice were sacrificed. To investigate 
the protective effects of hydroxychloroquine (Aladdin, 
C586514) and SAM disulfate tosylate (Aladdin, S196132), 
10 mg/kg hydroxychloroquine or 25 mg/kg SAM disul­
fate tosylate were intraperitoneally injected along with 
osimertinib. The health of all animals was monitored 
daily and their weights were recorded every 5 days.
Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Picrosirius red staining
Mouse liver tissues were immersed in a 10% formalde­
hyde solution for a minimum of 24 h for fixation and then 
embedded in paraffin for histological processing. Thin 
Sect. (3 μm) were prepared and stained with H&E or Pic­
rosirius Red staining. Necrotic regions identified using 
H&E staining or sirius red-positive area were measured 
using ImageJ software.
Cell culture and chemicals
MIHA cells were cultured in DMEM, high Glucose 
(Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone) and 1% 
penicilin/streptomycin (Beyotime). NCI-H1975 was cul­
tured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum 
and 1% penicilin/streptomycin. AML12 cells were cul­
tured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% 
fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. 
All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Osimer­
tinib (Aladdin, A302849), bafilomycin A1 (TargetMol, 
T6740), rapamycin (TargetMol, T1537), 3-Methyladenine 
(TargetMol, T1879), SAM disulfate tosylate (TargetMol, 
T6752), tiopronin (TargetMol, T1630), silymarin (Tar­
getMol, T6670), glycyrrhizic acid (TargetMol, T2741), 
necrostatin-1 (TargetMol, T1847), Z-VAD-FMK (Tar­
getMol, T89262), acetylcysteine (TargetMol, T0875), 
hydroxychloroquine (TargetMol, T9287), and cyclohexi­
mide (MCE, HY-12320) were dissolved in DMSO, and 
cisplatin (HY-17394, MCE) was dissolved in DMEM for 
cell treatment.

The number of cells seeded in a 96-well plate varied 
depending on cell type. MIHA or AML12 cells were

attachment, the cells were treated with a gradient con­

RNA sequencing and data analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using 
TRIzol (Takara, 9109) according to the manufacturer’s 
protocol. RNA quantity and purity were determined 
using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Nano­
Drop, Wilmington, DE, USA). High-quality samples 
were prepared for RNA sequencing. The RNA-seq tran­
scriptome library was prepared using 1 µg of total RNA 
following the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep Liga­
tion protocol (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Libraries were 
selected for cDNA fragments with a target size of 300 
bp. After quantification, paired-end RNA sequencing 
was performed using a NovaSeq X Plus sequencer with a 
2 × 150 bp read length.
Raw paired-end reads were trimmed and quality-
checked using Fastp with default settings. Next, the 
clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using 
HISAT2 in the orientation mode. For each sample, the 
mapped reads were assembled using StringTie following 
a reference-based approach.
To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 
the expression level of each transcript was calculated 
using the transcript per million reads (TPM) method. 
Gene abundance was quantified using RSEM. Differen­
tial expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 
or DEGseq. Genes with FC ≥ 1.5 and FDR ≤ 0.05 (for 
DESeq2) or FDR ≤ 0.001 (for DEGseq) were considered 
significantly differentially expressed. Additionally, func­
tional enrichment analyses, including GO and Kyoto 
Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), were 
performed to identify DEGs significantly enriched in 
GO terms and metabolic pathways, with a Bonferroni-
corrected P-value ≤ 0.05 compared to the whole tran­
scriptome background. GO summary plots showing 
upregulated and downregulated pathways were plotted 
according to https://toppgene.cchmc.org/ ​e​n​r​i​c​h​m​e​n​t​.​j​s​p​
. GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses were per­
formed using Goatools and KOBAS, respectively.

Quantification and statistical analysis
All quantifications were performed using GraphPad 
Prism 8.0.1 (San Diego, California, www.graphpad.com). 
Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). 
Data represent the mean ± SD of three to five indepen­
dent repeats. Standard sample sizes that were sufficient 
to show differences from the liver injury model were cho­
sen. Statistical significance was determined using two-
tailed Student’s t-tests, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s 
multiple comparisons tests, two-way ANOVA with 
Sidak’s multiple comparisons tests, or the log-rank (Man­
tel-Cox) test, as appropriate. Different levels of statistical 
significance were denoted by P-values (*P < 0.05, ** P < 
0.01, *** P < 0.001).
