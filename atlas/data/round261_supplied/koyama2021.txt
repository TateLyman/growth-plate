Abstract
The growth plates are key engines of skeletal development and growth and contain a top 
reserve zone followed by maturation zones of proliferating, pre-hypertrophic and hypertrophic/
mineralizing chondrocytes. Trauma or drug treatment of certain disorders can derange the 
growth plates and cause accelerated maturation and premature closure, one example being anti-
hedgehog drugs such as LDE225 (Sonidegib) used against pediatric brain malignancies. Here 
we tested whether such acceleration and closure in LDE225-treated mice could be prevented 
by co-administration of a selective retinoid antagonist, based on previous studies showing that 
retinoid antagonists can slow down chondrocyte maturation rates. Treatment of juvenile mice 
with an experimental dose of LDE225 for 2 days (100 mg/kg by gavage) initially caused a 
significant shortening of long bone growth plates, with concomitant decreases in: chondrocyte 
proliferation; expression of Indian hedgehog, Sox9 and other key genes; and surprisingly, the 
number of reserve progenitors. Growth plate involution followed with time leading to impaired 
long bone lengthening. Mechanistically, LDE225 treatment markedly decreased the expression of 
retinoid catabolic enzyme Cyp26b1 within growth plate whereas it increased and broadened the 
expression of retinoid synthesizing enzyme Raldh3, thus subverting normal homeostatic retinoid 
circuitries and in turn accelerating maturation and closure. All such severe skeletal and molecular 
changes were prevented when LDE-treated mice were co-administered the selective retinoid 
antagonist CD2665 (1.5 mg/kg/daily), a drug targeting retinoid acid receptor γ most abundantly

Introduction
The growth plate is the key engine of skeletal development, growth and morphogenesis.
(1,2) Skeletal elements such as femur or tibia in young animals and individuals contain 
a growth plate at each of their proximal and distal ends, while elements such as ribs 
contain a single growth plate at the chondro-osseous junction. Regardless of location, 
growth plates display a stereotypic structure composed of multiple chondrocyte columns 
each of which is distinguishable in a top reserve cell zone followed by proliferative, 
pre-hypertrophic, hypertrophic and mineralizing chondrocyte zones.(1,2) The reserve zone 
contains self-renewing progenitor/stem cells, and recent genetic cell lineage tracing studies 
from our group revealed for the first time that a single progenitor is responsible for 
generating all the chondrocytes present in a column.(3,4) This finding has been confirmed 
in subsequent studies indicating also that reserve zone progenitors are demarcated by 
expression of parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP).(5,6) Chondrocyte behavior and 
function in the remaining zones are regulated by complex mechanisms and interplays 
involving: signaling proteins such as hedgehog, fibroblast growth factor (FGF) and bone 
morphogenetic protein (BMP) family members; transcription factors including Runx2 
and Mef2c; and systemic hormones and other effectors.(2,7–9) One particularly important 
mechanism consists of a regulatory feedback loop between PTHrP expressed in the top zone 
and Indian hedgehog (Ihh) expressed in the pre-hypertrophic zone.(10–13) This relay system 
is critical for establishing and maintaining the rates at which chondrocytes proliferate and 
advance through the subsequent steps of maturation and hypertrophy, thus setting the overall 
pace of growth plate activity and skeletal element elongation.(1)
Trauma or drug treatment of certain pathologies can have adverse side effects on overall 
skeletal growth, can derange growth plate’s regulatory mechanisms and may lead to 
premature growth plate closure.(14,15) For example, children affected by epilepsy are 
commonly treated with drugs such as valproic acid, oxcarbazepine or topiramate that 
can affect the skeleton.(16,17) One study reported that some children receiving valproic 
acid for at least one year exhibited impairment of skeletal growth compared to a control 
group.(18) The authors also tested rat growth plate chondrocytes in vitro and found that 
treatment with valproic acid caused a significant decrease in cell proliferation, leading them 
to conclude that the skeletal growth retardation in the valproic acid-treated epileptic children

was due to a derangement of growth plate chondrocyte proliferation. Pediatric patients 
suffering from medulloblastoma provide another example of drug-induced side effects on 
skeletal growth. About 25% of these cancers are dependent on local hedgehog signaling 
and action for cell growth and survival,(19) and treatment with drug antagonists blocking 
the hedgehog signaling receptor Smoothened such as GDC0449 (vismodegit) and LDE225 
(sonidegib) has proven very effective in reducing tumor growth and enhancing survival of 
medulloblastoma patients.(20–22) However, studies reported that a few of the treated pediatric 
patients displayed skeletal growth retardation and premature growth plate closure, most 
likely caused by interruption of the Ihh-PTHrP relay system and consequent acceleration 
of chondrocyte maturation and hypertrophy.(23,24) The patients exhibited also bending 
of skeletal elements such as the radius as well as bony protrusions, possibly reflecting 
aberration of normal growth plate roles in alignment, orientation and three-dimensional 
sculpting of skeletal elements.(25) It should be underlined that once a growth plate is fully 
closed, there are no means at present to reverse the process and reestablish its function. 
Thus, there is a need to identify possible therapeutics that could preserve the growth plate 
and avoid its premature closure during drug treatment of various pathologies.
These and other considerations led us to consider the retinoid signaling pathway, an 
additional and important growth plate regulatory mechanism in which we have a long-
standing interest. This pathway involves natural ligands such as all-trans-retinoic acid 
(RA) that cells produce in response to physiologic needs and utilizing circulating inert 
precursors such as retinol released from liver storages.(26) Once produced by cytoplasmic 
enzymes, RA and other active retinoids such as 9-cis-RA are transported to the nucleus 
via carrier proteins where they interact with retinoic acid receptors (RARs) bound to 
RA-responsive elements (RAREs) and associated with RXRs, resulting in transcription of 
target genes.(27,28) Notably and importantly, in the absence of active ligands, the RARE-
bound RAR/RXR complexes operate as transcriptional repressors, and this function is 
essential for a variety of physiologic processes as well.(27,29) In previous studies, we and 
others showed that the top zones of growth plate are largely devoid of endogenous active 
retinoids and rely on unliganded RAR repression function, while the lower zones do contain 
retinoids, likely favoring terminal chondrocyte maturation and hypertrophy and transition 
to endochondral bone.(30–32) Of particular relevance to the present study was also our 
finding that when RAR repressor function was pharmacologically enhanced by treatment 
with RAR antagonists (acting as inverse agonists),(33) it further slowed down chondrocyte 
maturation and hypertrophy and transition to bone.(34) These and other considerations raised 
the possibility that co-administration of a retinoid antagonist could be able to slow down 
or even prevent the premature chondrocyte hypertrophy and growth plate closure caused 
by cancer drugs such as LDE225, thus preserving growth plate function and maintaining 
skeletal elongation. The proof-of-principle data reported here provide strong evidence that 
this is in fact the case, at least in mice.

Materials and Methods
Ethics statement regarding mouse studies
All studies involving wild type and transgenic mice were reviewed by our institutional 
IACUC at The Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. All animals were handled, treated and 
cared for according to the approved protocols, and all procedures fully complied with the 
ARRIVE guidelines. CD1 and Pthrp mCherry/+ mice (JAX032872) were obtained from the 
Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA).
Drug administration
Stock solutions of the hedgehog antagonist LDE225 (ChemieTek, Indianapolis, IN, USA) 
were prepared in DMSO at 100 mg/ml final concentration. Stock solutions of the selective 
retinoic acid receptor antagonist CD2665 (Tocris Bioscience, Minneapolis, MN, USA) were 
also prepared in DMSO at 100 mM final concentration. CD2665 has a Ki value of 110 nM 
for RARγ and 306 nM for RARβ.(35) Stocks were subdivided into small aliquots and stored 
at −30°C until use. On days of treatment, a sufficient number of aliquots were thawed and 
diluted with a mixture of DMSO/corn oil mixture (3:7) to working concentration. Mice were 
administered drugs by daily gavage at 100 mg/kg LDE225 and/or 1.6 mg/kg CD2665 in a 
total of 100 to 200 μl/gavage. Control mice were given the same volume of DMSO/corn oil 
mixture.
Sufficient numbers of male and female mice per experiment were placed in separate 
cages and randomized into control and experimental groups to minimize bias. Mice were 
sub-divided into 4 subgroups and treated with LDE225, CD2665, LDE 225/CD2665 
combination or vehicle. Each subgroup contained a minimum of 5 mice per experiment. 
Drug treatment started at 3 weeks of postnatal age (P21). Mice receiving LDE225 only were 
treated on P22 and P23. Mice receiving the combination drug regimen were pre-treated with 
CD2665 on P20 and P21, were given both LDE225 and CD2665 on P22 and P23 and were 
then given a daily dose of CD2665 until end of experiment. Mice receiving CD2265 alone 
were given the drug daily throughout the period used as were companion controls receiving 
daily vehicle administration. Before gavage, mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 1.5% 
isoflurane in 98.5% oxygen to minimize discomfort as prescribed by IACUC. Oral gavage 
probes were 22 gauge and 25 mm long (Instech Laboratories, Plymouth Meeting, PA, USA). 
Following drug administration, animals were regularly monitored for signs of overt toxicity 
including lethargy and dystonia, and Institutional LAS personnel provided additional daily 
animal monitoring and care.

Preparation, treatment and analysis of micromass cultures
Micromass culture were prepared from E11.5 CD-1 mouse embryos limb buds as described.
(38) Briefly, limb buds was dissociated in single cell suspensions with 0.5% trypsin-EDTA 
incubation at 37°C for 20 min. Dissociated cells were re-suspended at a concentration of 
10×106 cell/ml in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics. Micromass 
culture was initiated by spotting 15 μl of the cell suspensions (1.5×105 cells/spot) on the 
surface of 24-well tissue culture plates. After a 2 h incubation at 37°C in a humidified CO2 
incubator to allow for cell attachment, cultures were given 500 μl of medium. After 24 h, 
medium supplemented with LDE225 (5 μM), CD2665 (0.3 nM) or a combination of the 
two was added to the cultures. Fresh reagents were given with medium change every other 
day. Equivalent amount of vehicle (DMSO) was given to control cultures. Cultures were 
stained with alcian blue to detect cartilage nodule formation. Images were taken by a Nikon 
SMZ-U microscope equipment with a SPOT insight camera (Diagnostic Instruments Inc., 
Sterling Heights, MI, USA) and acquired with SPOT 4.0 software. Micromass analysis was 
performed using ImageJ. Images were made binary under an RGB threshold and Particle 
Analysis was utilized to measure alcian blue positive area.
Total RNA was isolated form control and drug treated micromass cultures on day 3 
and 6 using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol and quantified by 
Nanodrop. One microgram total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the Verso cDNA 
kit (Life Technologies: Thermo Scientific, Newark, CA). Quantitative real-time PCR was 
carried out using SYBR Green PCR master mix in an Applied Biosystems 7500 according 
to manufacturer’s protocol. Gapdh was used as endogenous housekeeping control, and 
relative expression level of Sox9, aggrecan (Acan) and Col II was calculated using the ΔΔCt 
method. Multiple samples were analyzed in each experiment, and experiments were repeated 
independently a minimum of 3 times. Sequences of forward and reverse PCR primers used 
are presented in Supplemental Table S1.

RESULTS
Acute growth plate closure in juvenile mice is preventable by drug co-treatment
In order to establish an effective and rapid experimental model of premature growth 
plate closure for our proof-of-principle studies, we used the hedgehog antagonist LDE225 
(sonidegib). The drug is in clinical use for conditions such as basal cell carcinoma and is 
in general safe at currently prescribed doses in adults.(39) As pointed out above, the drug 
has also been found to cause skeletal growth retardation in a small number of pediatric 
medulloblastoma patients(23,24) as well as rapid growth plate closure in juvenile mice when 
given at excess experimental research doses.(40) Thus, 3 week-old male and female CD-1 
mice were administered a daily oral experimental dose of LDE225 at 100 mg/kg in corn 
oil for 2 days total (postnatal days 22 and 23; P22 and P23) (Fig. 1A). Companion mice 
received the same regimen of vehicle (corn oil). After drug administration, the mice were 
allowed to resume activities and did not exhibit obvious changes in behavior or fur coat 
appearance over the duration of the study (see Fig. 2B below). However, histological 
examination of long bones harvested at P24, P27 and P31 (1, 4 and 8 days from the 
last LDE225 administration, respectively) revealed the occurrence of deleterious effects 
on growth plate architecture (Fig. 1). In control mice receiving vehicle, the growth plates 
in tibia or femur displayed their stereotypic organization in which the major resting (re), 
proliferative (pl), pre-hypertrophic (ph) and hypertrophic/mineralizing (hy) zones were 
easily recognizable (Fig. 1B–E). Instead, the tibial growth plate in LDE225-treated mice 
already displayed a reduction in height by P24 and P27 (Fig. 1F–H) and was essentially 
closed by P31 (Fig. 1I). Histomorphometric quantifications showed that the overall growth 
plate height as well as the height of pre-hypertrophic and hypertrophic zones height (Fig. 
1C vs 1G–H, vertical yellow bar) were progressively reduced by drug treatment until full 
closure by P31 (Fig. 1I and R–S).
To determine whether such rapid LDE225-induced growth plate involution could be 
prevented or at least ameliorated by drug co-administration, we tested the efficacy of 
the synthetic RAR antagonist CD2665 that preferentially inhibits RARγ(35) increasing 
RAR repressor function.(33) We chose a selective antagonist rather than a global RAR pan-
antagonist because RARγ is the major RAR family member expressed in growth plate(32,34) 
and because a compound antagonizing all the RARs may have unwanted side effects. As 
above, mice were given a dose of LDE225 at P22 and P23, but were also pre-administered 
the RARγ antagonist at 1.5 mg/kg at P20 and P21 and thereafter daily until P30 (Fig. 
1A). The antagonist dose was chosen based on original mouse effectiveness studies.(41) 
Remarkably, growth plate closure had largely been prevented in LDE225/RARγ antagonist 
co-treated mice compared to those receiving LDE225 only, evident both visually (Fig. 1J–
M) and by histomorphometry (Fig. 1R–S). As expected, companion mice receiving RARγ 
antagonist alone displayed an appreciable delay in chondrocyte maturation and ossification 
indicated by a slight increase in overall growth plate height compared to control at P31

To determine whether the changes in growth plate had repercussions on overall skeletal 
growth and elongation, groups of companion male and female P20 mice receiving vehicle, 
LDE225, RARγ antagonist or LDE225/RARγ antagonist combination exactly as above 
(Fig. 2A) were monitored for body length and body weight over time. The general 
appearance, behavior and fur coat were not overtly different in treated versus control mice of 
either gender during the short time course of these experiments (Fig. 2B–C). However, there 
was a significant decrease in both body length and weight in LDE225-treated versus control 
mice, amounting to about 10–15% decrease by P31 (Fig. 2D–G). Notably, such deficits were 
largely, albeit not completely, rescued by RARγ antagonist co-administration (Fig. 2D–G). 
Whole body X-ray imaging confirmed that tibia length was appreciably reduced by LDE225 
treatment, and this trait also was partially rescued by RARγ antagonist co-treatment (Fig. 
2H–J). The X-ray images showed that the tibia growth plate was open and clearly visible 
in control as well as LDE225/RARγ antagonist co-treated mice, while it was reduced in 
LDE225-treated mice (Fig. 2H, arrowheads in green box insets). All the above skeletal 
parameters were essentially unaffected in companion mice receiving RARγ antagonist 
alone (Fig. 2). Given the paucity of responses, this treatment regimen was not included 
in subsequent experiments.
Growth plate gene expression patterns are rectified by retinoid antagonist co-
administration
The beneficial effects of RARγ antagonist co-administration on growth plate and skeletal 
growth above suggested that the drug could have also normalized the growth plate at 
the phenotypic and molecular levels. To assess this prediction, serial longitudinal sections 
of tibias harvested at P31 from control, LDE225-treated and LDE225/RARγ antagonist 
co-treated mice (Fig. 3A) were processed for in situ hybridization to analyze the expression 
of key marker genes for different growth plate zones.(2) Control growth plates from vehicle-
receiving mice exhibited typical and strong expression of: Sox9 and collagen II (Col II) 
in proliferative and pre-hypertrophic zones; collagen X (Col X) in the hypertrophic zone; 
and osteopontin in mineralizing zone and primary spongiosa (Fig. 3B–E). In comparison, 
expression of Sox9, Col II and Col X was virtually undetectable in tibia sections from 
P31 LDE225-treated mice (Fig. 3F–H), while osteopontin expression was widespread 
and scattered across the bony tissues now occupying the epiphyseal-metaphyseal region 
and replacing the growth plate (Fig. 3I). As clearly shown in Fig. 3J–M, the gene 
expression patterns of Sox9, Col II, Col X and osteopontin had been largely preserved 
in LDE225/RARγ antagonist co-treated mice.

LDE225/RARγ antagonist co-treatment suggested that chondrocyte proliferation might have 
been greatly inhibited in the former condition, but maintained after drug co-treatment. To

As pointed out above, the reserve zone of growth plate contains slow-cycling progenitor 
stem cells that are engaged in cell renewal and also replenish the growth plate over time 
by providing newly committed chondrocytes.(3,4) Reserve zone cells are usually identified 
by a pulse-chase strategy using the thymidine analogue 5-ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine (EdU) 
followed by a protracted chase period.(43) Accordingly, mice received a daily systemic 
administration of EdU by intraperitoneal injection from P5 to P10 (100 mg/kg EdU/
injection) (Fig. 5A). Mice were then chased until P20 by which time EdU incorporated 
in proliferating chondrocytes would be expected to largely dissipate through ongoing 
mitotic activity, while EdU incorporated into slow cycling reserve progenitors would still 
be detectable. The P20 mice were then subdivided into 3 groups, received vehicle, LDE225 
or LDE225/RARγ antagonist combination starting at P21 exactly as above, and were then 
sacrificed at P27. Immunohistochemistry did show that EdU-labeled cells were present and 
largely confined to the reserve zone of control tibial growth plate (Fig. 5B–C, bracket). 
Their number was greatly diminished in the growth plate in LDE225-treated mice (Fig. 
5E–F), but had been preserved in LDE225/RARγ antagonist co-treated mice (Fig. 5H–I, 
bracket). On average, the top zone contained an average of 15–17 labeled cells/5 randomly

fields in LDE225-treated mice and maintained at about 10–11 cells/5 fields in co-treated 
mice (Fig. 5K). To verify these findings, we monitored skeletal progenitors in the reserve 
zone of growth plate by virtue of their Pthrp expression.(6) Thus, Pthrp mCherry/+ knock-in 
mice (Pthrp reporter mice) were subdivided into 3 groups that received vehicle, LDE225 or 
LDE225/RARγ antagonist combination exactly as above. Prhrp reporter-positive cells were 
largely confined to the reserve zone of control tibial growth plate (Fig. 5D). Their number

preserved in LDE225/RARγ antagonist co-treated mice (Fig. 5J). There were an average of

maintained at about 8–11 cells/5 fields in co-treated mice (Fig. 5L).

vehicle, LDE225 treatment or LDE225/RARγ antagonist co-treatment as above and their 
hindlimbs were harvested at P24 and P27 and processed for in situ hybridization, using 
serial longitudinal tibia sections (Fig. 6A). We initially analyzed all family members 
Raldh1, Raldh2 and Raldh3(44) and Cyp26a1, Cyp26b1 and Cyp26c1(45), but found that 
the postnatal mouse growth plates mainly expressed Raldh3 and Cyp26b1. Cyp26b1 
expression characterized the upper zones of control P24 and P27 growth plates (Fig. 6B, N), 
whereas Radlh3 expression was restricted to the hypertrophic zone (Fig. 6C, O) overlapping 
collagen X expression (Fig. 6D–E and P–Q). In P24 growth plates from LDE225-treated 
mice, Cyp26b1 expression was sharply decreased (Fig. 6F), while Raldh3 expression was 
increased and much broader, overlapping the thicker Col X-expressing hypertrophic zone 
(Fig. 6F, H–I, vertical brackets). By P27, expression of Cyp26b1 and Raldh3 was barely 
appreciable given the dramatic involution of growth plate by then (Fig. 6R, S), with just a 
few Col X-expressing chondrocytes remaining visible (Fig. 6T–U). It was very interesting 
to observe that nearly normal patterns of Cyp26b1, Raldh3 and Col X gene expression had 
been preserved in the growth plates of LDE225/RARγ antagonist co-treated mice at either 
time point (Fig. 6J–M and V–Y).
Chondrogenic cells respond directly to drug treatment and co-treatment
The ability of the RARγ antagonist to prevent growth plate closure caused by LDE225 is 
likely due to direct action of the drugs on chondrocytes, though it could also entail indirect 
systemic mechanisms. Previously we showed that another anti-hedgehog signaling drug 
acted directly on chondrogenic cells in vitro and suppressed their differentiation,(38) whereas 
treatment with RAR antagonists boosted it.(46) Thus, we asked whether LDE225 and RARγ 
antagonist exerted similar anti- and pro-chondrogenic action, respectively and whether co-
treatment counteracted LDE225 effects. Accordingly, primary limb bud mesenchymal cells 
isolated from E11.5 mouse embryos were reared in high density micromass cultures in 
standard medium or medium containing LDE225 or drug combination. Fresh drugs were 
added daily along with a medium change, and effects were analyzed after 4 to 6 days. In 
control vehicle-receiving cultures, many cartilage nodules had formed by day 6 and were 
appreciable after staining with alcian blue (Fig. 7A). Treatment with LDE225 or RARγ 
antagonist did result in sharp decreases and increases in nodule formation and alcian blue 
levels, respectively (Fig. 7B, E). Such opposing responses were paralleled by declines 
or increases in expression of chondrogenic marker genes Sox9, aggrecan and Col2a1 as 
revealed by qPCR (Fig. 7F–H). Indeed, co-treatment with RARγ antagonist counteracted the 
LDE225 effects, and the co-treated cells displayed normalized levels of cartilage nodules, 
alcian blue staining levels and expression of chondrogenic markers (Fig. 7D–H). To gain 
insights into mechanisms of phenotypic expression, we extended the qPCR analyses to

Discussion
The data we present here provide clear and novel evidence that systemic co-administration 
of a retinoid antagonist can prevent the deleterious effects on growth plates caused by 
experimental doses of LDE225. The co-treatment allows the growth plates to maintain their 
morphological organization, phenotypic expression and contribution to skeletal elongation, 
at least within the time frames studied. As pointed out above, LDE225 and other 
similar drugs are eliciting measures of therapeutic success against malignancies such as 
hedgehog-dependent pediatric medulloblastomas that are extremely severe and usually fatal 
pathologies.(20–22) Given the fact that the hedgehog signaling pathway regulates a very large 
number of developmental and physiologic processes including growth plate functioning,(50) 
it is not at all surprising that anti-hedgehog drugs could have unwanted side effects in a few 
of the treated patients. While data from mouse studies may or may not necessarily apply 
to patients, our present proof-of-principle data are encouraging and provide support for a 
possible drug combination strategy(51) to prevent or at least reduce side effects on growth 
plate caused by hedgehog cancer drugs.
One of the most dramatic responses observed in our study is the rapidity with which the long 
bone growth plates underwent precocious closure after the start of LDE225 treatment at the 
doses used. Original cell kinetics studies revealed that the average half-life of chondrocytes 
in the growth plate is 4 to 6 days,(52,53) corresponding to the time period required for 
chondrocytes in the proliferative zone to transit through the pre-hypertrophic, hypertrophic 
and mineralizing zones before undergoing involution or participating in primary spongiosa 
formation.(54) Differences in chondrocyte kinetics were noted in the proximal vs distal 
growth plates of long bones,(55) but they are not dramatic and still point to the overall 
ephemeral nature of the cells. These and other studies provide an explanation as to why the 
overall height of growth plates was already reduced after a few days of LDE225 treatment, 
delineating how dependent the growth plate must normally be on continuous hedgehog 
signaling. Given the known essential roles of the Ihh-PTHrP signaling loop in determining 
the rates of proliferation and maturation of growth plate chondrocytes, it is likely then that

LDE225 interference with such loop would have caused a rapid depletion of proliferating 
chondrocytes and rapid involution of the growth plate. But what could explain the observed 
rapid depletion of EdU-labeled slow cycling reserve progenitors and what could account for 
the ability of the RARγ antagonist to rescue them?
The presence of reserve progenitors in the growth plate has been known for quite some 
time(56,57), but only recently have studies begun to define their phenotypic characteristics 
and regulatory mechanisms, including Pthrp expression itself and Tsc1 signaling.(5,6) 
Tellingly, one of these studies showed that conditional ablation of Pthrp-expressing resting 
cells by a diphtheria toxin fragment A (DTA) strategy altered the growth plate architecture 
and caused premature chondrocyte hypertrophy.(6) Based on our data after LDE225 
treatment, we can now add hedgehog signaling as another critical pathway to maintain slow 
cycling cells in the resting zone. Thus, the rapid involution of growth plate during LDE225 
administration was likely caused not only by a decrease in mitotic activity in proliferative 
zone, but also a failure to maintain resting cells and replenish the growth plate. Given that 
the RARγ antagonist was able to fully counter the deleterious effects of LDE225 and that 
the top zones of growth plate are normally devoid of endogenous retinoids,(31) it appears 
that unliganded RAR repression function also is very important if not critical for behavior 
and phenotype of both resting cells and proliferative chondrocytes and can compensate for 
LDE225-induced hedgehog signaling deficiency.
These interpretations are in line with additional data here and previous studies showing that 
endogenous retinoids are kept under strict spatiotemporal control to sustain normal skeletal 
development and growth plate function.(30,31,58) In agreement with our data, Minegishi et 
al. showed that Cyp26b1 is selectively expressed in the upper zones of growth plate to 
keep endogenous retinoids at very low levels and that its conditional ablation in cartilage 
caused a significant drop in chondrocyte proliferation and derangement of growth plate 
zonal organization.(30) We reported a similar drop in chondrocyte proliferation and abnormal 
growth plate structure and functioning in mice in which we conditionally deleted RARα 
and RARγ in cartilage.(32) The drop in Cyp26b1 expression following LDE225 treatment 
combined with broader and higher Raldh3 expression observed here is thus likely to have 
caused a major overall increase in -and broader distribution of-active retinoids, deranging 
growth plate homeostasis, accelerating maturation and hypertrophy and ultimately causing 
precocious closure (see our model in Fig. 8). Relevantly also, it was recently found 
that hedgehog signaling is normally needed for embryonic expression of Cyp26 family 
members(59), suggesting that the same may occur in growth plate and that LDE225 could 
have acted directly to down-regulate Cyp26b1 expression. Together, the studies support 
the overall notion that in addition to hedgehog signaling and other mechanisms(2), the 
retinoid pathway is intimately interwoven into growth plate biology, contributes to fine-
tune its activities and roles, and can have major phenotypic effects once genetically or 
pharmacologically modulated. We should note that mutual interactions between the retinoid 
and hedgehog pathways have been demonstrated in a number of developmental processes 
and systems, with interactions being negative or positive depending on biological context 
and developmental stage.(59–62) Within the growth plate, the specific retinoid action is 
dictated by lack or presence of ligands as indicated above, thus allowing this pathway to 
exert distinct zone-specific effects in relation to other pathways -including the hedgehog

pathway- and contribute to the regulation of overall growth plate physiology.(63) Our in

coordinating both chondrogenic cell differentiation and chondrocyte terminal maturation. 
The complex phenotypes displayed by the cells during drug co-treatment -such as low 
Mef2c expression and high Ihh expression compared to control- indicate that chondrogenic 
cells can modulate those pathways in independent manners to adjust to given circumstances. 
It remains to determined whether similar subtle changes in regulatory interactions between 
these pathways occur in vivo and could adjust the responses of growth plate and the skeleton 
in general to diverse normal and pathological conditions.
Many complex diseases including cancer and musculoskeletal disorders involve changes 
in a spectrum of cellular and molecular regulatory mechanisms.(64) This is of course 
not surprising given the multitude of cell-cell and tissue-tissue interactions and local 
and systemic interplays that govern physiology and the fact that disease culprits can 
affect different tissues and organs in distinct manners. As elaborated in a recent incisive 
review, many such diseases are currently treated with single-target monotherapy, and drug 
development programs are often directed on identifying mono-therapeutic regimens.(51) To 
increase effectiveness, a number of combination drug therapies have been conceived, tested 
and implemented in several areas of clinical medicine, with measures of success particularly 
in cancer.(51) By definition, combination therapies concurrently target distinct pathogenic 
pathways, could be efficacious at drug doses lower than respective monotherapies, could 
even be adjusted to meet patient-to-patient variability, or could address unwanted side 
effects. The latter describes the drug combination in our present proof-of-principle study. 
For such co-treatment to work, it would of course be necessary to establish that the RARγ 
antagonist does not in itself affect the pharmacokinetics of LDE225 and its efficacy on 
targeted cancer cells. Notably, previous studies described beneficial therapeutic effects of 
other retinoid antagonists against malignant cells including breast and prostate cancer cells.
(65–67) Thus, it is possible that a combined LDE225/ RARγ antagonist regimen may be more 
efficacious against certain cancers. Future research is needed to address these and related 
questions, but our data do identify intriguing therapeutic possibilities for the future.

Fig. 8. 
Working model of growth plate mechanisms operating in control and drug-treated mice. 
(A) In control conditions, the Ihh-PTHrP axis sets the rates of chondrocyte proliferation 
and maturation within upper growth plate zones that express Cyp26s, contain minimal 
endogenous retinoids in a downward gradient (green triangle) and are regulated by 
unliganded RARs exerting repressor function. Transition to hypertrophy would activate 
Raldh expression and production of biologically active retinoids and mainly all-trans-
retinoic acid (RA), creating an upward gradient (red triangle) and enabling liganded 
RARs to exert transcriptional activity and promote terminal chondrocyte maturation 
and transition to endochondral bone. Retinoid action and Ihh signaling would normally 
interact, balance and restrain each other, maintaining a physiologic dynamic boundary 
between upper zones and hypertrophic zone. (B) By interfering with hedgehog signaling, 
LDE225 would destabilize growth plate homeostasis by directly or indirectly favoring 
Raldh expression, expanding the levels, distribution and action of RA and other active 
retinoids. In turn, this would dampen the Ihh-PTHrP axis, narrow the upper zones, accelerate 
chondrocyte maturation and hypertrophy and ultimately cause growth plate closure. (C) 
Co-treatment with RARγ antagonist would largely counter the acceleration of hypertrophy 
and ossification, restore more normal Ihh and Pthrp expression and reciprocal restraining 
interactions amongst the zones, and allow the growth plate to maintain organization and 
function. Rz, pl, ph and hy: resting, proliferative, pre-hypertrophic and hypertrophic zones, 
respectively; GP, growth plate.
