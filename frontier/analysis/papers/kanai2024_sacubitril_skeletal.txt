Abstract C-type natriuretic peptide (CNP) plays a crucial role in enhancing endochondral bone growth and holds promise as a therapeutic agent for impaired skeletal growth. To overcome CNP’s short half-life, we explored the potential of dampening its clearance system. Neprilysin (NEP) is an endopeptidase responsible for catalyzing the degradation of CNP. Thus, we investigated the effects of NEP inhibition on skeletal growth by administering sacubitril, a NEP inhibitor, to C57BL/6 mice. Remarkably, we observed a dose-dependent skeletal overgrowth phenotype in mice treated with sacubitril. Histological analysis of the growth plate revealed a thickening of the hypertrophic and proliferative zones, mirroring the changes induced by CNP administration. The promotion of skeletal growth observed in wild-type mice treated with sacubitril was nullified by the knockout of cartilage-specific natriuretic peptide receptor B (NPR-B). Notably, sacubitril promoted skeletal growth in mice only at 3 to 4 weeks of age, a period when endogenous CNP and NEP expression was higher in the lumbar vertebrae. Additionally, sacubitril facilitated endochondral bone growth in organ culture experiments using tibial explants from fetal mice. These findings suggest that NEP inhibition significantly promotes skeletal growth via the CNP/NPR-B pathway, warranting further investigations for potential applications in people with short stature. C-type natriuretic peptide (CNP) is the third member of the natriuretic peptide family, along with atrial natriuretic peptide and brain natriuretic peptide, and acts as a selective ligand for natriuretic peptide receptor-B (NPR-B) (1, 2). Past studies re­ vealed that CNP is a potent stimulator of endochondral bone growth using knockout rodents (3-5) and transgenic mice (6, 7). In humans, loss or gain of function mutations of the CNP/NPR-B system caused impaired skeletal growth (8-14) or skeletal overgrowth (15-20), respectively. Moreover, CNP is expected to act as a therapeutic reagent for the impaired skel­ etal growth observed in achondroplasia (6, 21-23), mucopoly­ saccharidoses (24, 25), or glucocorticoid-induced retardation (26, 27). In fact, vosoritide, a biological analog of CNP, was approved as the first therapeutic drug for impaired skeletal growth observed in achondroplasia in a clinical setting (23). As is often the case with other peptide hormones, CNP has a short half-life because of its clearing system (28, 29). NPR-C, known as a clearing receptor of CNP, reduces CNP in the extracellular environment via a receptor-mediated internal­ ization and degradation process (30, 31). We previously re­ ported that mice overexpressing osteocrin, the inhibitory ligand for NPR-C, exhibit a skeletal overgrowth phenotype by enhancing the CNP/NPR-B pathway (32). Therefore, acti­ vating CNP/NPR-B signaling by modifying the clearance sys­ tem of CNP is a promising strategy for the treatment of short stature. In addition to clearance via NPR-C, CNP is also inactivated via cleavage by neprilysin (NEP), also known as neutral endo­ peptidase (30, 31). NEP is a zinc-activated endopeptidase that catalyses the degradation of a number of peptides of up to 40 to 50 amino acids, including natriuretic peptides (33). NEP cleaves CNP initially at the ring compartment and subse­ quently at other sites. Indeed, NEP inhibitors elevate CNP and NT-proCNP, its amino-terminal molecular precursor, in humans (34) and animal models (35). Recently, sacubitril, which inhibits NEP activity, combined with the angiotensin II type 1 receptor antagonist was used in the treatment of chronic heart failure in adults (36, 37). However, during growth periods, little is known about the effect of NEP inhib­ itors on bone elongation. In the present study, we administered sacubitril to mice in order to investigate its impact on skeletal growth. Additionally, we administered sacubitril to cartilage- specific NPR-B deficient mice to validate that the effects of sa­ cubitril on bone growth were indeed nullified. Furthermore, we conducted ex vivo experiments using tibial explants from fetal mice to elucidate the direct influence of sacubitril on en­ dochondral bone growth. Our findings reveal that sacubitril promotes skeletal growth in mice through the local CNP/ NPR-B pathway. Materials and Methods Animals Three-week-old male and female C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan). Cartilage-specific NPR-B deficient mice with C57BL/6J back­ grounds were generated as previously described (38). Briefly, Col2a1-Cre mice (39), where the Cre recombinase is activated under the control of the collagen type 2α1 gene promoter, were bred with Npr2flox/flox mice. We designed primers for detecting the Col2a1-Cre transgene and the floxed Npr2 gene in this study. Genotyping was performed by PCR. The following pri­ mer pairs were used. Cre forward: 5′-TCCAATTTACTGAC CGTACACCAA-3′, Cre reverse 5′-CCTGATCCTGGCAAT TTCGGCTA-3′; Npr2 floxdel allele forward: 5′-GTAAC CTGGGTAGACTAGTTGTTGG-3′, Npr2 floxdel allele reverse: 5′-ATGGTGGAGGAGGTCTTTAATTCC-3′. The animals were housed in a humidity- and temperature- controlled environment with an automatic 12-hour light/ dark cycle. They were fed a standard pelleted lab chow diet. Surgical procedures were performed under isoflurane-induced anesthesia, and carbon dioxide was used for euthanasia. All efforts were made to minimize suffering. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Research Committee, Kyoto University Graduate School of Medicine (permit number: MedKyo07598). Care of animals and all animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines of the Kyoto University Graduate School of Medicine and the United States National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Administration of Reagents to Mice For administration to mice, we purchased sacubitril calcium (Toronto Research Chemicals, Toronto, Ontario, Canada) and CNP-53 (PEPTIDE INSTITUTE, Ibaraki, Japan). Sacubitril calcium was dissolved with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and administered to mice via drinking water from 3 or 4 weeks of age. As vehicle, an equal volume of DMSO was administered. The final concentration of sacubitril in drinking water was 0.025%, 0.05%, and 0.1%. The final con­ centration of DMSO was 0.5%. CNP was injected subcutane­ ously at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day from 3 weeks of age. The dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day was determined in accordance with our previous report in which the dose of CNP-53 restored the dwarfism of CNP-deficient rats (40). As vehicle, water was injected, and the volume of the vehicle was equivalent to that of the CNP solution. Growth Curve and Skeletal Analysis Naso-anal length and body weight were measured under anes­ thesia with isoflurane every week. At the end of the experi­ ment, the anteroposterior and transverse diameters of the cranial bone and the lengths of the humerus, radius, ulna, fe­ mur, tibia, and lumbar vertebrae of each mouse were meas­ ured on soft X-ray film. Lengths of the humerus, radius, ulna, femur, and tibia were averages of the right and the left. We measured the span from the first lumbar vertebra to the fifth as the length of the lumbar vertebrae. Histological Analysis The tibias and femurs of the treated mice were fixed in 10% formaldehyde neutral buffer solution (Nacalai tesque, Kyoto, Japan) and decalcified with 10% ethylenediaminetetra­ acetic acid for 2 weeks before they were embedded in paraffin and sagittally sliced to evaluate their growth plates. Alcian blue staining was performed for morphologic analyses. The width of the growth plate was measured for each specimen. RNA Extraction and Quantitative Reverse Transcription-PCR Analysis Three-week-old and 5-week-old C57BL/6J male mice were sacrificed, and their lumbar vertebrae were resected. Total RNA of lumbar vertebrae was extracted using RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands) and then was reverse-transcribed using ReverTra Ace (TOYOBO Life Science, Osaka, Japan). Quantitative PCR analyses using 20 ng of total RNA were performed using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR MIX (TOYOBO Life Science) with the StepOnePlus™ Real-time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Results were normalized us­ ing Ppia as reference genes and quantified messenger RNA (mRNA) expression of Nppc and Mme by comparison with that of cerebrum or kidney, respectively. The primers used in this analysis were as follows. Nppc forward: 5′-CATGAGCG GTCTGGGATGTTA-3′, Nppc reverse: 5′-ATTGCGTTGGA GGTGTTTCC-3′; Mme forward: 5′-TGCTCGTCTTGCTC CTGAC-3′, Mme reverse: 5′- ATTGCGTTTCAACCAGCC −3′; Ppia forward: 5′-CGCGTCTCCTTCGAGCTGTTTG-3′, Ppia reverse: 5′-TGTAAAGTCACCACCCTGGCACAT-3′. Organ Culture On day 16.5 of pregnancy, wild-type ICR mice were sacrificed and tibias were resected from their fetal mice. These bones were cultured in minimum essential medium Eagle alpha modification (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan) containing 5 μg/mL ascorbic acid (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 1 mM β-glycerophosphate pentahydrate (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), antibiotic-antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 0.2% bovine serum albumin (Nacalai tesque). The tibias were incubated at 37°C in a hu­ midified atmosphere of 5% CO2 with vehicle, 0.1 or 1 μM of sacubitril, and 0.1 μM of CNP. The medium was ex­ changed every day. The length of each tibia was measured by a light microscope with a linear ocular scale at the start and at the end of the culture period. Histological analysis of each tibia was performed at the end of the experimental peri­ od. Preparation of the specimen and alcian-blue staining were performed by the method described previously. Results Effects of NEP Inhibitor on Bone Growth To determine whether NEP inhibitor promotes skeletal growth, C57BL6/J male mice were treated with 0.1%, 0.05%, or 0.025% sacubitril or vehicle in free drinking water for 4 weeks. We started the administration at 3 weeks of age. Since there was no difference between the 0.1% sacubitril group and the 0.05% sacubitril group during the first week (0.18 ± 0.01 cm/day in 0.1% sacubitril group and 0.19 ± 0.007 cm/day in 0.05% sacubitril group), the experiment was continued only in the 0.05% sacubitril group and 0.025 sacubitril group. There were no significant differences in the naso-anal length between 3 groups at the start of this study. The growth curves showed that sacubitril significantly elon­ gated naso-anal lengths of mice at 7 weeks of age in a dose- dependent manner compared to the vehicle group: 103% in the 0.05% sacubitril group (P < .001) and 102% in the 0.025% sacubitril group (P < .02) (Fig. 1A and 1B). The growth velocity of the mice treated with 0.05% sacubitril was significantly higher than that of the mice treated with ve­ hicle only at 3 to 4 weeks of age (P < .01), with no difference at 4 to 7 weeks (Fig. 1C). Meanwhile, the body weights of the 3 groups were not significantly different at each week of the study (Fig. 1D). Analyses of soft X-ray images at 7 weeks of age revealed that lengths of the anteroposterior diameter of the cranium (P < .01) and lumbar vertebrae (P < .001) in mice treated with sacubitril were significantly lengthened com­ pared with mice treated with vehicle in a dose-dependent man­ ner, but there were no differences in bone lengths of the front limbs and hind limbs (Fig. 1E). On the basis of these results, we adopted 0.05% sacubitril in the following experiments. Because a significant increase in naso-anal length was ob­ served only at 3 to 4 weeks during the 4-week treatment of sa­ cubitril, we treated female mice with sacubitril or vehicle for 1 week from 3 weeks of age to confirm the effects of sacubitril on bone growth. Naso-anal lengths of mice treated with sacubitril were significantly longer than those of mice treated with vehicle (P < .01), and there were no differences in body weight (Fig. 1F and 1G). Soft X-ray images of female mice also showed signifi­ cantly longer lengths of anteroposterior diameter of the cra­ nium (P < .05) and lumbar vertebrae (P < .01) in mice treated with sacubitril than those treated with vehicle. Therefore, skeletal growth observed in mice treated with sacubitril was statistically significant and reproducible in both sexes. Comparison of the Effects Between NEP Inhibitor and CNP on Bone Growth To investigate whether the effects of NEP inhibitor on bone growth are mediated by the CNP/NPR-B pathway, we treated male C57BL/6 mice at 3 weeks of age with NEP inhibitor and CNP for 2 weeks. According to the treatment, we divided the mice into 4 groups. The first group was composed of mice treated with sacubitril and water as a vehicle for CNP (sacubi­ tril/vehicle group). The second was the control group com­ posed of mice treated with DMSO and water as vehicles for sacubitril and CNP, respectively (vehicle/vehicle group). The third was composed of mice treated with DMSO as a vehicle for sacubitril and CNP (vehicle/CNP group). The last was composed of mice treated with sacubitril and CNP (sacubi­ tril/CNP group). Growth curves are shown in Fig. 2A. Compared to the ve­ hicle/vehicle group, final naso-anal lengths of the sacubitril/ vehicle group (102%, P < .05) and vehicle/CNP group (104%, P < .001) were significantly longer. There was no addi­ tive effect for those in the sacubitril/CNP group (Fig. 2B). The body weights of the 4 groups were not significantly different (Fig. 2C). To clarify whether the effects of sacubitril on the growth plate were similar to that of CNP, we elucidated the histological structures of tibial growth plates of mice at 4 weeks of age. Representative images stained by alcian blue are shown in Fig. 2D. As shown in the figure, the whole growth plate including proliferative and hypertrophic zones of the ve­ hicle/CNP group was significantly thicker than that of the ve­ hicle/vehicle group, similar to those observed in our previous study (24, 26). Importantly, growth plates of the sacubitril/ve­ hicle group also showed similar changes. Sacubitril treatment significantly thickened the growth plate including proliferative and hypertrophic zones (Fig. 2E and 2F). There was no addi­ tive effect for the width of growth plates in the sacubitril/ CNP group. In summary, sacubitril and CNP treatment stimu­ lated bone elongation in mice by similar mechanisms such as thickening the hypertrophic and proliferative zones. Administration of NEP Inhibitor to NPR-B Deficient Mice To further investigate whether the effects of sacubitril are mediated through the CNP/NPR-B pathway, we administered sacubitril to cartilage-specific NPR-B deficient mice (Col2a1-Cre; Npr2flox/flox) and their control counterparts (Npr2flox/flox). We initiated administration at 3 weeks of age and continued for 2 weeks. Similar to the wild-type C57BL/6 mice, a significant increase in body length was observed in Npr2flox/flox mice (Fig. 3A-3C). As expected, the growth curves showed that sacubitril administration did not result in significant differences in naso-anal lengths of Col2a1-Cre; Npr2flox/flox mice: 100% in Col2a1-Cre; Npr2flox/flox/ sacubitril group compared to Col2a1-Cre; Npr2flox/flox/vehicle group (P = .392) (Fig. 3D and 3E). Similarly, the growth velocities and body weights of Col2a1-Cre; Npr2flox/flox mice were not affected by sacubitril (Fig. 3F and 3G). Furthermore, the analysis of soft X-ray images at 5 weeks of age revealed that bone lengths, including the anteroposterior diameter of the cranium and lumbar vertebrae, were not al­ tered in Col2a1-Cre; Npr2flox/flox mice treated with sacubitril. The Efficient Periods of NEP Inhibitor on Bone Growth Although the effect of sacubitril on bone elongation was observed only during 3 to 4 weeks of age (Figs. 1A and 2A), long-term treatment of sacubitril did not diminish the antihy­ pertensive effect on blood pressure (41) and the increased blood levels of atrial natriuretic peptide (42) as in previous re­ ports. Thus, we speculated that the effect of sacubitril was not attenuated by long-term administration and that the effect on bone elongation occurred more strongly in younger ages with more dynamic states of growth. To verify this hypothesis, we treated mice with sacubitril for 1 week from 3 weeks of age (sa­ cubitril 3–4 group) or 4 weeks of age (sacubitril 4–5 group) and compared them with mice treated with vehicle (vehicle group). Growth curves are shown in Fig. 4A. As expected, com­ pared to the vehicle group, the final naso-anal lengths of the sacubitril 3–4 group were significantly longer (103%, P < .05), but those of the sacubitril 4–5 group were not changed (99%, P = .717) (Fig. 4B). The growth velocities of the sacubitril 3–4 group mice were significantly higher than those of vehicle group mice only during 3 to 4 weeks of age (P < .05) (Fig. 4C). The growth velocity of the sacubitril 4–5 group did not show any difference. The body weights of the 3 groups were not significantly different at each week of the study (Fig. 4D). Discussion In the present study, we have demonstrated that sacubitril, a NEP inhibitor, significantly promoted the skeletal growth of C57BL/6 mice. Histological analyses of tibial growth plates revealed that growth plates, including proliferative and hyper­ trophic zones, of mice treated with sacubitril were significant­ ly thickened. The changes in the growth plate due to treatment with sacubitril were similar to those with CNP treatment. Sacubitril promoted skeletal growth in mice only during 3 to 4 weeks of age, when Nppc and Mme expressions were higher in lumbar vertebrae. The promotion of skeletal growth ob­ served in wild-type mice treated with sacubitril was abrogated by the cartilage-specific deletion of NPR-B. In addition, sacu­ bitril also promoted endochondral bone growth in organ cul­ ture experiments using tibial explants from fetal mice by thickening the hypertrophic layer of the growth plate similar to CNP. Analysis of intracellular signaling showed that NEP inhibited phosphorylation of ERK as well as CNP. These re­ sults suggested that sacubitril enhances the action of the CNP/NPR-B pathway, thereby promoting skeletal growth. Although NEP-deficient mice were associated with lower blood pressure (44), accumulation of amyloid beta in the brain (45-47), and age-related obesity (48) in previous re­ ports, there were no studies on skeletal growth. Sacubitril, a NEP inhibitor, has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration as a treatment for hypertension and chronic heart failure in humans only for use in adults. As discussed earlier, NEP-deficient mice exhibit various phenotypes, indicating that NEP affects a variety of tissues other than growth plates. The off-target effects of sacubitril on tissues other than growth plate should be considered. Although the cartilage-specific deletion of NPR-B have suc­ cessfully shown that sacubitril promotes bone growth via the CNP/NPR-B pathway, it remains possible that NEP inhib­ ition in tissues other than cartilage affects bone elongation. Thus, we have conducted tibial organ culture experiments and found that sacubitril promotes endochondral bone growth directly. The absence of increased bone elongation in the limb may be attributed to the fact that sacubitril is effective only during younger periods when the expressions of CNP and NEP are high. Indeed, in tibial organ cultures of fetal mice, the bone lengths of tibias incubated with sacubitril were significantly longer than those with vehicle. There is potential for limb length increase in in vivo experiments if sacubitril is adminis­ tered earlier than 3 weeks of age. However, due to the limita­ tion of administering sacubitril via drinking water, we were unable to provide it to mice before weaning, which represents a limitation in our oral administration experiments. We previ­ ously confirmed elongation of long bone by CNP administra­ tion for 4 weeks (27). Sacubitil may not have a sufficient effect on long bones because the effect is only present for a week. It may take a long time to achieve macroscopic long bone elongation. In the present study, coadministration of CNP and NEP inhibitor did not show any synergistic effects. There are 2 possible reasons for the lack of synergistic effect of coadmi­ nistration of sacubitril and CNP. The first is that the CNP used in this experiment was CNP-53, the 53-amino acid form of CNP. CNP-53 has a higher resistance to NEP than CNP-22, the 22-amino acid form of CNP (50). The lack of an additive effect with sacubitril and CNP-53 would indicate a strong NEP resistance of CNP-53. However, this experi­ ment does not directly measure the NEP resistance of CNP-53. While NEP resistance would be a possible reason for the lack of synergistic effect, we also consider another rea­ son. The second reason is that local endogenous CNP expres­ sion was suppressed by exogenous CNP. The enzymatic activity of NEP in vivo is believed to be attributed mostly to the tissue-associated isoform of NEP (51, 52), and we previ­ ously revealed CNP was a local regulator of the growth plate cartilage using cartilage-specific CNP or NPR-B defi­ cient mice (38). In fact, in the present study, the promotion of skeletal growth observed in wild-type mice treated with sa­ cubitril was nullified by the knockout of cartilage-specific NPR-B. On the basis of these results, the bone growth in­ duced by NEP inhibition is suggested to be a result of its local action. We previously reported that the systemic administra­ tion of CNP reduced endogenous CNP expression in growth plates in vivo (53). Therefore, when treated with CNP, the ex­ ogenous CNP, not endogenous local CNP, mainly acts on bone elongation, and the suppression of endogenous CNP levels by exogenous CNP administration potentially reduced the efficacy of the NEP inhibitor. GH is a current therapeutic agent used in clinical settings to treat short stature of patients with and without disorders of the GH axis such as Turner syndrome, Prader-Willi syn­ drome, and Noonan syndrome (54, 55). For GH-resistant short stature, clinical use of CNP analogue has already begun in achondroplasia, and it is anticipated that its applications will expand to include glucocorticoid-induced retardation and mucopolysaccharidosis. Both GH and CNP, reagents cur­ rently used for short stature, require injections to show their growth effects. However, the NEP inhibitor, which has dem­ onstrated promotion of bone growth effects in this study, is an oral medication. This presents a potential advantage, as it may have a lower initiation barrier and be more user- friendly compared to injectable formulations. Another advantage of sacubitril is an accumulation of clin­ ical data. Sacubitril is widely used in clinical settings as sacu­ bitril/valsartan, and a large amount of clinical data on sacubitril/valsartan ensures its safety. Although little informa­ tion is available on sacubitril administration alone, past re­ ports of NEP-deficient mice suggest that hypotension, amyloid beta accumulation, and obesity are possible side ef­ fects (44-48). Sacubitril has been reported to reduce systolic blood pressure by 4.2 mmHg (56). There are no reports on de­ mentia or obesity with sacubitril alone, but as sacubitril/val­ sartan it has been reported not to increase dementia (57) and to improve insulin resistance without increasing body weight (58). In conclusion, we have demonstrated that NEP inhibition significantly promotes the skeletal growth of C57BL/6 mice via the CNP/NPR-B pathway in vivo and ex vivo. Whether our results could be applied to disease models, such as achon­ droplasia, is a very important matter, and further research is needed in the future.
