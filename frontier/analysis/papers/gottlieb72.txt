Tissue Gold Concentration in a Rheumatoid Arthritic 
Receiving Chrysotherapy 
Norman L. Gottlieb, Patricia M. Smith and Edward M. Smith 
Postmortem specimens from a patient with rheumatoid arthritis who had re- 
ceived 5 g of aurothioglucose were examined histologically, and their gold con- 
tent was determined by activation analysis. The highest concentration of gold 
was found in the lymph nodes followed by the adrenal gland, renal cortex and 
other organs of the reticuloendothelial system. Comparatively low concentrations 
were found in tissues comprising the joint structure. Organs in which the great- 
est quantities of gold were stored include the bone marrow, liver, skin and 
bone. These findings demonstrate the body's capacity to store relatively large 
quantities of gold without adverse reaction, support the concept of a systemic 
rather than local mechanism of action (at the joint level) and demonstrate in 
vivo gold concentrations in the range expected to produce local cellular biologic 
effects.

and in the urine and feces of human and 
experimental animal models (2-6). T' issue

rheumatoid arthritis, by the ARA criteria. of 16 
years duration who died in November 1969, of an 
overdose of barbiturate. When first seen in the 
Arthritis Clinic at Jackson Memorial Hospital in 
June 1958, she related a 5-year history of progres- 
sive polyarthritis with morning stiffness. Physical 
examination showed active synovitis of metacarpal 
phalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints,

may be a contributing factor. Unfortunate- 
ly, lymph nodes from other locations were 
not analyzed, and no other published hu- 
man data is available for purposes of com- 
parison. There are conflicting reports in 
the literature concerning gold content of 
lymph nodes in experimental animal mod- 
els. Jeffrey et al (13) have found high

Further, there was less depletion of lymph 
node gold stores with the passage of time 
than occurred in other tissues suggesting 
either stronger binding of the metal in 
certain tissues, preferential gold loss, or 
redistribution within the body. On the oth- 
er hand, Bertrand et al (10) who studied 
the distribution of Au-198 labeled gold 
sodium thiosulfate in rabbits and white rats 
after a single injection, found low gold 
concentration in the lymph nodes of the 
rabbits but did not report rat lymph node 
levels. 
The high concentrations in the renal 
and adrenal glands, and in the spleen, liver 
and bone marrow are in accord with previ- 
ously published animal studies. Thus, 
Block et a1 (S), who sacrificed white rats 
following a course of therapy with several 
gold compounds, found highest levels in 
the kidney, liver and spleen. Swartz et a1 
(14) reported the distribution of sulfur-35 
and lS5Au thioglucose in sacrificed mice, 
and found an initial (30 minute) “flush” of 
the kidneys and adrenals with subsequent 
highest deposition in the reticuloendotheli- 
a1 system. Bertrand ,and co-workers (lo), 
while noting some species differences, 
found highest gold concentrations in the 
kidneys and spleen, with lesser values in 
the liver, adrenal, synovial membrane and 
elsewhere. The higher gold concentration 
in the renal cortex than in the renal me- 
dulla has been previously noted in animal 
studies (15). No information regarding gold 
levels in these tissues in patients with rheu- 
matoid arthritis could be found except for 
the account by Parr and Taylor (16) of a 
cirrhotic patient who had received a total 
of 1.8 g of gold 13 years previously and 
whose concentration of gold in the liver 
was 2 rg/g. They also reported the “natu- 
ral” gold concentration in human liver by 
analyzing random post-mortem specimens 
and found the median value to be 57ppgIg. 
Using 198Au-labeled sodium thiomalate and 
quantitating deposition sites with a scintil- 
lation counter, Lawrence (9) studied gold 
distribution in rheumatoid patients. Al- 
though highest counts were over the injec- 
tion sites during the first two weeks, a shift 
occurred at 20 days with higher counts 
developing over the liver, spleen and kid- 
neys. 
The articular and para-articular tissues 
exhibited significantly less affinity for gold 
than the organs of the reticuloendothelial 
system. This finding is in keeping with the 
animal data of Bertrand (10). Of the articu- 
lar structures, the synovium had the 
highest gold concentration and cartilage 
had the least. Bertrand’s study (10) of a 
single patient with rheumatoid arthritis 
(previously without gold therapy, but who 
received a single injection of a radioactive- 
ly labeled gold compound prior to knee 
surgery) also demonstrated the greater 
affinity for gold of the synovial membrane 
compared to other articular tissues. Tonna 
et a1 (17) using the in situ neutron ac- 
tivated 19SAu and autoradiography tech- 
nics, studied skeletal tissue gold distribu- 
tion in mice. They found deposition on the 
surface of the synovium, the reticuloen- 
dothelial cells of the marrow and in select- 
ed osteogenic cells. However, gold could 
not be identified in cartilage. 
The greater gold concentration at the 
injection site (gluteal muscles) than other 
muscles has been previously noted in ani- 
mal studies and presumably represents res- 
idual gold remaining ,at the needle sites (8). 
The very low concentration of gold in 
hair is of interest, in that certain heavy 
metals (eg, lead, arsenic) concentrate heavi- 
ly in hair (18). In fact, lead has been 
reported in greater concentrations in h,air 
than in any other body tissue (19).

The plasma, whole blood, and urine 
gold concentrations are higher than antici- 
pated. Presumably, dehydration and tissue 
autolysis accompanying early body decom- 
position account for these spurious eleva- 
tions. While routine urine gold values are 
of a lesser magnitude than plasma, the 
altered ratio found in this case can best be 
attributed to the very concentrated urine 
specimen obtained. 
The relative plasma-synovial fluid gold 
concentrations found in our patient is com- 
patible with previous studies of Freyberg et 
a1 (2) and Lawrence (9) who found synovi- 
a1 fluid gold generally to be equal to or 
lower than plasma levels. 
The variable gold concentrations in du- 
plicate tissue specimens requires comment. 
Biopsy specimens from different regions of 
the organ were obtained for each analysis. 
Perhaps gold is not evenly concentrated 
within a given organ, its distribution being 
dependent upon a number of factors such 
as vascularity, cell type, cell longevity, etc. 
The wet weight to “dry weight” gold con- 
centration ratios are fairly constant be- 
tween like specimens suggesting that tissue 
drying is not the explanation. 
An attempt to quantitate organ gold was 
made, with the realization that substantial 
errors are involved in estimating the 
weight of certain organ systems (eg, muscle, 
bone) as well as in the assumption that 
gold concentration from a limited number 
of biopsy specimens is representative of 
that found in the entire organ. It appears, 
as in Block’s rat study (S), that significant 
quantities of gold are stored in the “car- 
cass” tissues (muscle, bone) due to their 
great weight. 
It is noteworthy that the gold concentra- 
tion in certain tissues of our patient (eg, 
lymph node, adrenal) approached or sur- 
passed the level needed to inhibit certain 
lysosomal enzymes (acid phosphatase, p-glu- 
curonidase) as reported by Persellin and 
Ziff (20). In their in vitro studies, using 
guinea pig macrophages bathed in a gold 
sodium thiomalate (Myochrysine) media, 
they demonstrated intra-macrophage gold 
transport and inhibition of the aforemen- 
tioned enzymes. However, threshold gold 
concentration to affect this inhibition was 
250 pg/ml, a concentration which does not 
prevail in the serum in the clinical setting. 
However, our findings demonstrate that 
selected tissues attain such high gold con- 
centrations. It is likely that much of this 
gold is stored in a bound form in subcellu- 
lar particles and released into a micro- 
environment within and between the par- 
enchymatous cells. In these terms, our data 
lends support to the theory that gold may 
localize and act (to inhibit lysosomal en- 
zymes) in the widely dispersed cells (macro- 
phages) of the reticuloendothelial system 
It is our belief that firm conclusions 
drawn from the study of a single patient is 
hazardous. Further, it is realized that 5 gm 
of gold salt is somewhat more than most 
patients with rheumatoid arthritis receive 
in a 5-year period. Additional study is 
needed, and our results suggest that partic- 
ular attention should be paid to the lymph 
nodes and other reticuloendothelial organs. 
Lymph nodes from multiple areas should 
be analyzed for gold content to learn if 
comparable concentrations are reached 
throughout the lymphatic system.
