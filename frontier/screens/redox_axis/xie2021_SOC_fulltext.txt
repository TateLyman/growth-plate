Abstract 
Bone age is used widely by pediatricians to assess the skeletal maturity of  a child and predict growth 
potential. This entails  measuring the size of secondary ossification centers (SOCs), which develop 
with age in the ends of long bones, which are initially cartilaginous. However, little is presently known 
about the developmental biology, evolution and  functional role of th ese skeletal elements. Here, we 
summarize the knowledge currently available in this area and  discuss potential primary functions of 
the SOC.  
Keywords 
Secondary ossification center (SOC), evolution, development, function, cartilage canal, chondrocytes 
 
Introduction 
In mammals, longitudinal skeletal growth  occurs primarily through endochondral ossification, which 
involves the formation of  bone on a cartilage template. During this process, a primary ossification 
center (POC) is formed within the diaphysis and a secondary ossification center (SOC) within the 
epiphysis (Figure 1), which is initially cartilaginous [1]. The SOC separates the epiphysis into articular 
cartilage and the growth plate  (Figure 1), the former of which is responsible for skeletal articulation 
and the latter for its linear growth.  
The POC and SOC develop  at different time -points in the  different long bones, with the POC 
developing much earl ier. For example, in the femur and tibia  of the mouse , the POCs form around 
embryonic day 15.5, whereas the SOCs appear between 5 and 7 days of postnatal life [2, 3]. Similarly, 
in the human tibia the POC is formed between the sixth and twelfth  weeks of gestations, whereas the 
proximal SOC arises around the time of birth.  
Although the cellular processes underlying formation of POCs and SOCs  are relatively similar , 
including resorption of  cartilage, angiogenesis, and deposition of bone matrix , there are also key 
differences. The initial stage of  POC formation involves the appearance  of a bony collar around the 
midshaft of the cartilage rudiments  [4-6], followed by chondrocyte hypertrophy, calcification of the 
extracellular matrix, vascular invasion and deposition of osteogenic cells [7]. In contrast, in most cases 
development of the SOC is preceded by the formation of canals within the hyaline cartilage of the 
epiphysis that later are utilized by  invading blood vessels and osteogenic cell precursors [8], with 
exceptions being  small animals such as rats and mice [9, 10] . An even m ore obvious anatomical 
difference is that as the POC grows, the cartilaginous diaphysis is rapidly replaced by bony tissues, 
whereas even after the SOC has been fully formed, the cartilage of the growth plate  and articular 
cartilage are retained for a relatively long time. 
Cartilage canals 
The presence of blood vessels in cartilage was first demonstrated in the  early eighteenth century  by 
Willian Hunter, who injected a colored liquid wax into the synovial joints of adults to visualize these 
structures [11], later referred to as  “cartilage canal s” by Howship [12]. These canals, which, as 
mentioned above, form in the epiphysis well before the appearance of a SOC [10], are composed of  
muscular arterioles, venules, loose connective tissue and perivascular capillaries [13].

Two functional explanations for the formation of cartilage canals  have been proposed.  The theory of 
invasion emphasizes the importance of the primitive connective tissue cells within these canals. From 
the sub-perichondrial zone, these cells  grow inward into the cartilage mass dissolving the matrix and 
forming channels [14] that provide gateways for vascular invasion a nd the recruitment of bone cells 
[14]. To account for new discoveries, t his theory was extended in the theory of inclusion to identify 
these canals as part of the perichondrial connective tissue , with  the incoming blood vessels being 
engulfed in the growing cartilage, rather than growing into it by dissolution of its  matrix [10].  
Anatomical observations indicate that major functions of the cartilage canals are to provide nutrients, 
blood and cell s to the developing epiphyseal center [10].  In rabbits , from the initial appearance of 
these canals in the cartilaginous epiphysis until the formation of a SOC, the volume of hyaline 
cartilage increase s 25-fold, while  the volume of the canals themselves (containing mainly  
mesenchymal-like and blood cells) increased 400-fold [15]. 
Three morphologically distinguishable phases are involved in the formation of cartilage canals [15]. 
Initially, infoldings in  the perichondrium appear, followed by  chondrocyte hypertrophy within the 
center of the epiphysis and a simultaneous increase in the number of mesenchymal cells within the se 
infoldings. Finally, vascular invasion and matrix mineralization transform the cartilage canal s into a 
complex set of interconnected blood vessels.  When this process is finished, the canals that have not 
been incorporated into the SOC degenerate, with elimination of their blood vessels and transformation 
of surrounding connective tissues  into fibrocartilage [16], thereby completely removing  plugged 
canals from the medullary cavity [16].  
Although, as mentioned, this network of cartilage canals appears before the SOC, there is at present no 
direct evidence that these two processes are connected . In fact, the development of the SOC without 
cartilage canals in small laboratory rodents, as well as  the lack of co-localization of the SOC with the  
area of the highest canal density in human cartilaginous epiphysis [10] suggest that these two  
developments are independent.  
What triggers the development of a SOC? 
Several proposals concerning the primary trigger for the development of the SOC are described in the 
scientific literature. The early view was that this development is initiated by the ossification of large 
areas of the avascular cartilage that are malnourished and probably receive to o little oxygen as well  
[17]. However, the realization that the extensive network of cartilage canals in the epiphyses of large 
animals provides a rather homogenous distribution of nutrien ts and oxygen has caused this idea to be  
mostly abandoned.  
Today, the m ore common  viewpoint is that mechanical stress  initiates formation of the SOC. 
Mechanical stress is  known to be an important determinant of cartilage development . Exemplifying, 
compression of the  rat ulna  by either static or dynamic loading during growth attenuates  its 
longitudinal growth in a manner  that is dependent on the extent of the load , with the greatest force 
causing a larger hypertrophic zone and more hypertrophic cells accumulating [18]. Mechanical 
compression or stretching of vertebrae in the rat tail can either suppress or stimulate their growth, to a 
more pronounced extent by compression than with tensile force [19]. Application of either intermittent 
or continuous compressive force to  cartilaginous rudiments of the embryonic murine metatarsal bone 
in culture causes the ir hypertrophic cells  to deposit more calcium phosphate in the matrix [20]. 
Mechanical loading on the distal femoral condyle of the neonatal rabbit in culture  accelerates 
formation of the SOC, with elevated  expression of Collagen X (ColX), Runt-related transcription

As a complement  to these empirical albeit indirect findings, the effect of mechanical stress on

Thus, mechanical stress probably influences and maybe even triggers formation of the SOC. On the 
other hand, numerous evolutionary observations suggest that the SOC develops prior to mechanical 
loading. For example, in bats ( Chiroptera) in utero the SOC appears in different skeletal elements of 
the forelimb at dramatically different time -points, depending on  how early the  bone will later be 
subjected to mechanical demands [26]. Similarly, in marsupials ossification of the forelimbs, which 
are required to climb to the mother´s pouch, is much more advanced at birth than that of the hindlimbs 
[27, 28] . Furthermore, undulates, wh ose newborns must be able  to flee from  predators as soon as 
possible after birth, develop SOCs in their leg bones before they are born [29, 30]. These variations in 
the rate of ossification  in utero indicate that genetic program plays a major role , probably preceding 
the mechanical influence. This proposal is in agreement with our recent observations that unloading of 
the growing limbs of rats does not affect development of their SOC [26].  
 
The molecular mechanisms underlying development of the SOC 
Although the g enetic program ming that  drives development of the  SOC has not yet been fully 
elucidated, several molecular factors are known to be involved. During excavation of the SOC in  the 
developing epiphysis of the rat tibia, both the matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) and the sub-families of 
aggrecanase enzyme contribute in a site- and stage-specific manner to the lysis of cartilage matrix [31]. 
Areas of cartilage that are destined to undergo resorption are first lysed  specifically through a 
cooperative attack by both gelatinase B (MMP-9) and collagenase‐3, resulting in disintegration of the 
framework of collagen fibrils [32]. In mice  homozygous for  a null mutation in the MMP -9 gene, 
apoptosis, vascularization and ossification are all delayed, thereby delaying formation of the SOC until 
2.5 weeks of age [33].  
In addition, in mice deficient in membrane‐type 1 metalloproteinase  (MT1‐MMP) the formation of 
SOCs in long bones is delayed for approximately 8 days, due to failure of the vessels to grow into the 
cartilaginous epiphysis [34]. Spatiotemporal analysis of the proximal epiphysis of the rat tibia during 
development reveals that MMP‐9, MT1‐MMP and MMP‐13 are expressed in areas at the ends of the 
growing blood vessels and the expanding borders of the marrow in close proximity to one another, but 
with no overlap [35]. The angiogenic peptide vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is expressed 
by hypertrophic chondrocytes contributing to formation of the bone marrow cavity, not to formation of

It has been proposed that the β‐ catenin expressed by the chondrocytes is the upstream regulator of the 
MMPs in volved in initiation of the  SOC [3]. A gain -of-function mutation in  β‐catenin localized

Moreover, knockout of insulin ‐ like growth factor ‐ I (IGF-I) in cells that express osterix (Osx) also

Other sporadic observations, which can shed light on molecular mechanisms of SOC development, 
include murine models involving constitutive activation of the stimulatory alpha -subunit of the G 
protein (Gsα) in the early limb-bud mesenchyme [39] or global knockout of salt-inducible kinase 3 
(SIK3), both of which primarily inhibit hypertrophy in chondrocytes. In the latter, the SOC is not fully 
developed even at 8 months of age [40]. A neomorphic point  mutation, but not a loss -of-function 
mutation in microRNA (miRNA), miR-140, was recently reported  to delay formation of the  SOC in 
the femur, humerus, carpal and metacarpal bones of mice with negligible impact on chondrocyte 
hypertrophy [41]. This same heterozygous nucleotide substitution has been observed in patients with a 
newly identified form of skeletal dysplasia characterized by delayed epiphyseal ossification of the hip 
and knee [41]. To our knowledge, this  is the only mutation that has so far been reported to affect 
development of the SOC without substantially impairing chondrocyte hypertrophy, vascularization or 
expression of MMPs. On the other hand, the underlying  mechanism may involve the downregulation 
of HIF1a and VEGF in chondrocytes associated with this mutation in miR-140 [41], which may impair 
the required ingrowth of blood vessels.   
Interestingly, the tim epoint at which the  SOC initially appears coincides closely with the peak 
circulating level of thyroid hormone in several species, including  the mouse [2], rat [42] and human 
[43, 44]. In some human fetuses, the SOCs of the distal femur and proximal tibia start to appear during 
the third trimester of pregnancy  [44] and the late second/early third trimester is a  crucial period of 
transition with respect to  fetal metabolism of  thyroid hormone [43]. On postnatal day 21, 
endochondral ossification of the SOC in the femur and tibia i n mice deficient in thyroid hormone is 
severely compromised and this situation can be rescued by treatment with thyroid hormone [2].  
Treatment of mice which carry a mutation in the  thyroid stimulating hormone receptor (Tshr) with 
thyroid hormone  triggers hypertrophy in chondrocytes within the area where the  SOC will later 
develop, w ith accompanying elevations in the levels  of markers for pre-hypertrophic chondrocytes, 
such as Indian hedgehog (Ihh) and Osx and, subsequently, of markers for hypertrophic chondrocytes, 
such as collagen 10 ( Col10) and osteocalcin  [2]. A follow -up study by these same investigators

At the same time, it must be emphasized that in humans, with the exceptions of  the epiphyses in the

These observations disfavor the hypothesis that peak levels of thyroid hormone  constitute a major 
trigger for formation of the SOC. 
Thus, several genetic factors appear to be  involved in SOC development, mainly through the  
regulation of chondrocyte hypertrophy , matrix resorption and/or invasion by blood vessels.  At the 
same time,  detailed knowledge concerning  the primary genetic machinery  that triggers the 
development of SOC in specific bones and at specific time-points is still lacking.   
Evolution of the SOC 
In stem-tetrapods (the first vertebrates with recognizable limbs that appeared 385-365 million years 
ago) such as Eusthenopteron and Acanthostega, the bones grow in a manner similar to that in modern 
tetrapods such as humans – i.e., longitudinally via endochondral formation [49]. At the same time, 
ossification of the  bones of those early vertebrates  was very slow and remnants of hypertrophic 
chondrocytes remained within the bone shaft almost until adulthood, with  no secondary ossification 
centers [49]. Today´s mammals all have ossified SOCs, although there are some exceptions with sub-
specialized limbs such as whales. Among reptiles, which consists of two clades, Lepidosauria (lizards, 
snakes, tuatara) and  Archosauria (crocodilians, chelonians, birds and non -avian dinosaurs) , this 
structure in present  in the Lepidosauria  clade with exception of limb-less snakes , but not in the 
Archosauria clade [26]. Albeit certain birds do develop SOCs in their leg bones  [26]. Amphibians do 
not exhibit classical SOCs, but the adults of some species in the order Anura (frogs) develop calcified 
secondary centers, probably as an adaptation to their specialized gait  [50-53]. 
According to the report by Haines in 1939 [54], evidence for the earliest evolutionary presence of a 
SOC in a long bone was observed in fossils of the Jurassic Sapheosaurus by the German scientists 
Meyer and Fuchs in 1859 and 1908, respectively. These SOCs probably developed in a manner similar 
to that in its living relative, Sphenodon (tuatara) – i.e., a large secondary calcified center forms and 
expands throughout the cartilage epiphysis, at the adult stage  becoming resorbed and replaced by 
marrow and cancellous bone [53]. The epiphyses of  Agama atra  illustrate the type  of epiphysis 
common in lizards, w ith a large secondary center surrounded by a layer of undifferentiated cartilage 
[55]. Calcification of the secondary center always begins in  the central region of the cartilaginous 
epiphysis, subsequently expanding  toward the perichondrium at the expense  of undifferentiated 
cartilage [55, 56].  
This arrangement is observed in all groups of lizards that possess a calcified secondary center, except 
for the  Varanus genus that includes  the largest living lizards [55]. In Varanus niloticus , the

In general, w ithin the Archosauria clade no ossification centers are present. In this case, linear bone 
growth in volves uneven resorption of cartilage at the chondro -osseous junction , with bone marrow 
protruding into cartilaginous epiphysis from the metaphyseal side to varying extents sequestering the 
hypertrophic chondrocytes  between them [52, 53, 57, 58] . In turtles ( Chelonians) and crocodiles 
(Crocodilians) the protrusions are relatively rudimental and primitive, whereas in birds and non-avian 
dinosaurs they are very extensive and uniform in shape with clearly calcified or ossified walls [52, 53, 
57, 58].  
In the order of chelonians, bone ends  in newly hatched animals are covered by a large amount  of 
undifferentiated cartilage, with the hypertrophic cells forming cone-shaped projections that anchor the 
entire epiphysis to the periosteal bone [52]. As the animal develops, endochondral bone growth occurs 
via uneven erosion of hypertrophic chondrocytes, with the marrow developing into a series of 
protrusions, each of which erodes an elongated bay with ossified walls  having large parts of the 
hypertrophic zone in between these bays [52, 53]. The epiphyseal organization in the crocodilian order 
is similar, except that protrusions are longer, more numerous and narrower [52].  
Non-avian dinosaurs and birds develop long protrusions in the bone marrow that are  well-organized, 
numerous, narrow, and aligned along the longitudinal axis, with calcified or ossified walls that extend 
deep into the epiphyseal cartilage, sequestering essentially all of the hypertrophic chondrocytes [52, 53, 
57, 58]. These protrusions probably link the extensive resorption of cartilage in these rapidly growing 
animals to invading  blood vessel s. In general , i n non-avian dinosaurs and birds, vascularizatio n is 
more extensive, cortices thicker and osteocyte lacunae more abundant and more well-organized than in 
chelonians and crocodilians [58]. Some birds do develop SOCs in their weight -bearing bones such as 
tibiotarsus and tarsometatarsus [27, 49, 57] , possibly as  a secondary adaptation  that provides 
additional stiffness to weight-bearing joints  and/or as compensation for the  evolutionary loss of the 
SOC that might theoretically occurred in most species of this order [52].  
As mentioned above,  with a few specialized exceptions, mammals have well-developed and ossified 
SOCs in their epiphyses [26]. The most striking exceptions are Cetaceans, including whales, dolphins 
and porpoises, which all evolved from terrestrial artiodactyls 50-60 million years ago  [59-61] and are 
the only mammals  that shifted their entire life -cycle to a marine lifestyle. During the transition from 
land to water, their forelimbs designed for terrestrial locomotion developed into flippers. Interestingly, 
cetaceans appear to have lost their SOCs during evolution gradually. Indeed, early Cetaceans that lived 
in the mid-Eocene period, such as Maiacetus inuus, retained fully developed hind limbs linked to the 
vertebral column  that allowed locomotion on land , as well as typical  SOCs [62]. In contrast, later 
Cetaceans from the Miocene, such as members of the Balaenopteridae and Balaenidae families, were 
fully aquatic, with relatively narrow and elongated flippers, and the SOCs of the ir metacarpals and 
phalanges were reduced in size by as much as 80% [26, 63] . These observations p oint towards  a 
potential association between terrestrial lifestyle and the need of the SOC in certain skeletal elements.  
In summary, it appears plausible that animals that had become entirely terrestrial (amniotes) developed 
the SOC or ossif ied protrusions as adaptations to their new lifestyle . The exact relationship between

From an evolutionary perspective, various traits can evolve independent ly, develop in parallel or 
converge. The evolutionary relation ship between the  appearance of the SOC in Mammalia and 
Lepidosauria is not obvious  and likely represents independent traits . Albeit, characterization of the  
organization of the genome of Sphenodon, the most ancient order of Lepidosauria still extant, recently

Marsupials apparently does not involve canals and this structure is  histologically very similar to the 
typical SOC in lizards [55], in contrast to the situation in  numerous placental animals , including 
human, where cartilage canals are formed during embryonic development [65-67].  
 
Functionals of the SOC and its clinical significance 
Anatomically, the SOC separates the articular cartilage from the growth  plate cartilage , which 
provides several advantages in connection with formation of the epiphysis [52], including, as proposed 
by Haines in 1938, a flexibility for  the growth plate cartilage to take a  shape that allows proper 
arrangement of the trabeculae  [52] . In addition, this separation allows the formation of a more 
complex joint surface [52] and provides additional stiffness to the epiphyses (as recently confirmed 
both experimentally and b y mathematical modeling [27]) , thereby enhancing the firmness of  limbs 
[52]. Interestingly, elevated mechanical stress in some regions of the articular surface  and alterations 
in subchondral bones  precipitate most cases of osteoarthritis (OA) [68] and, therefore, the additional 
stiffness that the SOC provides to the epiphysis might protect against the development of this 
condition.  
Another function of the SOC might be to  protect hypertrophic chondrocytes within the underlying 
growth plate against extensive mechanical stress associated with weight-bearing. We have shown both 
that these cells are highly sensitive to mechanical stress and that the SOC efficiently reduces this stress 
within the growth plate  in a manner that increases the load that can be tolerated by hypertrophic  
chondrocytes several-fold [26]. Evolutionary observations support the proposal that such protection is 
important for terrestrial animals. Indeed, in this context , early development of the SOC in particular 
limbs of several mammalian species that will later be subjected to extensive mechanical demands (see 
further above) also indicates that this structure plays a protective role. With respect to humans, one can 
speculate that toddlers should not be forced to walk or be subjected to other excessive loads on their 
long bones (e.g., in connection with weight -lifting, jumping or gymnastics) at too early an age, since 
this might have a negative impact on chondrocyte hypertrophy and, thus, on their linear growth  and 
final height. Indeed, the peak predicted force on various joints during such activities can be as much as 
approximately 5-15 times body weight  [69-71] and the extent to which  the SOC can protect against 
such high force remains unknown for human bones . These observations and our speculations are in 
good agreement with the empirically derived law, formulated by Hueter and Volkmann between 1862

Another potential function of the SOC is related to formation of a stem cell niche within the growth 
plate. We recently demonstrated that the mature SOC promotes renewal of epiphyseal stem cells [73] 
within the growth plate by altering the extracellular matrix and secreting morphogens in a manner that 
creates a niche for these cells  [36, 74] . This niche allows the  depletion of chondroprogenitors 
associated with longitudinal growth during the fetal and neonatal periods to  be replaced by self-
renewal of these same cells [36].  
Several of the various types of cell s present in the SOC produce Sonic hedgehog (Shh), which could, 
at least in theory, diffuse from the SOC into the resting zone of the growth plate to promote formation 
of the epiphyseal stem cell niche [36, 75]. This proposal is supported by the finding that  inhibition of 
hedgehog ( Hh) signaling with an antagonist leads to premature fusion of the growth plate [76], 
whereas activation with a smoothened agonist promotes the proliferation of stem cells. The latter stays 
true if the agonist  applied after the SOC has matured [36], but  it inhibits such proliferation when 
applied prior to this maturation [73]. Thus, the SOC may facilitate linear growth not only by protecting 
hypertrophic chondrocytes, but also by  supporting the renewal of epiphyseal ste m cells, thereby 
facilitating the generation of chondrocytes required for linear growth.  On the other hand, the SOC is 
formed at the expense of chondro -progenitors that normally constitut e the central region of the 
cartilaginous epiphysis and extensive development  of the SOC appears to be  associated with limited 
growth potential in some species [26], but not others (e.g., ungulates [29, 30]).  
As mentioned above, i n humans the stage of SOC development, i.e., bone age, is widely used as a n 
estimate of subsequent  growth potential [77, 78] . At the same time, the observation  that patients 
deficient in  estrogen show unlimited growth until treated  [79, 80]  suggests that the relationship 
between the SOC size and growth potential may not be functional but reflects parallel ph ysiological 
processes.    
Clinically, various types of fractures can occur during  the development of the SOC, from initiation of 
ossification to its fusion with metaphysis and cessation of growth [46]. When the appearance of the 
SOC in newborns is delayed, proper diagnosis of skeletal injuries that occur at an early age can be 
highly challenging. For instance,  separation of the distal humeral physis, which typically occurs in  
children under the age of 3, is often misdiagnosed with radiography, due to potential absence of  the 
SOC in the radius, ulna and humerus [81, 82]. In addition, fractures in the vicinity of a  child’s elbow, 
such as medial epicondyle fractures, are often difficult to diagnosis exactly because of the presence of 
multiple SOCs [83]. Moreover, the unique manner in which the SOCs in the proximal tibia epiphysis 
and tibia tubercle apophysis (i.e., the site tendon or ligament attachment) fuse creates a relatively weak 
zone between the se two structures , rendering the tibial tubercle more susceptible to fractures in 
athletes between the age s of 12 and 15 [84-86]. Furthermore, during closure of  the patella at the age 
of 5 to  6, the formation of multiple SOCs involves ossification from the  center to the periphery, 
leaving a cartilaginous peripheral rim more vulnerable to injury [87].  
Thus, in addition to serving as an indicator of bone age, the most clinically relevant feature of the SOC 
at the present time involves epiphyseal fractures. Such fractures often lead to numerous complications, 
such as formation of bone bridges and impairment of growth. We sincerely hope that a more detailed 
understanding of the biology and evolution  of the SOC will help develop  better treatment for such 
fractures.

Conclusions and Future Directions 
Altogether, current extensive histological, evolutionary and genetic evidence provides novel insights 
into the role which the SOC plays in the skeleton. Numerous  fundamental mechanisms and various 
signaling pathways underlying SOC development have been characterized. However, almost all 
established models involving mutated mice  and genetic or pharmacological perturbation  only delay 
formation of the SOC and we still lack a model where this development can be eliminated specifically 
and completely. Such a model would allow  definitive determination of the role of this structure  in 
connection with various processes, including linear growth, the development of osteoarthritis, gait, etc.  
One possible explanation for this lack  is that since the SOC is composed of a variety of  cell types, 
ranging from bone-related cells to nerve fibers and vascular cells, complete elimination might require 
altering multiple factors simultaneously. Another possibility is that despite all our empirical evidences, 
we still do not know exactly which  molecular mechanisms trigger formation of the SOC in the 
epiphysis. In addition, the m olecular cues that originate from the SOC and regulate stem cells in  the 
growth plate, as well as the behavior of articular cartilage are still largely unknown. In this context 
characterization of the behavior of epiphyseal stem cells using mutant mouse strains already available 
could be highly informative. Moreover, the growing number of mouse strains for lineage tracing of 
various types of cells and the rapid devel opment of various single-cell- and omics - approaches can 
help dissect the cellular and molecular mechanism(s) underlying SOC formation.

Highlights 
 The formation of cartilage canals precedes development of the SOC. 
 This development is influenced by various regulatory molecules , including β ‐catenin, thyroid 
hormones, and insulin‐like growth factor‐I (IGF-I). 
 During evolution, the SOC first appeared in tetrapod vertebrates that moved from an aquatic to 
the terrestrial environment.  
 The SOC protects chondrocytes in the growth plate from the mechanical stress associated with 
weight-bearing in the terrestrial environment. 
 The SOC regulates epiphyseal stem cells in the growth plate. 
 The development of the SOC can affect susceptibility to and the diagnosis of bone fractures.
