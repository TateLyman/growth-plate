Symposium Paper
Tradeoffs Between Oxygen and Energy in Tibial Growth
at High Altitude
S.M. BAILEY ,1* J. XU,2 J.H. FENG,3 X. HU,4 C. ZHANG,3 AND S. QUI3
1Department of Anthropology, Tufts University, Medford, Massachusetts
2Department of Applied Mathematics, Sichuan University, Chengdu, Sichuan, People’s Republic of China
3Department of Exercise Sciences, Sichuan University, Chengdu, Sichuan, People’s Republic of China
4Department of Geography, South China Normal University, Guangzhou, People’s Republic of China
ABSTRACT Some studies of high altitude populations argue that stature reduction results
from caloric, rather than hypoxic, stress. However, tradeoff models of oxygen and glucose me-
tabolism predict that in hypoxemia, glucose metabolism will be downregulated. We used trade-
off assumptions in two hypotheses: First, that hypoxia targets leg segment growth differentially ,
and second, that proportions of leg segments partition the impact of high altitude into hypoxe-
mic and energetic components. A group of 113 Han and Tibetan middle school children at 3100
m aged 8 to 11 were measured for segment anthropometries, skinfolds, vital capacity , blood oxy-
gen saturation, and percent body fat. MANOV A showed that Tibetan children were signiﬁcantly
larger and fatter than Han children. Independent of ethnicity or caloric status, absolute and rel-
ative tibia length was signiﬁcantly reduced in children with lower blood oxygen saturation.
Height, chest circumference, sitting height, tibia length, and ankle diameter were greatest in
fatter children, independent of ethnicity or blood oxygen. For children of either ethnicity with
the lowest blood oxygen, size as well as proportion was impacted. These results support the
tradeoff model. Caloric reserves and ethnicity independently affect total skeletal size. Oxygen
saturation and ethnicity affect leg proportions. In hypoxemia, body fat has less impact on
growth than when ample oxygen is present. Therefore, we should qualify the claim that size in
high altitude populations stems from nutritional stress. The ﬁndings also suggest that decanali-
zation may have different meanings and outcomes depending on which body segments contrib-
ute to the effect. Am. J. Hum. Biol. 19:662–668, 2007. ' 2007 Wiley-Liss, Inc.
Legs are benchmarks of our adaptive his-
tory . Compromised leg growth under environ-
mental stress has been demonstrated over
timespans ranging from the evolutionary
through the gestational (Bailey and Hu, 2002;
Bass et al., 2000; Floyd, 2007; Gunell et al.,
1998; Holliday , 1997; Jantz and Jantz, 1999;
Katzmarzyk and Leonard, 1998; Lampl and
Jeanty , 2003; Lampl et al., 2003; Lawlor et al.,
2003; Roberts, 1978; Ruff, 1987; Tanner et al.,
1982; Theintze et al., 1993; Trinkhaus, 1981).
However, the functional signiﬁcance of this
sensitivity is less clear. Our lower limbs are
made of linked anatomical segments that do
not grow as a piece. The few growth studies
that have measured human or animal seg-
ments suggest that the tibia may be more per-
turbed by environmental stress than the fe-
mur (Bailey and Hu, 2002; Cameron et al.,
1982; Daly et al., 2000; Holiday and Ruff,
2001; Lampl et al., 2003; Ruff et al., 1991).
Although there are well-theorized physio-
logical explanations supporting a ﬁnding of
differential skeletal sensitivity to insult
(Lampl et al., 2003, 2004; Lampl, 2005), we
know less about proximate mechanisms that
might privilege one’s calf at the expense of
one’s thigh, or compromise both relative to sit-
ting height, and we know virtually nothing
about what—if any—selective consequences
spring from these differences.
A second problem is that environments
affecting leg growth typically present multi-
factorial stresses. Children’s bodies adapt to
each stress with varying success, and it is

unclear if a morphological outcome is a proxy
for additive and linear effects of each stress,
or something else altogether. Nonetheless, we
tend to assay growth with familiar biomedical
models developed using one stress, undernu-
trition, as their causal agent. Decanalization,
not surprisingly , then signals lack of nutrients
and descent into compromised health or frank
pathology .
A number of studies, for instance, have
attributed total stature reduction at high alti-
tude chieﬂy to socioeconomics, rather than ox-
ygen. Hypobaric hypoxemia, these studies
argue, affects both indigenous and migrant
morphology equally , and save perhaps in chest
shape and size. (Dittmar, 1998; Greska et al.,
1985; Leatherman et al., 1995; Leonard et al.,
1990; Pawson, 1977; Stinson, 1982; Weitz and
Garruto, 2004; Weitz et al., 2000; Weitz et al.,
2004).
By contrast, numerous studies indicate that
physiological differences between indigenous
and immigrant populations at high altitudes
are sufﬁciently profound to create measurable
natural selection (Beall et al., 2001, 2004;
Beall, 1981, 2000; Moore et al., 1999, 2000;
More and Sun, 1990; Niermeyer et al., 1995;
Zamudio et al., 1993), and there exist evolving
genetic differences related to adaptive range
(Beall et al. 1994, 1997b). Moreover, the few
studies that measure anatomical segments
indicate that the tibia responds more to hy-
poxemia than the femur, and that both may
reﬂect different underlying adaptive re-
sponses than does total stature reduction (Bai-
ley and Hu, 2002; Bailey , 2004; Lampl et al.,
2003; Lampl and Jeanty , 2004). If so, then as
clinical signs, legs and total stature appear to
point at different biological referents.
Our title’s partitioning of calories from oxy-
gen thus ﬂags a seldom discussed, but founda-
tional disagreement about how we theorize
the relationship between growth and adapta-
tion. Can children sharing the same stature
percentile and physical environment, none-
theless have very different adaptive histories
written into their body proportions? Such dif-
ferent histories could yield more divergent
risks of subsequent morbidity and mortality
than we would expect from a shared dot on a
chart. Good health might not be synonymous
with good growth.
To approach this problem through our own
data, we assume that throughout growth,
each child’s genome will attempt to minimize
hypoxemia through a series of integrated met-
abolic tradeoffs between cell oxygen tension
and cell energy utilization. This is an applica-
tion of Lampl’s ‘‘Bow Tie’’ model (Lampl,
2005). Morphological outcomes thus are a
snapshot of an ongoing system of physiological
adaptations, some of which will necessitate
clinically measurable decanalization, and
some of which will not. The systems are spa-
cially and temporally dynamic. Some target
tissues will be more involved in tradeoffs than
others, and current tradeoffs may not predom-
inate as the child matures. The proximal tibia
appears to be a particularly active site of
tradeoffs during the fetal period (Lampl et al.,
2003, 2005; Lampl and Jeanty , 2003).
We predict, then, that these tradeoffs should
leave behind a signature: lower leg propor-
tions will differ relative to the type of tradeoff
necessitated between hypoxic and energetic
stresses. Populations of children better
adapted to hypoxia should have more energy
available at the cellular level for lower limb
growth. Populations of children who are
poorly adapted may show comparable total
axial growth, given sufﬁcient calories, but it
will be concentrated in the trunk.
Tibetans offer a useful population for
extending the fetal tradeoff model into child-
hood. Their evolving major gene complex sig-
niﬁcantly improves adaptation to hypoxia
(Beall et al., 1994, 1997b, 2001, 2005). Follow-
ing our general model, we predict that Tibetan
children will show greater lower leg growth
than their classmates, independently of stat-
ure, other skeletal measures, or caloric status.
Because the major gene complex is still
spreading in the population, we do not assume
this will hold for every Tibetan child. Caloric
status, we predict, should have its own, more
familiar, morphological signature, involving
total size.
METHODS
To best partition the effects of ethnicity , hy-
poxia, and caloric status, our present analysis
compares 113 Han and Tibetan children aged
8–11 in two schools near Hongyuan, Sichuan
Province, at an altitude of 3100 m. Unlike
some urban areas in Tibet, Sichuan, or Qing
Hai, there are scant socioeconomic differences
among the children to confound analysis of
hypoxic stress. Because differences in mean
age were small (Tibetan mean 9.7, Han mean
10.2, H 1 P ¼ 0.132), analyses presented here
pooled age data, which were further adjusted
where appropriate using either analysis of co-

RESULTS
As seen in Table 1 for age covariate adjusted
data, Tibetan children were signiﬁcantly
larger than their Han peers in nearly all
dimensions. They were taller and heavier
with longer legs. They had larger chests,
greater arm muscle lean area, and wider
ankles, a good measure of skeletal robusticity .
They had higher BMI’s and more body fat.
Some sexual dimorphism was present—
Tibetan boys were signiﬁcantly shorter and
leaner than Tibetan girls, Han girls were sig-
niﬁcantly fatter than Han boys, both sets of
boys had greater arm muscle area—but there
were no clear trends and the effects were
small compared to ethnicity .
To partition the relative impacts of oxygen
and calories, we performed several three-way
MANOV A’s, with ethnicity , blood oxygen satu-
ration or vital capacity terciles, and percent
body fat or BMI terciles as factors, and a set of
skeletal size measures as dependent variables.
For the latter, we used standardized residuals
of regressions to correct for age and sex.
Table 2 shows relevant outcomes for the
three factors. The overall model was highly
signiﬁcant for all size variables. With blood ox-
ygen saturation and percent body fat included
in the model, ethnicity showed borderline sig-
niﬁcance for the ratio of tibia to leg, and was
nonsigniﬁcant for tibia to height. By contrast,
oxygen saturation solely impacted the lower
leg, whereas body fat targeted size and skele-
tal robusticity , but missed affecting propor-
tion. There were no interaction effects save for
ankle diameter in body fat terciles crossed
with oxygen terciles, and sitting height in oxy-
gen terciles crossed with body fat terciles.
The results are distilled into Figure 1. Rep-
resentative Z-scored skeletal measurements
are grouped by ﬁrst and third body fat terciles
nested within ﬁrst and third blood oxygen ter-
ciles.
Overall scores for Tibetan children within
each tercile were larger, suggesting that
Tibetan children may be genetically predis-
posed to be larger than Han, at least in this
environment. Caloric status had a similar,
highly signiﬁcant independent impact on size
variables. Blood oxygen terciles in turn had a
signiﬁcant independent impact focused more
on the distal limb: on tibia length, tibia to leg
length ratio, and tibia length to height ratio.
Children better adapted to hypoxia had dis-
proportionately longer lower legs, regardless
of ethnicity and caloric status, and regardless
of total body size. As shown, the total effect of
all three factors was dramatic, nearly 1.5 Z-

The effects were not uniform, however. Chil-
dren of either ethnicity with the lowest body
fat showed the greatest impact of oxygen sta-
tus, even on size variables. Those with more
bodyfat showed oxygen effects primarily on
leg proportion. Interestingly , Tibetan children
with higher blood oxygen saturation still
showed the impact of caloric reserves on both
size, and to a lesser degree leg proportion.
Han children in the higher saturation tercile
showed little impact of body fat except on leg
proportion.
CONCLUSIONS
Our most noteworthy ﬁnding is the answer
to our title’s choice, oxygen or calories. It
appears to be both, and more. Caloric reserves
and ethnicity each independently affect skele-
tal size. Oxygen saturation and probably eth-
nicity affect lower leg growth. For children
with the worst proﬁles of oxygen, size as well
as proportion was impacted.
These ﬁndings suggest we need to qualify
the claim that morphology in high altitude
populations largely mirrors nutritional,
rather than hypoxic stress. Both effects are
present in our sample, as would be predicted
by a tradeoff model that impacts each func-
tional anatomical domain differently . Our low-
est oxygen and lowest body fat group are chil-
dren in which cell oxygen is barely main-
tained, and at the expense of energy
metabolism. This group, largely Han, appears
to lack compensatory adaptations. That it
includes any Tibetans appears to follow Beall’s
model of a major gene complex that is spread-
ing, but has not yet ﬁxed at high frequencies
among Tibetans (Beall et al., 1994, 1997a,b).
Paradoxically , the marked impact of excess
calories on both Han and Tibetan children
with low blood oxygen children’s might seem
to demonstrate a nutritional etiology for their
size. But this is a false lead.
To understand why , we can look to children
in the highest oxygen tercile. They showed far
less pronounced fat increments. With adequate
compensatory mechanisms against hypoxe-
mia, they do not need tradeoffs with caloric
availability . In other words, these mostly
Tibetan children are privileging calories pre-
cisely because they have enough oxygen to do
so. Han and Tibetan children in the highest ox-
ygen and body fat terciles grow with a different
internal environment than their classmates,
whatever the altitude of their playground.
These ﬁndings are concordant with data
from metabolic studies. These ﬁnd that hypox-
emia targets leg bone growth via depressed
protein synthesis, inhibited afferent muscle
responses of mechanoreceptors, and possibly
reduced arterial blood supply (Hunter and
Clegg, 1973; Lagier-Tessonier et al., 1993;
Mori et al., 2006; Preddy et al., 1985).
Tradeoff mechanisms at the cellular level
center on the hypoxia inducible factor (HIF) a
protein that upregulates enzymes to at once
increase cell oxygen tension and decrease cell
energy utilization. HIF also induces Insulin-
like Growth Factor Binding Protein (IGFBP)
(Lampl, 2005; Lampl and Jeanty , 2003, 2004;
Lee et al., 2004; Ratcliffe et al., 1998;
Semenza, 2004). Moreover, while we have
known for some time that bone growth is sen-
sitive to local tissue anoxia (Tachakra and
Sevitte, 1975), there is now evidence that
expression of HIF subtypes differs by cell pop-
ulation (Kim et al., 2002), suggesting a proxi-
mate mechanism for targeting one bone region
over another.
It is difﬁcult to make a comparison of our
ﬁndings with other studies, not least because
they have not measured legs. Andean chil-
dren, moreover, spring from different gene
pools, and show different patterns of adapta-
tion (Beall, 2000; Beall et al., 1997a; Moore
et al., 2000). Finally , our sample appears bet-
ter nourished at the same age ranges than
most, including Weitz’s relevant Qing Hai
sample (Weitz et al., 2004, 2005).
Dung et al. (2004) found that Tibetan chil-
dren sampled throughout the country were
over 1 standard deviation below standard
CDC/WHO references for HAZ and WAZ, with
39% stunted and 24% underweight at 2 years
(see also Harris et al., 2001). Similarly ,
Weitz’s Qinghai sample’s height for our sam-
ple’s age range was 126.3 cm for Tibetans, and
128.15 for Han, compared with our children’s
136.8 and 130.6 cm. Their BMI for our age
ranges was 15.4 for Tibetans and 14.9 for
Han, compared with our sample’s 16.3 and
15.4, respectively . Their summed biceps and
triceps skinfold thicknesses was 10.6 for Tibe-
tans and 10.9 for Han, compared with our
groups 12.2 and 12.3, respectively . Our sam-
ple, then, while not afﬂuent by any measure,
may have insufﬁcient undernutrition for
nutritional variables to contribute fully to a
MANOV A model.
Nonetheless, at the least our ﬁndings call
for re-examination of the prevalent assump-
tion that hypoxia does not explain growth dif-

ferences among or within populations. Overall
size and even total leg length are strongly
inﬂuenced by blood oxygen saturation terciles
in children of comparable caloric status.
Poorly nourished children probably have
equally dismal blood oxygen saturation. Body
proportion is affected in all groups. In this
regard, neonatal studies show that disparate
levels of fetal stress—and leg growth—during
the third trimester nonetheless lead to compa-
rable birth weights and sizes (Lampl et al.,
2003). Different tradeoffs seem to operate to
conserve overall length than to promote leg
growth across the growth span.
Traditionally , such apparent conservation of
size, as well as postnatal catch-up growth,
have been seen as evidence for genetically pro-
grammed trajectories. We suspect, however,
that age appropriate stature in some subjects
could be cryptic; a sacriﬁce of long term sur-
vival for near term functionality . For instance,
shorter leg to stature proportions have been
tied recently to elevated risk of a host of adult
chronic diseases (Gunnell et al., 2003; Lawlor
et al., 2003). In this sense, stature may
follow Kuzawa’s comment about birthweight
(Kuzawa, 2005); it is not robust when used to
infer particular adaptive events. We might
then consider that stature may not be robust
when used to predict future risk proﬁles based
on that adaptive history .
Now it is reasonable to ask if, regardless of
our lower legs’ sensitivity to stress, whether
their relative length has any direct evolution-
ary value. Are they simply texts of adaptive
history? While the question is beyond the
scope of our data, it is worthwhile to note that
such texts may have considerable selective
value. Modern sexual selection theory (Daw-
kins, 1986; Hamilton and Zuk, 1982; Symons,
1979; Singh, 1993) posits that our appearance,
including body proportion, provides potential
mates with subliminal information about our
developmental history and current fecundity .
This in turn helps them grade our likely value
to producing his or her offspring, and fuels or
blocks reproductive impulses. But whatever
its possible evolutionary role, relative tibial
growth is an excellent model for investigating
adaptive responses that impact bone. The
lower leg offers a number of investigative
advantages. Not least, it is a reliable measure-
ment accessible with various methodologies in
most ﬁeld or clinical settings. Taken with stat-
ure or total leg length, leg proportions give us
a clearer sightline to the desired referent
adaptive history .
