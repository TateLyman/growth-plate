The Domain of Hypertrophic Chondrocytes in Growth Plates Growing
at Different Rates
G. J. Breur,1 M. D. Lapierre,1 K. Kazmierczak,1 K. M. Stechuchak,2 G. P. McCabe2
1Department of Veterinary Clinical Sciences, Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana 47907
2Department of Statistics, Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana 47907
Received: 2 December 1996 / Accepted: 24 March 1997
Abstract. In this study, we tested the hypotheses that (a)
both the domain volume (volume of the cell and the matrix
it has formed) and matrix volume of juxtametaphyseal hy-
pertrophic chondrocytes in the growth plate is tightly con-
trolled, and that (b) the domain volume of juxtametaphyseal
hypertrophic chondrocytes is a strong determinant of the
rate of bone length growth. We analyzed the rate of bone
length growth (oxytetracycline labeling techniques) and
nine stereologic and kinetic parameters related to the jux-
tametaphyseal chondrocytic domain in the proximal and
distal radial and tibial growth plates of 21- and 35-day-old
rats. The domain volume increased with increasing growth
rates, independent of the location of the growth plate and the
age of the animal. Within age groups, the matrix volume per
cell increased with increasing growth rates, but an identical
growth plate had the same matrix volume per cell in 21- and
35-day-old rats. The most suitable regression model ( R2 4
0.992) to describe the rate of bone length growth included
the mean volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chon-
drocytes and the mean rate of cell loss/cell proliferation.
This relationship was independent of the location of the
growth plate and the age of the animal. The data suggest that
the domain volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chon-
drocytes, as well as the matrix volume produced per cell,
may be tightly regulated. In addition, the volume of jux-
tametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes and the rate of
cell loss/rate of cell proliferation may play the most impor-
tant role in the determination of the rate of bone length
growth.
Key words: Bone length growth — Growth plate — Chon-
drocytic domain — Matrix — Hypertrophic chondrocyte.
The increase in bone length of immature long bones is
mainly the result of new cartilage formation within the
growth plate, resulting in the epiphysis moving away from
any given point of the diaphysis and subsequent elongation
of the bone (bone length growth). Simultaneously and with
approximately the same rate, the newly formed chondro-
cytes and the matrix they have produced are converted into
metaphyseal bone (endochondral ossification) [1]. Both
bone length growth and endochondral ossification are
highly organized processes and the spatial arrangement of
chondrocytes in the growth plates reflects the temporal
changes that take place during the lifetime of a chondrocyte
and its matrix. At the epiphyseal end of the growth plate,
chondrocytes divide (proliferate), form matrix, and slowly
enlarge. Toward the metaphyseal side, cells rapidly enlarge
(hypertrophy) and synthesize matrix [2–8]. In the juxta-
metaphyseal portion of the growth plate, the cartilage matrix
mineralizes and hypertrophic chondrocytes die by the pro-
cess of apoptosis [9–11].
Several hypotheses regarding the determination of the
rate of bone length growth have been proposed. Because
thicker growth plates had faster growth rates, Seinsheimer
and Sledge suggested that the rate of bone length growth is
a function of the thickness of the growth plate [12]. Kember
and other workers suggested that the rate of bone length
growth is determined by the rate of cell division in the
columns and the height (measured parallel to the long axis
of the bone) of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes
[7, 13–17]. Because of the large variety of the number of
newly produced chondrocytes in growth plates growing at
different rates and the relatively minor observed changes in
the mean height of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondro-
cytes, it has been suggested that the rate of cell division is
the principal determinant of the rate of bone length growth
[13, 14, 16–18]. Recently, a more active role of juxta-
metaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes in the determina-
tion of the rate of bone length growth has been suggested. In
a study, including 16 growth plates, spread among 4 loca-
tions, 2 species, and 2 different ages, we found a strong
correlation (rat 4 0.98; pig 4 0.83) and a positive linear
relationship between the rate of bone length growth and the
volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes
[19]. Other workers found similar relationships in rabbits,
ducklings, and chicks [20, 21]. Thus, chondrocytic enlarge-
ment may play a more important role in the determination of
the rate of bone length growth than was previously thought.
It is generally understood that bone length growth is a
function of chondrocytic proliferation, chondrocytic en-
largement, and matrix synthesis, and that both the juxta-
metaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocyte and the matrix it has
produced (chondrocytic domain) are converted into metaph-
yseal bone [1, 5, 12, 22]. However, previous studies on
determinants of the rate of bone length growth only focused
on components of the juxtametaphyseal chondrocytic do-
main, such as the height and the volume of juxtametaphy-
seal hypertrophic chondrocytes, but not on the domain itself
[13–17, 19–21]. Thus, a limitation of these studies may beCorrespondence to: G. J. Breur

Several studies have demonstrated that, in the juxta-
metaphyseal stratum of the growth plate, the ratio total vol-
ume hypertrophic chondrocytes:total volume of the stratum
(chondrocytic volume fraction) ranges from 40% to 70% [3,
5, 7, 23]. Because conversion of juxtametaphyseal growth
plate cartilage into metaphyseal bone is imminent, the dem-
onstrated ranges of the chondrocytic volume fraction imply
that the volume of the cartilage matrix contribution to lon-
gitudinal bone growth may vary from 30% to as much as
60%. We believe that it is unlikely that the important con-
tribution from cartilage matrix to bone length growth is
completely inconsequential for the determination of the rate
of bone length growth. Therefore, revisiting the role of car-
tilage matrix formation in bone length growth is indicated.
In this study, we hypothesize that (a) both the domain
volume and the volume of matrix within the domain of
juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes is tightly con-
trolled, and that (b) the domain volume of juxtametaphyseal
hypertrophic chondrocytes is a stronger determinant of the
rate of bone length growth than either the height or the
volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes.
The goal of the present study is to test these hypotheses by
analyzing the rate of bone length growth and nine param-
eters related to the juxtametaphyseal chondrocytic domain
in growth plates growing at different rates and from four
different locations in 21- and 35-day-old rats.
Animals, Materials, and Methods
Animals, Tissue Collection, and Tissue Processing
For this study, the proximal and distal radial and the proximal and
distal tibial growth plates of four 21- and four 35-day-old hooded
rats were analyzed. Both male and female animals were included
in the study and animals were randomly assigned to the 2 age
groups.
Forty-eight and 24 hours before euthanasia, animals were in-
jected with oxytetracycline hydrochloride (OTC), 1–2 mg/kg IP.
Animals were anesthetized with pentobarbital (30 mg/kg IP). Fol-
lowing exsanguination and sacrifice with an overdose of barbitu-
rate, the bones and growth plate of the left side of the animal were
collected. Immediately following isolation, growth plates of inter-
est were immersed in the fixation solution and further trimmed into
1×1×3m m blocks, containing epiphyseal bone, growth plate,
and metaphyseal bone. The fixation protocol consisted of 2% glu-
taraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.05 M cacodylate buffer
(pH 7.3) for 2 hours. Ruthenium hexammine trichloride (0.7%)
was added to preserve chondrocytes in their native, fully hydrated
state [24–27]. Following fixation, tissue blocks were rinsed in 0.1
M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.3), dehydrated in graded alcohols,
cleared in propylene oxide, and embedded in epoxy-araldite.
Growth plates of interest from the right side were removed, put in
airtight bags, and stored at −20°C until they were used for the
measurements of the rate of bone length growth. All tissue collec-
tions were performed between 9:00 and 11:00 A.M.
Stereological Analysis
Three blocks of each growth plate were randomly chosen for ste-
reologic analysis. Of each block, one 1- mm thick vertical section
(parallel to the long axis of the bone) was cut and stained with
azure II, basic fuchsine, and methylene blue [28]. Two fields per
section, chosen in an unbiased manner, were photographed and
analyzed. Each field was located along the chondro-osseous junc-
tion with the metaphysis and contained the most distal 75 mmo f
the growth plate. Thus the fields were defined independently from
the subjective morphologic criteria most commonly used to delin-
eate growth plate zones [1, 3, 5, 6]. The fields represent an unbi-
ased, repeatable measuring zone, containing profiles from chon-
drocytes in the terminal phase of hypertrophy.
Chondrocytic Volume Fraction. The chondrocytic volume frac-
tion V(c)/V(str), the ratio of the total cellular volume in the distal 75
mm of the growth plate (V(c)), and the total volume of the distal 75
mm of the growth plate (V (str)), were estimated using point-
counting techniques [4].
Mean Chondrocytic Profile Height. The mean chondrocytic pro-
file height X (vert) (profile diameter measured parallel to the long
axis of the bone) in the distal 75 mm of the growth plate was
measured from micrographs of the unbiasedly defined fields with
the aid of an image analysis system (NIH Image 1.56, NIH,
Bethesda, MD). The profile height was measured in a location
bisecting the chondrocytic profile in a plane parallel to the long
axis of the bone [2].
Mean Chondrocytic Profile Width. The mean chondrocytic profile
width X(hor) (profile diameter measured perpendicular to the long
axis of the bone) in the distal 75 mm of the growth plate was
defined as the diameter of that profile measured in a location
bisecting the chondrocytic profile in a plane perpendicular to the
long axis of the bone [2]. Profiles were measured from micro-
graphs of the unbiasedly defined fields with the aid of an image
analysis system (NIH Image 1.56, NIH, Bethesda, MD).
Mean Chondrocytic Profile Axial Ratio. The mean chondrocytic
profile axial ratio X (vert)/X(hor) in the distal 75 mm of a growth
plate was calculated by dividing the mean chondrocytic profile
height X(vert) by the mean chondrocytic profile width X (hor) [2].
Mean Chondrocytic Volume. Assuming minimal cell size variabil-
ity, the volume weighted mean cell volume v(c) in the distal 75 mm
of the growth plate was estimated using the point sampled inter-
cept technique [4, 29]. The point sampled intercept length (l o)o f
individual cell profiles was measured with the aid of an image
analysis system (Bioquant IV, R&M Biometrics Inc., Nashville,
TN) from micrographs of the unbiasedly chosen fields (800×).
Between 300 and 400 intercepts per growth plate were measured,
distributed over 16 randomly chosen angles. The forbidden line

growth plate area measured perpendicular to the long axis of the

compare the performance of growth plates from different loca-

compare the rate of cell loss at the chondro-osseous junction with
the metaphysis in the growth plates of this study, we estimate the
rate of cell loss at 1 mm2 of the chondro-osseous junction with the

converted into metaphyseal bone per mm2 growth plate area per 24

N(mm2/day) =( LG * 1m m2) / v(d) (5)
Because growth plates, over a short time period, can be considered
a steady state system with the only cell loss taking place at the
chondro-osseous junction with the metaphysis, the rate of cell loss
at the metaphyseal junction approximates the rate of cell prolif-
eration in the proliferative zone [7, 8, 13, 14, 16, 17]. Thus, the
parameter N(mm2/day) can be used to compare the rate of cell loss or
cell proliferation of growth plates with different growth plate
cross-sectional areas and different rates of bone length growth.
Statistical Analysis
The arithmetic means, standard deviations, and coefficient of
variations of all parameters for each of the studied age-matched
growth plates (8 groups) were calculated. The group means of
parameters of interest were compared using a 1-way analysis of
variance and the Bonferroni significant difference technique.
When comparisons were made between means of identical growth
plates collected at different times, a t-test for independent samples
was used [31, 32]. The relationships between the rate of bone
length growth and each of the parameters related to the chondro-
cytic domain in the distal 75 mm of the growth plate were analyzed
using least-square general linear regression techniques, including
transformations, outlier analysis, and residual analysis [33]. Three
specific questions were addressed: (a) what is the most suitable
model to predict the rate of bone length growth, (b) what is the
most suitable model to predict the domain volume of juxta-
metaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes, and (c) what is the most
suitable model to predict the volume of juxtametaphyseal hyper-
trophic chondrocytes. The most appropriate models were choosen
using Mallows’ Cp as selection criterion [31, 33]. This selection
criterion is particularly useful if there are many variables, because
it allows comparison of models with a different number of vari-
ables.

Discussion
In this study, we analyzed the domain of juxtametaphyseal
hypertrophic chondrocytes in 8 growth plates, distributed
among 4 locations in 21- and 35-day-old rats. We demon-
strated a wide range in domain volume and ultimate matrix
volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes in
growth plates growing at different rates. This suggests that
the domain volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chon-
drocytes as well as the matrix volume produced per cell may
be tightly regulated. We also demonstrated that, of the pa-
rameters characterizing the domain of juxtametaphyseal hy-
pertrophic chondrocytes, the volume of juxtametaphyseal
hypertrophic chondrocytes and the rate of cell loss/rate of
cell proliferation may play the most important role in the
determination of the rate of bone length growth. Parameters
describing chondrocytic shape, such as the traditionally
used height of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes,
were less accurate predictors of the rate of bone length
growth. All these relationships were independent of the lo-
cation of the growth plate and independent of the age of the
animal.
The Domain of Juxtametaphyseal Hypertrophic Chondrocytes
In this study, we demonstrated a wide range in the mean
domain volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondro-
cytes of growth plates growing at different rates. In addi-
tion, we found a strong positive relationship between the
mean domain volume and the rate of bone length growth
(R2 4 0.919), independent of the location of the growth
plate or the age of the animal. Thus, the results of this study
suggest that, in growth plates growing at different rates, the
development of the juxtametaphyseal domain volume is not
a random event and that regulatory mechanisms of the do-
main volume and matrix volume produced per cell in the
juxtametaphyseal portion of the growth plate may exist.
The results of this study suggest a two-level regulation of
the mean matrix volume per juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic
chondrocyte. First, within age groups, the mean matrix vol-
ume per cell was higher in growth plates growing at faster
rates, and a moderately strong positive relationship between
these parameters was found (R 2 4 0.662). Second, no sta-
tistically significant differences were found between the
mean matrix volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic
chondrocytes in identical growth plates collected at differ-
ent ages. This finding supports and generalizes the results of
a previous study restricted to the proximal tibial growth
plates in rats [7]. Together, these studies suggest that the
mean matrix volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic
chondrocytes, and thus the ultimate matrix volume pro-
duced per cell, may be predetermined and may remain con-
stant during the period of active growth of a growth plate.
It is poorly understood how the ultimate matrix volume
per growth plate chondrocyte is regulated. The matrix vol-
ume per cell is a function of matrix synthesis and matrix
swelling [3, 22]. Many putative regulators of matrix syn-
thesis have been identified in in vitro studies, however, their
efficacy in vivo has not been demonstrated [34–44]. It has
been hypothesized that the matrix swelling toward the me-
taphysis, at least in part, may be the result of decreasing
concentrations of calcium ions in unmineralized regions of
the hypertrophic zone [45]. However, control points and
regulators of matrix swelling are largely unknown.
In the 8 growth plates studied, the transformation of
juxtametaphyseal growth plate cartilage into metaphyseal
bone (endochondral ossification) occurred regardless of the
size of the juxtametaphyseal domain or the final matrix
volume per growth plate chondrocyte. This may suggest
that, just as with chondrocytic enlargement [19], the in-
crease in the domain volume and the matrix volume per cell
may be regulated independently from the regulated gene
expression of matrix constituents. Therefore, we believe
that the juxtametaphyseal chondrocytic domain volume, as
well as its components (chondrocytic volume and matrix
volume), are regulated to accomplish bone length growth,
whereas the changes in cartilage matrix during the matura-
tion process are initiated to allow the conversion of growth
plate cartilage into metaphyseal bone.
The estimated chondrocytic volume fractions in the pre-
sent study suggest that the relative matrix volume per jux-
tametaphyseal domain (1–V (c)/V(str)) ranged from 33%–

57%. The chondrocytic volume fraction was not a signifi-
cant predictor of the rate of bone length growth. Also, in the
studied growth plates, mineralization of the growth plate
occurred regardless the relative matrix volume per domain.
Thus, the relative matrix volume per juxtametaphyseal do-
main does not appear to play a role in either the rate of bone
length growth or endochondral ossification.
Finally, the results of this study suggested that the rate of
cell proliferation was the same in identical growth plates in
21- and 35-day-old rats. A limitation of this study is that the
rate of cell proliferation was estimated indirectly. However,
these results are in agreement with studies from other work-
ers done on the proximal tibial growth plate [7]. Thus, to-
gether these studies suggest that, in rats between 21 and 35
days of age, within a growth plate, the rate of chondrondo-
cytic proliferation may remain constant.
The Relationships Between the Rate of Bone Length Growth and
the Domain of Juxtametaphyseal Hypertrophic Chondrocytes
Traditionally, growth plate kinetics and performance have
been analyzed with a kinetic model using the 1-dimensional
parameter height of the juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic
chondrocyte [7, 13–17]. According to this model, the esti-
mated rate of bone length growth is the product of the
number of newly produced cells per column per day, and the
mean cell profile height (measured parallel to the long axis
of the bone) of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondro-
cytes. The 1-dimensional kinetic model is based on the un-
derlying assumption that the complete chondrocytic column
(from epiphyseal to metaphyseal end of the column) is the
functional unit of bone length growth. In addition, the col-
umn is considered a closed system where (a) the rate of cell
production at the epiphyseal end of the column is equal to
the rate of cell loss at the metaphyseal side of the column,
and (b) the number of cells per column is constant over the
period of a couple days [7, 13–17].
The 1-dimensional kinetic model has several limitations.
First, the model can only be used if the growth plate of
interest contains complete chondrocytic columns [7, 13,
14]. However, many growth plates—such as, avian growth
plates [20, 46], slow growing mammalian growth plates
[47], and growth plates from people and animals suffering
from dwarfism [23, 48, 49]—lack normal column forma-
tion. Furthermore, three studies suggest that seemingly
complete columns are often incomplete and are divided in
subunits of chondrocytes [2, 47, 50]. Thus, this underlying
assumption of the 1-dimensional model is often not ful-
filled, and special counting rules to accomodate this under-
lying assumption have been devised [46]. Second, the con-
tribution of matrix formation via transverse septa is com-
pletely ignored, and therefore this 1-dimensional model
does not acknowledge the contribution of matrix formation
to bone length growth. Third, because juxtametaphyseal
hypertrophic chondrocytes are rarely perfect spheres or
spheroids, and often have an irregular shape [2, 4, 7], the
physiologic height as related to bone length growth of jux-
tametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes or their profiles is
hard or perhaps impossible to determine [4, 7]. Finally, this
model only considers longitudinal bone growth and does not
consider the contribution of cartilage formation to growth in
width of the growth plate.
In this study, we introduced a kinetic model describing
growth plate performance in relation to bone length growth,
which makes use of the 3-dimensional parameter mean do-
main volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondro-
cytes. This is a slight modification of a model presented in
a previous study [2]. Both models are based on the notion
that the volume of newly produced bone per day is the
product of the total number of newly produced chondro-
cytes in the growth plate, the mean domain volume of jux-
tametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes, and the cross-
sectional area of the growth plate. The underlying assump-
tions in this model are that (a) the rate of cell production at
the epiphyseal end of the growth plate is equal to the rate of
cell loss at the metaphyseal side of the growth plate, and (b)
the number of cells in the growth plate is constant over the
period of a couple days [4, 5, 13, 16, 17]. The model used
in this study is independent of column kinetics and chon-
drocytic profile or cell shape and, in addition, it acknowl-
edges the contribution of cartilage matrix formation to both
longitudinal and latitudinal bone growth.
The results of our general linear regression analysis sup-
port the application of the kinetic model using 3-
dimensional parameters. Both the kinetic and the most suit-
able regression model (Model 3, including v (c) and N(mm2/
day)) consist of a factor describing the rate of cell loss/rate of
cell proliferation and a term describing the domain volume
of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes. We were
surprised that the mean volume and not the mean domain
volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes was
included in the most suitable regression model. The expla-
nation for this may be that (a) in this data set the mean
chondrocytic volume explains 98% of the variability of the
mean chondrocytic domain volume, (b) the mean matrix
volume per cell is constant in identical growth plates in
animals with different ages, whereas the chondrocytic vol-
ume and the domain volume in identical growth plates
change with age, and (c) the cross-sectional growth plate
area was not included as a factor.
It is interesting to note that the 2 suitable models to
describe the rate of bone length growth that did not include
the rate of cell loss were in fact the same model. The vari-
ables in the model describing the mean chondrocytic vol-
ume are the same variables as in the model describing the
rate of bone length growth (mean height and mean axial
ratio of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes). In
addition, if the regression equation describing the mean cell
volume (Model 5) is substituted for the mean cell volume in
the model describing the rate of bone length growth (Model
1), then the result is a model similar to Model 2. However,
the R2-value of the model using a 3-dimensional parameter
is higher (R2 4 0.950) than the R2-value of the model using
a 1-dimensional parameter (R2 4 0.929). This is additional
evidence that the mean volume of juxtametaphyseal hyper-
trophic chondrocytes may be an important determinant of
the rate of bone length growth.
The present study also suggested that, as expected, the
mean domain volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic
chondrocytes was best predicted with the mean chondro-
cytic volume and the mean matrix volume per cell (Model
4). A model including the mean height of hypertrophic
chondrocytes had a high C p-value and was unsuitable to
predict the domain volume of juxtametaphyseal hypertro-
phic chondrocytes. In addition, the mean height of hyper-
trophic chondrocytes by itself also was unable to predict the
mean cell volume appropriately. Thus, this may suggest that
it is unlikely that the mean height of juxtametaphyseal hy-
pertrophic chondrocytes is an indicator of either the mean
chondrocytic domain volume or the mean chondrocytic vol-
ume itself.

Conclusions
This study’s contribution to the understanding of mecha-
nisms of bone length growth is twofold. First, we demon-
strated that the domain volume and the mean matrix volume
per cell in the juxtametaphyseal portion is tightly regulated
and that the ultimate matrix volume produced per cell may
remain constant during the period of active growth of a
growth plate. Second, we introduced a new kinetic model
describing the rate of bone length growth and provided evi-
dence that the rate of cell proliferation and the volume of
juxtametaphyseal hypertrophic chondrocytes may play the
most important role in the determination of the rate of bone
length growth. The results of this study do not support the
application of the traditionally used kinetic model to de-
scribe the rate of bone length growth, because models using
the 1-dimensional parameter mean height of juxtametaphy-
seal hypertrophic chondrocytes were unsuitable or not as
robust as models using 3-dimensional parameters. These
findings suggest a need for a better understanding of kinetic
models used to describe the rate of bone length growth.
Such studies are important, because they may allow better
analyses of growth plate performance following chemical,
physical, or genetic perturbations, which may result in a
better understanding of and novel approaches to the treat-
ment of growth plate disorders.
