Species diversity in limb lengths and proportions is thought to have evolved adaptively in the context of locomotor and habitat
specialization, but the heritable cellular processes that drove this evolution within species are poorly understood. In this study,
we take a novel “micro-evo-devo” approach, using artiﬁcial selection on relative limb length to amplify phenotypic variation in a
population of mice, known as Longshanks, to examine the cellular mechanisms of postnatal limb development that contribute to
intraspeciﬁc limb length variation. Cross-sectional growth data indicate that differences in bone length between Longshanks and
random-bred controls are not due to prolonged growth, but to accelerated growth rates. Histomorphometric and cell proliferation
assays on proximal tibial growth plates show that Longshanks’ increased limb bone length is associated with an increased
number of proliferative chondrocytes. In contrast, we ﬁnd no differences in other growth plate cellular features known to
underlie interspeciﬁc differences in limb bone size and shape, such as the rates of chondrocyte proliferation or the size and
number of hypertrophic cells in the growth plate. These data suggest that small differences among individuals in the number of
proliferating chondrocytes are a potentially important determinant of selectable intraspeciﬁc variation in individual limb bone
lengths, independent of body size.
KEY WORDS: Artiﬁcial selection, chondrocyte proliferation, chondrocyte hypertrophy, growth plates, limb development, Long-
shanks, micro-evo-devo.
Across mammals there is great diversity in limb bone lengths and
proportions, which are thought to evolve adaptively in the con-
text of specific locomotor behaviors and environments (Biewener
1983; Kear et al. 2008; Y oung et al. 2010; Y oung 2013). Mech-
anistically, this diversity is likely the outcome of the long-term
action of natural selection on standing phenotypic and genetic
variation in limb bone length, which is a highly heritable trait
(Norgard et al. 2008; Marchini et al. 2014). This skeletal varia-
tion within populations is itself the product of small shifts in the
processes of limb development. Evolutionary developmental biol-
ogy (evo-devo) provides a useful framework to study limb length
evolution, and this comparative approach has shed light on the
cell and molecular basis for major evolutionary novelties such as
digits and wings (Rolian 2014). However, evo-devo, as currently
practiced, provides limited insight into the developmental basis
of intraspecific limb length variation available to natural selec-
tion. Limb diversity between species must ultimately derive from
intraspecific phenotypic variation, particularly in developmental
mechanisms of bone formation and growth (i.e., micro-evo-devo)
(Nunes et al. 2013). However, developmental variation in com-
plex traits (such as limb bone length) is difficult to study in natural
populations, because the expected effect sizes are often smaller
than measurement error, and therefore require impractically large
sample sizes to achieve even minimal analytical resolution.
The main cellular mechanisms that drive limb develop-
ment and elongation are well established, but how these are

modulated to produce limb length variation is still not fully un-
derstood (Li et al. 2015). Limb bones develop by endochondral
ossification. This process involves the formation of a cartilage
model (anlagen) of mesenchymal cells, which undergo differenti-
ation into osteoblasts and chondrocytes, with the formation of pri-
mary and secondary ossification centers separated by the growth
plate (Mackie et al. 2008). The growth plate is composed of three
distinct cellular zones (resting, proliferating and hypertrophic)
reflecting different stages in the life cycle of chondrocytes, and
plays a key role in the ossification and elongation of long bones.
Small stem-like cells in the resting zone regulate the production of
proliferative chondrocytes and maintain spatial orientation of the
growth plate (Abad et al. 2002). These cells undergo differentia-
tion to become proliferative chondrocytes, which are organized in
columns parallel to the direction of longitudinal growth. Follow-
ing a number of rounds of cell division, these cells differentiate
into hypertrophic chondrocytes. Hypertrophic chondrocytes in-
crease in volume, produce, and remodel extracellular matrix rich
in Collagen X, and eventually undergo programmed cell death,
leaving behind a scaffold to be replaced with mineralized bone
matrix by osteoblasts (Mackie et al. 2008). Some recent studies
have also suggested that a portion of the growth plate hypertrophic
chondrocytes do not undergo programmed cell death, but rather
transdifferentiate into osteoblasts (Bahney et al. 2014; Zhou et al.
2014).
Several cellular mechanisms influence the rate of longitudi-
nal growth at the growth plate, and hence the final length of the
limb bones: the number and cell-cycle duration of proliferative
chondrocytes, the number and size of the hypertrophic chondro-
cytes, and the rate of synthesis and degradation of the hypertrophic
matrix (Hunziker and Schenk 1989; Wilsman et al. 1996a; Rolian
2008). V ariation in these mechanisms is a major determinant of
variation within and between species in limb bone length. For ex-
ample, some evo-devo studies have shown a role for hypertrophic
chondrocyte size in determining size of different individual skele-
tal elements (Wilsman et al. 1996a) and in extreme skeletal phe-
notypes (Cooper et al. 2013), while others show that the number
of proliferative chondrocytes underlie interspecific bone length
diversity (Kember 1993; Rolian 2008). In addition, variation in
the duration of growth across individual bones, and/or the timing
of growth plate senescence, can contribute to differences in length
at skeletal maturity (S ´anchez-Villagra 2002; Rolian 2008; Geiger
et al. 2014). Although such mechanisms are clearly important in
determining species differences in the length of specific bones,
and differences within species between limb bones (e.g., humerus
vs femur, Rolian 2008), it is not clear which, if any, contribute
to intraspecific length variation in a given limb bone. The ex-
treme morphologies of evo-devo models such as bat and jerboa
may represent evolutionary novelties that are not representative
of the type of standing genetic variation available to selection
within most natural populations. Similarly, comparisons between
separate elements within a single skeleton (e.g., radius vs tibia)
are based on the untested assumption that similar selectable gene
networks and cellular processes underlie the development and
adult phenotype of each bone. Ultimately, if we want to under-
stand more fully the developmental basis of limb length evolution
and diversification, then we must ask directly which develop-
mental mechanisms generate intraspecific variation in individual
elements.
We used artificial selection to increase tibia length relative to
body mass in two parallel lines of mice bred from outbred CD1
stock (Longshanks (Marchini et al. 2014)), thereby amplifying
heritable intraspecific variation in the length of a single limb bone
to empirically tractable levels. By comparing these unique mice
with random-bred controls from the same genetic background,
this approach allowed us to investigate the cellular determinants
of intraspecific limb bone length variation, and how these re-
spond to selection at a microevolutionary level (i.e., micro-evo-
devo) (Nunes et al. 2013; Fern´andez-Mazuecos and Glover 2017).
After 14 generations of selective breeding, Longshanks mice show
/223c13% increase in tibia length compared to random-bred controls
at sexual maturity.
In this study, we tested the hypothesis that the increased limb
bone length in Longshanks is reflected in the growth plates by
at least one of four, nonmutually exclusive mechanisms: (1) a
prolonged duration of linear growth (2) increases in chondrocyte
number, (3) increases in hypertrophic chondrocyte size, and/or (4)
increases in proliferation rate during endochondral ossification of
the limb.
Materials and Methods
SAMPLE COLLECTION
All procedures involving animals were approved by the Univer-
sity of Calgary Health Sciences Animal Care Committee (protocol
AC13-0077). The animals were humanely euthanized and stored
at –20°C or immediately processed for histology, as needed (see
below). We used animals from the Longshanks artificial selection
experiment from multiple generations, depending on the specific
experiment (see below for details). This artificial selection ex-
periment consists of three independent lines of CD1 mice bred
under similar conditions for the same number of generations: two
lines artificially selected for long tibia relative to body mass (LS1
and LS2), and a random-bred control line (Control) (Marchini
et al. 2014). Breeders were selected at eight weeks, at which point
longitudinal tibia growth is over 97% complete (Farooq et al.
2017, see further). Longshanks mice at generation F14 showed
an increase of 9–13% in tibia length relative to body mass. For
additional information see Marchini et al. (2014).

SENESCENCE
We recently published cross-sectional growth curves of tibia
length in Longshanks and Control mice (Farooq et al. 2017).
Briefly, we used μCT scans to measure tibia length at multiple
developmental stages ranging from 0 to 150 days postnatal. Next,
we fit the cross-sectional data, in each line separately, with a Gom-
pertz logistic curve (German et al. 1994; Rolian 2008). We used
these curves and their first derivatives, which represent growth
rates, to determine whether the tibiae of LS1, LS2, and Control
mice complete growth at the same age, and to identify the age of
most rapid linear growth. Based on growth cessation data obtained
from these curves, we selected two additional developmental time
points, at postnatal days 42 and 75, to examine growth plate struc-
ture across the lines at these ages, using histological procedures
described below.
MICRO-COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY
To investigate differences in tibia length and thickness of growth
plate zones, we compared generation F15 and F16 LS1, LS2, and
Control using μCT scanning and histomorphometry. We used
mice at postnatal stage P14 (LS1 n = 16 LS2 n = 17, Con-
trol n = 16). Whole carcasses were thawed at room temperature
and scanned using a SkyScan 1173 μCt scanner (Bruker, Kon-
tich, Belgium), using appropriate scan parameters for each mouse
(isotropic voxel size 29.8 μm, x-ray 70 kV , 90–114μA, no filter).
Scans were reconstructed as tomographic stacks using NRECON
v1.6.9 (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) and linear measurements were
derived by placing digital landmarks on homologous anatomi-
cal features across individuals (i.e., type I landmarks (Bookstein
1991)), using the 3D landmarking tool in AMIRA v.5.4.2 (Visage
Imaging, Berlin, Germany). Specifically, to measure tibia length,
we placed two digital landmarks on homologous sites of the left
tibia: one on the anterior-most lip of the proximal epiphysis, and
one at the most distal point on the medial malleolus. To measure
the thickness of the proximal growth plate, we placed two ad-
ditional landmarks at the center of the growth plate: one on the
distal part of the growth plate on the surface of the metaphysis,
and the second on the proximal the growth plate on the surface of
the secondary ossification center.
HISTOMORPHOMETRY
After scanning, left and right tibiae from P14 mice were fixed
in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) for 4–5 days at 4 °C. The tibiae
were then decalcified using Poly-NoCal (Polysciences, Inc.), with
solution changed every 12–24 hours until decalcified (from 3 to
5 days), as determined using the Poly-NoCal End Point Determi-
nation Kit (Polysciences, Inc.). Proximal tibial growth plates were
dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned in the coronal
plane at 5 μm. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, stained
with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Sigma), 1% Alcian blue in 1% acetic
acid (Sigma), 1% Phosphomolybdic acid (Sigma), 0.5% Sirius
red (Direct Red 80, Sigma) in 0.5% acetic acid and then cover
slipped. The stained sections were imaged at 5 × and 20 × mag-
nification using a digital microscope (AxioPlan 2, Zeiss) with
attached camera (Optronics), using Stereo Investigator v7 (MBF
BioScience, Williston, VT). We selected the central-most coronal
section of the growth plate along the cranio-caudal axis for each
specimen. In each section, we measured the total growth plate
thickness, resting, proliferative, and hypertrophic thickness at
5 × magnification. For each variable, we repeated measurements
10 times across the section and took the average of these mea-
surements. Within the growth plate, we determined the boundary
between resting and proliferative zones as the first appearance of
well-organized columns with abundant matrix between columns,
and the boundary between proliferative and hypertrophic zones as
increase of chondrocyte volume and reduction of matrix between
columns. We measured the most distal hypertrophic chondrocyte
height (in the direction of longitudinal growth) and counted the
number of proliferative and hypertrophic chondrocytes per col-

taken using the Straight Line and Cell Counter tools in ImageJ
v1.48 (Schneider et al. 2012).
For all histological images, group identification was removed
from each image by giving it a random identification code consist-
ing of a series of 4–6 digits, and then the images were measured
by M.M.
We calculated the means of the measures taken in differ-
ent regions of the growth plate for each sample before statistical
analysis. To estimate measurement error, we collected measure-
ments of all metrics for a subset of five images in four different
measurement periods and calculated the coefficient of variation
for each metric. We found measurement errors to be well below
the differences we found between groups (Table S1). Cell count
and measurements were collected for the most complete straight
chondrocytes columns and distal hypertrophic chondrocyte of the
proximal growth plate. The height of the most distal hypertrophic
chondrocytes adjacent to the chondro-osseous junction was taken
from at least 15 terminal chondrocytes in each sample (mean: 26,
range: 15–52 cells); an average of 24 separate columns for each
sample (range: 10–40 columns) were used to count the number
of proliferative chondrocytes; an average of 26 columns for each
sample (range: 11–50 columns) were used to count the number
of hypertrophic chondrocytes. To estimate measurement error, we
collected measurements of a subset of three growth plates on three
separate days and calculated the percent coefficient of variation.
We found measurement error to be below the differences we found
between groups (Table S1). We similarly collected measurements
of five terminal hypertrophic chondrocytes for a subset of four
images on four separate days (Table S1).

CELL PROLIFERATION ASSA Y
To measure the number of proliferative chondrocytes actively
dividing in the proximal growth plate, we injected specimens in-
traperitoneally with 5-Bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (Sigma) in PBS at
50 μg/g of body weight (Wojtowicz and Kee 2006). 5-Bromo-
2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU) is incorporated into DNA during the S
phase of the cell cycle. For each line (LS1, LS2, and Control),
P14 mice were euthanized two ( n = 10, generation F19), six
(n = 8, generation F19), or 24 (n = 8, generation F23) hours after
BrdU injection. Tibiae from the two- and six-hour treatments were
harvested, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) for 4 to
5d a y sa t4°C. The tibiae were then treated as above and sectioned
at 8 μm. Tibiae from the 24-hour treatment were harvested, fixed
in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for approximately 48 hours at
room temperature and then decalcified using Cal-Ex TM II (Fisher
Scientific) for 3 days with solution changes every 12 hours.
The proximal tibial growth plates of all specimens were dehy-
drated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned in the coronal plane at
8 μm. Paraffin embedded sections were deparaffinized in xylene,
treated with 0.5%pepsin/0.05HCl (Sigma) at room temperature
for 15 minutes for antigen retrieval and with hydrogen peroxide
(3% in methanol) for 20 minutes to block endogenous peroxidase.
Sections were then blocked with 10% goat serum/1%BSA/PBS
for an hour at room temperature and incubated with rat mon-
oclonal anti-BrdU antibody (1:100, AbD Serotec) overnight at
4°C. After washes, the sections were incubated with biotiny-
lated anti-rat IgG (1:200, V ector Laboratories) for an hour, and
then treated with streptavidin horseradish (1:150, Invitrogen) for
30 minutes. Liquid DAB substrate kit was used to detect the BrdU
signal (Invitrogen) and hematoxylin was used as a counterstain.
We selected the central-most coronal section of the growth plate
along the cranial-caudal (i.e., anterior-posterior) axis for each
specimen. Positive sections were imaged at 20 × as above. We
counted the number of positive BrdU-stained chondrocytes and
the total number of proliferative chondrocytes in a given column.
We then calculated the mean labeling index (number of BrdU pos-
itive cells per column divided by the total number of proliferative
chondrocytes per column) for each growth plate. The measure-
ments were taken using the Cell Counter tools in ImageJ v1.48
(Schneider et al. 2012).
As above, group identification was removed from each image
by giving it a random identification code consisting of a series of
4–6 digits, and then measured by M.M. We calculated the means
of the measures taken in different regions of the growth plate for
each sample before statistical analysis. An average of 21 columns
for each sample (range: 10–41 columns) were used to count the
number of the BrdU positive cells and number of proliferative
chondrocytes per column. We calculated the labeling index for
each sample as the mean number of BrdU positive cells divided
by the mean number of proliferative chondrocytes. To estimate
our measurement error, we collected measurements subset of four
individuals of the two-hour groups in three different measurement
periods and calculated the percent coefficient of variation. We
found measurement errors to be well below the differences we
found between groups (Table S1).

The histology of LS1, LS2, and Control at postnatal stage

The approximate cell division rate, also known as the label-

The cell cycle is divided into four main phases: G1, S, G2, and
M phase. In rats, a proliferative chondrocyte takes approximately
eight hours to transition from S to M phase (Farnum and Wils-

differences were found between the three groups. We found no

secondary ossification centers.

PROLIFERATION AND HYPERTROPHY IN LIMB
LENGTH EVOLUTION
Previous studies that have compared bones elongating at differ-
ent rates, within and between species, have found an important
role for hypertrophic chondrocyte size, hypertrophic zone size,
and hypertrophic zone turnover in determining limb proportions,
that is driving differences in length between limb bones within
a species, but also homologous bones between species (Wilsman
et al. 1996a; Sears et al. 2006; Cooper et al. 2013). For exam-
ple, at comparable early embryonic stages, forelimb digits in bats
and mice have similar cartilage condensation size, but bat fore-
limb digits dramatically increase in size through enlargement of
the hypertrophic zone and increases of the proliferation rate in
later stages of embryogenesis (Sears et al. 2006). This effect is
forelimb-specific; by two days postnatal, metacarpal, and fore-
limb phalanges of the Big Brown Bat ( Eptesicus fuscus )h a v e
thicker proliferative and hypertrophic zones with larger hyper-
trophic chondrocytes compared to homologous metatarsal and
hindlimb phalanges (Farnum et al. 2008).
Chondrocyte hypertrophy also plays an important role in
the dramatic metatarsal elongation in the lesser Egyptian jerboa
(Cooper et al. 2013), which accomplishes most of its hindlimb
elongation postnatally. Cooper at al. (2013) found that, in com-
parison with mouse, the Jerboa has a thicker growth plate and
zones with greatly enlarged hypertrophic chondrocytes. Hyper-
trophic chondrocytes in the jerboa increase their volume through
three different phases: (1) cellular increases in dry mass and fluid
(true hypertrophy), (2) cellular swelling, increasing the intake of
fluid with consequential diminution of dry mass, and (3) further
increases in volume and dry mass. Truncation or extension of the
third phase produces differences in hypertrophic chondrocyte size
between jerboas and mice (Cooper et al. 2013).
Although our data suggest that hypertrophic zone turnover
rates must be higher in Longshanks, we found no difference in the
cellularity of the hypertrophic zone, or in the size of the terminal
hypertrophic chondrocytes (Fig. 2). Thus, unlike in jerboa, the
third phase of hypertrophy does not appear to have been extended
in either Longshanks line. These observations suggest that, while
hypertrophy is important in driving length differences between
bones within a species (e.g., manus vs pes (Farnum et al. 2008)),
or homologous bones between species (Kirkwood and Kember
1993), variation in the size and cellularity of the proliferative

zone also contributes significantly to selectable length variation
among individuals in specific limb bones in this model organism.
In this context, it is noteworthy that proliferative zone thick-
ness and proliferative cell count are also greater in the elongated
elements of bats and jerboa, where increases in hypertrophic chon-
drocyte size have been reported (Sears et al. 2006; Cooper et al.
2013), as well as in elements that grow to be absolutely longer at
skeletal maturity within a species (Wilsman et al. 1996a). In fact,
both proliferation and hypertrophic chondrocyte parameters are
generally strongly positively correlated with the length at skeletal
maturity in most mammalian systems studied to date (Farnum
2006). This suggests that numbers of proliferative chondrocytes
and hypertrophic chondrocyte size evolve largely in tandem to
produce diversity in limb proportions at macroevolutionary scales.
The power of the Longshanks mouse, however, is to show that, at
least in this model and experimental system, changes in the char-
acteristics of the proliferative zone were sufficient to generate
selectable changes in individual limb bone length in two indepen-
dently selected lines (Atchley and Hall 1991). This is consistent
with the well-established principle in organismal development
that increases in body and organ size are more likely to happen
through increases in cell number rather than cell size (Levi 1925;
Tessier 1939).
Based on our data, we propose a model of limb length evo-
lution where initial phases of phyletic length increase of spe-
cific bones, for example within populations, occur mainly through
changes in proliferation, specifically in the pool of potentially or
actively mitotic cells, whereas increases in hypertrophic cell size
are later implemented to achieve greater elongation of limb ele-
ments (Fig. 4). Due to the limited taxonomic scope of this study,
this model may be provisionally restricted to mice, or to rodents
more generally. Broader taxonomic sampling, especially among
closely related species with both modest and extreme differences
in limb bone length, could test the predictions and applicability
of this model across mammals.
Our model reflects the possible existence of physiological
and mechanical constraints on the maximum size of the growth
plate (Fig. 4). These constraints could be physiological (e.g., dif-
fusion of oxygen in avascular cartilage) (Williams et al. 2007),
or physical due to the size of its bone elements or absolute body
size (Villemure and Stokes 2009). Hormones, oxygen, signaling
factors, and other molecules diffuse into the growth plate from the
perichondrium and vascular sites in the metaphysis and epiphysis.
Because the center of the growth plate is less permeable than the
periphery and the resting zone is less permeable than the pro-
liferative and hypertrophic zones (Villemure and Stokes 2009),
both the absolute width of the growth plate and the proportionate
sizes of the zones may have physiological limits. Increased hyper-
trophic cell size may help to overcome these limits by reducing the
number of cells producing an equivalent amount of ECM during a
given interval. However, mechanical load can also influence chon-
drocyte differentiation (Villemure et al. 2005; Stokes et al. 2007),
particularly within the less rigid hypertrophic zone, so in longi-
tudinal increases in growth plate size (and therefore growth plate
zone size), the hypertrophic zone may be more prone to deforma-
tion due to high bending loads (Sergerie et al. 2009; Villemure
and Stokes 2009; Sinclair et al. 2014). In addition, the risk of
mechanical failure of the growth plate itself may be a major con-
straint on growth plate zone size. Larger animals tend to have a
larger resting zone to provide mechanical support as it has greater
mechanical strength due to the higher matrix/cell volume ratio
compared to proliferative and hypertrophic zones (Sergerie et al.
2009). We note that, in addition to increased hypertrophy, both
bat and jerboas exhibit anatomical traits and behaviors that re-
duce the load on the developing growth plate. Extreme metatarsal
bone length in jerboa is associated with metatarsal fusion (Moore
et al. 2015) that may increase growth plate width and therefore
strength to compensate for the less rigid hypertrophic zone. In
bats, ossification of the autopod is completed early (Koyabu and
Son 2014) and the forelimb reaches adult size prior to initiation of
flight and therefore wing loading (Jones 1967; Swift 2001). This
ontogenetic delay in flight may help to protect the growth plate
from potentially harmful mechanical loads.
Our data suggest that absolute number of proliferative chon-
drocytes in the growth plate and/or its cartilaginous precursor
was more responsive to selection for longer limb bones than other
mechanisms for limb elongation in these mice, at least over the
first few generations of directional selection. Infinite increase of
longitudinal growth plate size is impossible, however. Hence, to
develop longer elements and extreme phenotypes such as those
seen in jerboas and bats, other mechanisms may be recruited to in-
crease longitudinal bone growth without increasing growth plate
size for a given body size. The cellular mechanisms of limb elon-
gation observed in our Longshanks selection experiment may thus
reflect an important, and sufficient, developmental mechanism to
drive adaptive limb evolution among closely related natural pop-
ulations. If true, then extreme limb bone lengths relative to body
size, such as those driven by cell hypertrophy in bats and jerboa
may represent extreme modifications to a developmental program
initiated by variation in cell population size among individuals.

MACROEVOLUTIONARY HYPOTHESES

proliferating chondrocytes as a key cellular mechanism driving
evolutionary change in the length of the tibia within populations.
However, this is one of several complementary developmental
mechanisms that can influence the final length of a bone, includ-
ing cellular hypertrophy, the duration of individual phases in the
cell cycle, and/or the duration of growth. Hence, it is possible that

For example, the CD-1 stock from which all three populations in

other Longshanks limb bones that have also increased in length

these cellular mechanisms, and their contributions to micro- and
macroevolutionary variation in limb size and shape.
Our results, which complement previous studies of natural
variation in bone development between species, show that there
are more pathways by which individual limb bone morphologies
can evolve than previously appreciated, and we suggest some of
these pathways may be more or less likely due to specific fac-
tors such as biomechanical constraints on growth plate fracture,
physiological constraints on diffusion within the avascular growth
plate, and ultimately, to the nature and magnitude of selectable
(co)variation in developmental genetic determinants of chondro-
cyte function. Selection experiments such as ours thus provide a
unique opportunity to more fully understand how complex quanti-
tative traits are determined by cellular and tissue-level processes,
and how these processes ultimately influence the paths by which
selection leads to macroevolutionary diversity.
