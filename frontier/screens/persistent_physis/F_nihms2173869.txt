Abstract
Hereditary Multiple Osteochondromas (HMO) is a rare, pediatric disease characterized by 
osteochondromas that form next to the growth plates, accumulate over time and cause major 
health problems. Most HMO cases are linked to loss-of-function mutations in Golgi-resident 
proteins EXT1 or EXT2, but tumor development remains unclear. In particular, the source of 
tumor-forming progenitors has long been debated, with recent studies pointing to a perichondrial 
origin. Perichondrium normally flanks the growth plates, has essential roles in skeletal growth, 
and is composed of an inner cuboidal cell layer and an outer fibroblastic cell layer. Here, we set 
out to determine by genetic approaches whether the tumors do in fact derive from perichondrium 
and if one or both layers provide progenitors. We employed Pdgfrα-CreER and Fgf18-CreER 
transgenic mice that respectively, target inner and outer layers or the outer layer only. Mice 
were mated with floxed Ext1 mice to conditionally ablate the causative gene, mimicking tumor 
induction in patients. Compound juvenile Pdgfrα-CreER;Ext1f/f and Fgf18-CreER;Ext1f/f mice 
were injected with tamoxifen and monitored for tumor development over time. By 4–8 weeks 
post-tamoxifen, osteochondromas had formed in Pdgfrα;Ext1 mutants targeting both layers, but 
none were appreciable in Fgf18;Ext1 mutants targeting the outer layer, based on μCT scans, 
histochemistry and td-Tomato cell lineage tracing. In situ analyses verified that Ext1 had been

1. Introduction
Hereditary Multiple Osteochondromas (HMO) is a rare pediatric skeletal disease that is 
characterized by formation and accumulation of cartilaginous-osseous tumors at multiple 
locations along the skeleton in young and adolescent patients [1, 2]. Known also as 
Hereditary Multiple Exostoses or Multiple Osteochondromas, HMO has a global incidence 
of 1:50,000 newborns and can bring about severe health problems over postnatal time 
including bone deformities, joint functional defects, limb length discrepancies, chronic pain 
and quality of life challenges [3]. When mature and established, the osteochondromas 
display a distal cartilaginous portion and a proximal bony base attached perpendicular 
to the skeletal element from which they originate. Most HMO patients are born with a 
heterozygous loss-of-function mutation in tumor suppressor genes EXT1 or EXT2 that 
encode Golgi enzymes responsible for the synthesis of heparan sulfate (HS) [4–6]. Studies 
in patients have shown that the congenital heterozygosity in EXT1 or EXT2 is not sufficient 
to cause the formation of osteochondromas and a full-blown HMO disease course, but 
additional somatic genomic changes are required [7–9] in line with the second-hit theory of 
tumorigenesis [10]. Among putative stochastic changes, loss-of-heterozygosity (LOH) has 
been documented in the osteochondromas of some patients, accompanied by a near total 
local loss of HS [11, 12]. Mouse studies have provided further support for these notions. 
Whereas heterozygous Ext1 or Ext2 mutant mice are largely normal [13, 14], mice in 
which both Ext1 alleles were conditionally deleted using drivers such as Col2-CreER or 
Acan-CreER developed multiple osteochondromas in long bones, ribs and other skeletal 
elements over time [15–17]. The same was observed in a unique conditional transgenic 
mouse model designed to mimic patient’s stochastic loss of both EXT1 alleles [18], a mouse 
model used in the present study also.
The above synopsis makes it clear that much has been learned about the genetics of 
HMO over the years. However, other fundamental aspects remain poorly understood, 
limiting the ability to delineate HMO pathogenesis and in turn design cell-, drug- or 
gene-based therapies, still unavailable for this disease at present [19]. One such key aspect 
is the origin and properties of cells initiating osteochondroma formation. Because the 
tumors invariably and exclusively start forming next to the growth plates, studies initially 
concluded that the osteochondroma-forming cells are chondrocytes located at the outer

In subsequent studies, we proposed that perichondrium may represent the main initial

cell contributions to osteochondroma genesis was reached by others more recently [16]. This 
important issue, however, is far from settled.
Perichondrium is a subtle but critical structure. Embryologists first realized many decades 
ago that all cartilaginous structures are surrounded by a fibrous perichondrial sheath [22]. 
Upon further analysis, perichondrium was revealed to mainly consist of an inner layer of 
cuboidal mesenchymal cells facing the cartilaginous growth plate and an outer layer of 
elongated fibroblastic cells facing surrounding tissues [23]. Like analogous cells present 
in periosteum, inner cuboidal cells are thought to have generative capacity and/or border 
function, whereas the outer layer of elongated fibroblastic cells would exert mechanical and 
structural roles [24–26]. Original analyses of cDNA libraries of perichondrium followed 
by in situ hybridization showed that inner and outer perichondrial layers possess distinct 
molecular signatures including expression of genes such as Col2A, Dkk3 and Tsp2 in 
inner layer and Crabp1, Abi3bp and Undulin in outer layer [27, 28]. Signals emanating 
from perichondrium and influencing the growth plate include those exerted by Tgfβ2 and 
Fgf18 [29–31]. The latter signaling protein has been shown to cross talk with its receptor 
Fgfr3 expressed in proliferating chondrocytes in growth plate and to limit chondrocyte 
mitotic activity [32], and gain-of-function mutations in FGFR3 cause achondroplasia, 
the most severe form of human growth retardation [33]. Given the unresolved questions 
on perichondrium in HMO pathogenesis and the enduring interest in perichondrium’s 
physiological and pathological roles in other conditions as well [26, 34, 35], here we set out 
to further define its roles in the initiation of osteochondromas, using the floxed Ext1 mice 
mimicking stochastic loss of both alleles seen in patient osteochondromas. Our data provide 
evidence that inner perichondrium may represent a main source of progenitors responsible 
for the initiation of osteochondroma formation.
2. Materials and methods
2.1 Genetic mouse models
All mouse procedures were conducted after review and approval by the Institutional Animal 
Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. Mice were 
housed in groups of up to five animals and kept at 21° to 23°C on a 12-hour light/dark 
cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Transgenic mice carrying an inducible loss-
of-function allele of Ext1 in which exon 2 is flanked by head-to-head loxP sites (Ext1f/f) 
were described previously [36] and obtained at The Jackson Laboratory (catalog no. 09326). 
Mice harboring the Pdgfrα-CreERT2 knock-in allele were previously described [37] and 
purchased from Jackson Laboratory (catalog no. 032770), whereas Fgf18-CreER mice were 
generously provided by David Ornitz [38]. Ai9/R26-tdTomato reporter mice were obtained 
from The Jackson Laboratory (catalog no. 007909).

2.2 Micro-computed tomography
Hindlimb samples were harvested and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (NBF) for 3 
days at 4°C then washed in 1X phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and stored in 70% ethanol. 
Samples were imaged using a μCT45 scanner (Scanco Medical AG) and analyzed using 
the manufacturer’s issued software. Serial images were acquired at 55-kilovolt peak energy 
and 145-μA intensity at 7.4-μm resolution and 400-ms integration time. Raw μCT data were 
compiled for 3D reconstruction using Scanco software.
2.3 Histochemical Analysis
Following fixation as described above, hindlimb samples were decalcified for 2 weeks in 
14% EDTA. Tissues were processed, embedded in paraffin and sectioned sagittally from the 
medial side at 5 μm thickness. Sections were stained with Safranin O/Fast Green to visualize 
bone, cartilage and soft tissues within and around the knee joint. For experiments involving 
tdTomato, samples were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified for 5 days and 
cryoprotected in 30% sucrose overnight before frozen sectioning using Kawamoto tape at 
10 μm thickness. Sections were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). 
Brightfield images were taken with a Nikon Eclipse Ci equipped with a Nikon camera 
operated with NIS-Elements software. Fluorescent images were taken with a Leica SP8 
confocal microscope and acquired with LAS software. The DAPI and tdTomato images were 
merged in Adobe Photoshop.
2.4 Immunohistochemistry and quantification using ImageJ
Immunohistochemistry was performed following a general protocol with antibody-specific 
antigen retrieval procedures and antibody details provided in Table S1. Sections were 
deparaffinized, rehydrated and air-dried, then rinsed in deionized water and permeabilized 
in 0.1% Triton™X-100 in 1X PBS. After a second rinse in deionized water, sections were 
blocked in 2.5% normal donkey serum for 30 minutes at room temperature and incubated 
with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Following washes in 1X PBS containing tween 
20 (PBST), sections were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 60 
minutes at room temperature. Sections were then rinsed in PBST, incubated in DAPI for 
10 minutes at room temperature and mounted with Prolong Gold Antifade mounting reagent. 
The AF555 Tyramide SuperBoost™ Kit (catalog no. B40923) was used for pSMAD1 and 
pSMAD2 detection. Negative control slides were prepared using species-matched IgG at the

number of pSMAD1+ and pSMAD2+ cells, expressed as a percentage of total DAPI+ nuclei

3. Results
3.1 Cell lineage reporter tracing of inner and outer perichondrial cells
At the outset of this study, we searched for potential transgenic CreER mouse drivers that 
could target perichondrium and possibly its main cell layers separately. Platelet-derived 
growth factor α (Pdgfrα) has emerged as a marker of progenitor cells in several tissues 
and structures including skeletal muscles, bone marrow, adipose tissue and periosteum [39, 
40]. Pdgfrα also marks cells in periosteum layers involved in bone fracture repair [41, 42]. 
Given the biological analogies of periosteum and perichondrium, we considered it possible 
for Pdgfrα to mark perichondrial cells as well. Fibroblast growth factor 18 (Fgf18) was 
originally found to be expressed by the perichondrium and also by progenitor cells at the 
interface between opposing developing long bone joints [30, 32]. As pointed out above, 
Fgf18 is known to be a major regulator of skeletal development and a coordinator of 
chondrogenesis and osteogenesis, in particular serving as a ligand for Fgfr3 to limit and 
set growth plate chondrocyte proliferation rates [43, 44]. Considering all the above, we 
tested the ability of Pdgfrα-CreERT2 and Fgf18-CreERT2 transgenic mice [37, 38] to target 
perichondrium and/or its layers in mice at a juvenile age when they are still actively growing 
[45] and mimic the major osteochondroma formation period in children [17, 21].
The two lines of CreER mice were mated with R26ROSA-tdTomatof/f reporter mice 
(heretofore termed Td). The resulting compound Pdgfrα-CreER+/−;Td and Fgf18-CreER+/
−;Td mice at 5 weeks of age were injected with tamoxifen twice (100 mg/kg) or (150 mg/
kg), respectively and analyzed over 4–8 weeks. Long bones were harvested and processed 
for histochemical and fluorescence analysis of Td+ cells, with long bones being the skeletal 
elements most frequently affected by osteochondroma development in both mice and 
patients and thus most telling [46]. At day 3 from tamoxifen injection, both CreER drivers 
elicited Td+ cells in perichondrium in tibia and femur but with clear differences. The entire 
thickness of perichondrium contained Pdgfrα-CreER;Td+ cells (Fig. 1A and C) and this was 
particularly evident at higher magnification when the inner (in) layer of cuboidal cells and 
the outer (ou) layer of elongated, fibroblastic cells were evident and appreciable (Fig. 1B 
and D; Supp Fig. 1A). As expected from previous studies, Td+ cells also characterized the 
epiphyseal secondary ossification center and the metaphyseal primary spongiosa below the 
growth plate (Fig. 1A) [42]. In contrast, Fgf18-CreER;Td+ cells were largely limited to the 
outer layer (Fig. 1E and G), and this finding also was best appreciable by closer examination 
(Fig. 1F and H; Supp Fig. 1B).

3.3 Phenotypic expression in osteochondromas and perichondrium
Next, we asked whether the emergence of osteochondromas was linked to ectopic expression 
of cartilage proteins in Pdgfrα-CreER;Ext1 mutants. Serial sections stained with safranin 
O/fast green verified that osteochondroma development was undergoing within mutant 
perichondrium at 4 weeks from tamoxifen induction (Fig. 5B, D and Fig. 6B, D) whereas 
the perichondrial border was normal and smooth in control mice (Fig. 5A, C and Fig.

proteins as SOX9 and COL2A1 (Fig. 5F and H) while in control mice SOX9 and COL2A1

were absent in perichondrium (Fig. 5G). To determine whether cartilage protein expression 
in osteochondromas reflected -and was likely induced by- activation of chondrogenic 
signals, we carried out immunohistochemistry for phosphorylated SMAD1 and SMAD2 
(pSMAD1 and pSMAD2) that mediate intracellular signaling of bone morphogenic protein 
(BMP) and transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ) members [47]. A conspicuous number 
of chondrocytes within the developing osteochondromas exhibited robust pSMAD1 (Fig. 
6F and H, yellow arrows) and pSMAD2 (Fig. 6J and L, yellow arrows) immunoreactivity, 
whereas control perichondrium lacked detectable staining (Fig. 6E, G, I and K). Cells 
outside of perichondrium and within the flanking growth plate and bone were positive 
for pSMAD1 and pSMAD2 (Fig. 6G, K, white arrows) as expected [47]. Quantification 
of pSMAD1 and pSMAD2 staining cells confirmed that their number was very high

4. Discussion
The origin of cells initiating the development of osteochondromas in HMO has long been 
sought out, given its basic and translational medicine importance. Our data here reveal 
that Ext1-deficient perichondrial cells targeted by Pdgfrα-CreER and lineage-traced by Td 
expression give rise to osteochondromas whereas cells targeted/traced by Fgf18-CreER in 
the outer layer do not, at least within the two-month time frame of the study. We interpret the 
data to signify that the inner layer of perichondrium is a principal source of progenitors 
responding to both Ext1 ablation and local HS loss and progressing from cuboidal 
mesenchymal cells into chondrogenic cells. This would set in motion the first cartilage-
formation phase of osteochondroma development that as our data show, entails, and is 
likely driven by, local ectopic expression of cartilage proteins SOX9 and COL2A1 and 
an abundance of pSMAD1 and pSMAD2 mediating BMP/TFGβ pro-chondrogenic signals. 
This initial inductive phase is followed by further osteochondroma growth, elongation and 
ossification over time that likely involve the recruitment of additional cell populations, 
including growth plate chondrocytes and osteoprogenitors [17, 21] and accounting for 
the known heterogenous cell composition of mature fully-grown osteochondromas in both 
mouse models and patients [12, 15, 48]. Recruitment of additional cell populations is a 
feature often seen in other conditions of tumorigenesis [49]. A perichondrial origin of cells 
involved in osteochondroma initiation had been proposed in previous studies as pointed 
out above, based on data revealing that ectopic expression of pro-chondrogenic pathways 
in perichondrium precedes osteochondroma formation [1, 12, 15]. A similar origin was 
proposed by a subsequent study in which Ext1 was ablated using constitutive Fsp1-Cre 
(fibroblast-specific protein-1) mice that, however, express Cre in several cell populations 
from embryogenesis on [16, 50]. Having relied on conditional CreER drivers that target 
perichondrium selectively and were first induced with tamoxifen at juvenile stages, our 
study thus provides the most solid evidence to date that the initiation of osteochondromas is 
in fact attributable to perichondrial cells, and inner layer cells more specifically.
As integral part of cell surface proteoglycans including syndecans and glypicans, HS has 
vital roles in numerous processes [51]. Indeed, global ablation of Ext1 or Ext2 and the 
resulting total absence of HS are incompatible with life, stopping embryonic development 
at the blastula stage [52]. As they are synthesized in the Golgi complex, the HS chains 
undergo concurrent modifications by which their carbohydrate residues are sulfated at 
diverse N- and O-positions around the 6-carbon ring and are also epimerized, processes 
that involve more than 25 enzymes [53]. Given that each chain is made of hundreds of 
sugar residues, the sulfation and epimerization processes introduce a remarkable number 
of specific pattern combinations, resulting in the creation of 6–8 sugar-long motifs that 
have selective protein-binding capacity [54]. Signaling proteins specifically interacting 
with HS include hedgehogs, BMPs, and insulin-like growth factors [51, 54, 55]. These 
interactions limit factor availability and distribution, exerting a restricting action on -and 
supervision of- their biological activities [51, 54, 55]. Given that, it is plausible that the HS 
deficiency in Pdgfrα-CreER;Ext1 mutants may permit broader availability and distribution 
of resident pro-chondrogenic factors. Perichondrium as a whole is known to express BMP 
members such as BMP-2, BMP-4 and BMP-5 [56]. Being more available because of

local HS deficiency, these and other chondrogenic factors could potentially act to induce 
chondrogenesis in inner perichondrium, initiating osteochondroma development. It is also 
possible that once depleted of HS, inner perichondrium could become more susceptible 
and responsive to chondrogenic factors diffusing away from the adjacent growth plate, 
including BMPs, TGFβs and hedgehogs [15, 57]. This type of process could be even more 
prominent in HMO patients given their general EXT heterozygosity, resulting in a systemic 
HS deficiency of about 50% [58]. But what could account for the apparent resistance 
of outer perichondrium to participate in ectopic chondrogenesis and osteochondroma 
formation in Fgf18-CreER mutants? In line with previous studies, this could reflect the 
native mechanical-structural character of its cells [24–26], being unresponsive to growth 
factors or other cues that could alter their phenotypic and differentiation status and fate. 
Alternatively, the outer layer may possess strong anti-chondrogenic proteins that would 
protect its status and resist external influences. Perichondrium expresses several such 
anti-chondrogenic proteins including Fgfs, Wnt/β-catenin and Noggin needed to normally 
preserve its phenotype and organization [19, 59–61] but it is not clear whether there is 
preferential expression and action by these or other similarly endowed proteins in outer 
versus inner perichondrium. Previous in situ hybridization studies and recent single cell 
RNAseq and spatial transcriptomic studies have provided insights into the gene expression 
repertoire and profiles of perichondrium, but not at the level of distinguishing between inner 
and outer layer cells in a comprehensive manner [62, 63]. One last -although unlikely- 
possibility is that outer perichondrium would participate in osteochondroma formation only 
if both inner and outer layers are Ext1 mutants and HS poor, as is the case in the Pdgfrα-
CreER;Ext1 mutants. If so, this would signify that inner perichondrium cells supervise 
and dictate the behavior of outer layer cells. Whatever the explanation, these interesting, 
intriguing and broadly relevant questions will require future dedicated studies to be tackled 
and answered.
Clinically, HMO remains a significant clinical challenge. Surgery is commonly used to 
remove accessible osteochondromas, but many tumors cannot be resected safely and are 
left in place, causing life-long health problems [3, 8]. At present, there is no approved 
drug treatment to prevent or dampen tumor formation in HMO [19], but the following 
strategies can be considered. (i) Drugs antagonizing BMP signaling are effective in reducing 
osteochondroma development in HMO mouse models [21]. The BMPs and their action are 
being explored as a therapeutic target in several other conditions [64] and could potentially 
be targeted safely in HMO patients as well. (ii) Hedgehog signaling has essential roles 
in driving osteochondroma inception and outgrowth [65]. Hedgehog antagonists are used 
clinically to treat aggressive pediatric cancers [66] and could be repurposed as HMO 
treatments. These drugs have side effects including premature growth plate closure in some 
patients, raising safety concerns [67]. However, HMO involves benign tumors and may 
require lower drug doses and intermitted regimens, plausibly rendering this treatment safe 
and implementable as an HMO therapy. (iii) Studies from several groups including ours 
reported that the retinoid gamma-agonist palovarotene reduces osteochondroma growth in 
HMO mice through its anti-chondrogenic action [17, 68, 69]. This approach was based on 
our original identification of this compound as a treatment for the severe pediatric disease 
Fibrodysplasia Ossificans Progressiva [70] that has since been approved by the FDA [71].

However, recently published results of a phase II clinical trial with HMO patients suggest 
that palovarotene had negligible disease modifying effects [72]. The authors did point out 
that the trial was most likely underpowered as it included only 30 patients completing a 
one-year drug treatment cycle. Also, the one-year treatment period may have been too short 
to evaluate efficacy for a disease that develops and exacerbates over time [2, 73], and the 
only monitoring tool used was whole body MRI that may not be sensitive enough to fully 
assess changes in cartilage and bone outgrowth. These and other limitations raise issues 
about the results and conclusions of the clinical trial and do not exclude the possibility that 
a retinoid agonist-based therapy may still turn out to be a valid therapeutic option for HMO. 
In conclusion, previous studies point to several potential therapeutic strategies for HMO that 
will require dedicated studies for validation. Happily, the data reported here suggest the new 
possibility that perichondrial cells may represent an effective, selective and safe target of 
therapeutic intervention, using to-be-designed genetic or drug-based approaches. Ongoing 
studies will tell whether this is a realistic and effective strategy.
