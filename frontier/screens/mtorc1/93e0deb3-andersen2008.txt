Introduction
Pigs are often reared under very intensive condi-
tions, breeds being selected for rapid growth, high
lean meat content and other economically desirable
traits. The selective breeding for fast growth can lead
to lameness and other leg problems, with pigs being
unable to support their own rapid weight gain. Sows
have the added problem of coping with rapidly
growing suckling piglets. This can result in a loss of
body weight (b.w.) and loss of bone tissue with sub-
sequent problems being bone fractures and joint dis-
orders (Mahan and Fetter, 1982; Giesemann et al.,
1998). The maximal amount of bone acquired at
skeletal maturity, is to a great extent (70–80%)
genetically determined. The remaining 20–30% can
be ascribed to environmental factors, particularly
diet and exercise (Eastell and Lambert, 2002). Die-
tary factors, e.g. calcium and milk proteins enhance
bone formation during growth (Johnston et al.,
1992; Takada et al., 2003). The period prior to matu-
ration is important, as in humans, a higher achieved
peak bone mass lowers the risk of osteoporosis on a
long-term basis (Hernandez et al., 2003) and the
rapid growth around early puberty seems particu-
larly sensitive to dietary factors (Johnston et al.,
1992). In addition, the skeleton undergoes dramatic
changes pre- and postnatally (Lawrence, 1997) and

Summary
The long-term effect of a-ketoglutarate (AKG) given for 21–24 days
post-partum, on the skeleton of commercial pigs, was investigated. In
experiment A, 12 pigs were given AKG [0.1 g/kg of body weight (b.w.)
per day per os ], while 12 controls were administered vehicle. At day
169, the left and right femur, humerus and sixth ribs were analysed for
mechanical and geometrical properties and quantitative computed
tomography. In experiment B, 32 piglets were divided equally into an
AKG group (0.3 g/kg of b.w. per day) or a control group. Blood, taken
at days 24 and 53 was analysed for plasma 17 b-oestradiol. The main
bone effect of AKG was to increase bone length in the sixth rib (7.3%,
p < 0.01), ultimate strength (23%, p < 0.05), Young ´s modulus (52%,
p < 0.001) and maximum elastic strength (31%, p = 0.056) compared
with controls. In both experiments, AKG preferentially increased the
growth of female piglets, whilst for male piglets AKG had the opposite
effect. In addition, AKG elevated plasma 17 b-oestradiol levels compared
to those of controls at the end of the period of treatment (20%,
p = 0.002). It is concluded that AKG has long-term effects on rib proper-
ties when given early in postnatal life whilst it elevates plasma 17 b-oes-
tradiol levels only so long as it is being administered.

a-Ketoglutarate (AKG) has been given to pigs,
sheep and turkeys as well as humans with effects on
the skeletal system and protein synthesis (Hammarq-
vist et al., 1991; Tocaj et al., 2003; Harrison et al.,
2004; Kowalik et al., 2005b; Tatara et al., 2005a,
2007). Endogenous AKG is generated in the tricar-
boxylic acid (TCA)-cycle taking place in the mito-
chondrial matrix and by the deamination of both
glutamine and glutamate. a-Ketoglutarate serves
many functions in the organism. For example, it
stimulates protein synthesis and inhibits protein deg-
radation (Hammarqvist et al., 1991), functions as an
ammonium ion scavenger via transformation to glu-
tamate and increases plasma leucine and proline
concentrations (Kristensen et al., 2002; Tatara et al.,
2005a). Proline is the precursor of hydroxyproline, a
qualitatively and quantitatively important amino
acid in terms of collagen.
Only a small percentage of AKG given lumenally to
the pigs appears in the portal circulation (10%) (Kris-
tensen et al., 2002; Lambert et al., 2002). Absorption
is facilitated through secondary active transporters
coupled to the sodium gradient known as the sodium
dicarboxylate cotransporters (Pajor, 1999) with the
highest rate of absorption in the small intestine fol-
lowed by the stomach and colon (Buddington et al.,
2004). Despite the low portal uptake of AKG and its
short systemic half-life (/C245 min) (Dabek et al., 2005),
AKG increases bone mineral density and mechanical
strength, when given orally to turkeys (Tatara et al.,
2004, 2005a), pigs (Kowalik et al., 2005b) and lambs
(Harrison et al., 2004; Tatara et al., 2007). Whilst the
question of whether AKG supplements, given postna-
tally, have a permanent effect on bone mineralisation
in pigs has yet to been resolved, a recent study has
shown the potential usefulness of AKG treatment in
preserving bone mass as well as lowering bone turn-
over in post-menopausal women (Tocaj et al., 2003),
results which suggest a link between enteral AKG
and an increase in oestrogen levels.
The present study was undertaken to investigate
the long-term effects on bone of exogenously sup-
plied AKG in pigs. The main questions were: (i) will
orally administered AKG given the ﬁrst 3 weeks of
life affect bone density as well as its mechanical and
geometrical properties at the time of slaughter (day
169); (ii) to what extent, if at all, might body
weight be affected, taking into account birth differ-
ences; and (iii) does AKG act through a change in
17 b-oestradiol concentration? A preliminary report
of some of these ﬁndings has been published in
abstract form (Andersen, 2006).
Materials and methods
Experimental design
This experiment was conducted in accordance with
national guidelines for animal experimentation. Data
were collected from pigs reared at two different com-
mercial farms giving rise to experiments A & B.
Experiment A
Experiment A was conducted in an indoor swine pro-
duction facility comprising 600 breeding sows and
1500 slaughter pigs. The piglets used ( n = 24) were
crossbred [sow (Yorkshire*Danish Landrace)*boar
(Hampshire*Duroc)], produced by artiﬁcial insemina-
tion. Data include measurements from 24 piglets
assigned to one of two treatment groups and chosen
at random from six different sows. One group was
given AKG, the other distilled water (control group).
From each sow two AKG piglets and two controls
were randomly chosen from the litter. At the age of
3 days, each piglet was given iron subcutaneously
(200 mg Idofer Vet 20%; Boehringer Ingelheim Vet-
medica), the distal part of the tail was amputated and
the males were castrated according to Danish law.
Piglets were sow fed up until weaning (day 23–24)
and kept warm under an electrical heater. They
were given standard diets manufactured by Vitfoss,
Graasten, Denmark. Water was present ad libitum.
The pigs were followed from birth to slaughter and
weighed on days 1–21, 31, 68 and 169. At slaughter
(day 169) 10 AKG piglets and 10 controls were ran-
domly selected for bone analysis of the left and right
femur, humerus and sixth rib. Subcutaneous back fat
thickness was measured at three sites on the left m.
Longissimus dorsi (LD). The right LD was dissected
out and its length and weight measured.
Experiment B
Experiment B was conducted in an indoor swine
production facility comprising 280 breeding sows,
where pigs were sold at a weight of 30 kg. The pig-
lets used ( n = 32) were crossbred [sow (York-
shire*Danish Landrace)*boar (Hampshire*Duroc)],
produced by artiﬁcial insemination. Data include
measurements from 32 piglets assigned to one of
two treatment groups and chosen at random from
four different sows. From each sow, four AKG piglets
and four control piglets were randomly selected from
the piglets within each litter. At the age of 3 days,

AKG treatment
Experiment A
Powdered AKG with a purity of 99% was mixed
with distilled water to produce a 1.53 m stock solu-
tion. A ﬁnal pH of 7.3 was achieved by addition
of NaOH. The AKG group was given 0.1 g/kg b.w. of
AKG (low dose) per day orally the ﬁrst 21 days of
life as a 0.44 ml/kg b.w. dose of the 1.53 m stock
solution. The control group was given an equal dose
of distilled water per kilogram body weight, orally
once a day the ﬁrst 21 days of life.
Experiment B
Powdered AKG with a purity of 99% was mixed
with distilled water to produce a 2.3 m stock solu-
tion. A ﬁnal pH of 7.3 was achieved by addition
of NaOH. The AKG group was given 0.3 g/kg b.w. of
AKG (high dose) per day orally the ﬁrst 24 days of
life a 0.89 ml/kg b.w. dose of the 2.3 m stock solu-
tion. The control group was given an equal dose of
distilled water per kilogram body weight, orally once
a day the ﬁrst 24 days of life.
Plasma 17 b-oestradiol concentrations
Plasma 17 b-oestradiol concentrations were analysed
for 24 animals in experiment B. The samples were col-
lected from 12 pigs (six control and six AKG animals)
at day 24, before weaning and at the end of treatment,
and from another 12 pigs (six control and six AKG
animals) at day 53, 29 days after the end of treatment.
At day 24, the ﬁnal AKG dose was given 4–7 h before
blood sampling. Treatment groups comprised three
female and three male piglets. Blood samples were
collected from truncus bijugularis and stored in hepa-
rinised tubes. Blood was centrifuged (1500 g ·
10 min) within 15 min. of collection and stored at
)25 /C176 C until further analysis. The plasma samples
were analysed using ElektroChemiLumineszenz Imm-
unoAssay (MedVetLab Aps., Taastrup, Denmark).
Bone analysis
Twenty pigs from experiment A were slaughtered at
169 days for bone analysis. Twenty-four hours after
slaughter, both the right and the left femur, humerus
and sixth rib were isolated, weighed and their lengths
measured. The bone samples were stored at )25 /C176 C
until further analysis. The quantitative computed
tomography (QCT) method and SOMATOM
EMOTION apparatus (Siemens, Germany) supplied
with VB10B software were used for the volumetric
bone mineral density determination, taken separately
for trabecular and cortical bone. The volumetric bone
mineral density was measured using sequential
2 mm thick, cross-sectional metaphyseal and diaphy-
seal QCT scans. The trabecular bone density (Td)
(except sixth rib) was measured in the distal metaph-
ysis with the scan positioned just below the distal
growth plate of the investigated bone. The cortical
bone density (Cd) was assessed at 50% of the length
of the bone (Harrison et al., 2004). Using the mid-
diaphyseal cross-section of the bones at 50% of their
length and on the basis of horizontal and vertical
diameter measurements obtained by computed
tomography, the geometrical parameters such as the
cross-sectional area (A), second moment of inertia
(Ix) and mean relative wall thickness (MRWT) were
estimated. Furthermore, the cortical bone area (CA)
at this cross-section was determined. The mechanical
properties of the investigated bone were determined
by a three-point bending test using an INSTRON
4302 test machine (Instron Corp., Canton, MA,
USA). The distance between supports in this test was
set at 40% of total bone length, whereas the measur-
ing head loaded bone samples at a constant speed
(V = 10 mm/min). The results are presented graphi-
cally, and the values of the maximum elastic strength
(Wy) and ultimate strength ( Wf) of the investigated
bone were determined. Moreover, using the cortical
bone area, the Young‘s modulus was determined
(Ferretti et al., 1993a,b; Brodzki et al., 2004).

Statistical methodology
Live weights
Two trials, one involving controls and a low dose of
AKG supplement (experiment A), and the other
involving controls and a high dose of AKG supple-
ment (experiment B), were analysed separately and
the effect of dose (high and low) was analysed by
comparing the differences between the parameters of
AKG-supplement and controls for the two different
doses, this was performed to eliminate variation due
to herd, management etc. A second order regression
model including repeated measurements using
Gaussian correction was used for analysis of the
weight data over the ﬁrst 3 weeks of life with time as
covariate and slopes dependent of treatment and sex,
including ln (weight) at birth as covariate and the
effect of pig and litter as random variables. To obtain
a normal distribution, the natural logarithm of the
weight was used as a response variable. The weight
after the treatment period (day 31, 64 and 169) for
experiment A was analysed using anova including
repeated measurements with Gaussian correction.
The natural logarithm of the weight was used with
response variable, sex, treatment and time as qualita-
tive variables, ln (weight) at day 21 as covariate and
litter as a random variable.
Bone analysis and back fat measurements
Bone characteristics for the three bones (Femur,
Humerus and sixth rib) were measured on each side
of the body. The average value of the left and right
side was analysed using treatment as a qualitative
variable, weight at slaughter as a covariate and litter
and sex as random variables. From all slaughtered
pigs, the length and weight of LD and the subcuta-
neous back fat thickness at three different positions
(a, b and c) were measured. These data are analysed
using treatment as a qualitative variable and weight
at slaughter as a covariate. Litter was used as a ran-
dom variable. The sas/C210 9.1 (SAS, Cary, NC, USA)
mixed procedure including repeated measurements
when relevant was used for analysis of the data.
Data are expressed as least square mean estimates.
Differences showing a p-value > 0.05 are considered
non-signiﬁcant.

Discussion
This study shows that AKG treatment during neona-
tal development has a long-term effect on the
mechanical properties of ribs in pigs at slaughter.
These results conﬁrm the ﬁndings of a recent study of
ribs in lambs (Tatara et al., 2007). However, rib bone
density and the parameters from the weight carrying
bones, such as humerus and femur were not gener-
ally affected at the time of slaughter. Furthermore,
AKG increased the growth of female piglets compared
to that of controls, whilst for male piglets AKG had
an opposite effect resulting in lighter body weights.
Plasma 17 b-oestradiol level increased during AKG
administration, which supports previous ﬁndings
(Kowalik et al., 2005a). Moreover, the effect of AKG
treatment on body weight and plasma 17 b-oestradiol
appears transient, remaining effective so long as
treatment is continued.
a-Ketoglutarate
Both AKG and ornithine AKG counteract protein
catabolism and preserve glutamine, being used suc-
cessfully as nutritional support in trauma situations
(Wernerman et al., 1990; Hammarqvist et al., 1991;
Blomqvist et al., 1995; LeBricon et al., 1997; Tatara
et al., 2005b, 2006).
In terms of bone formation, non-oxidative metab-
olism of AKG (Matthews et al., 1993; Stoll et al.,
1999; Lambert et al., 2006) involving intestinal syn-
thesis of proline and hydroxyproline, formed from
glutamate semialdehyde, increases the conversion of
pro-collagen to collagen, which is the main organic
constituent of bone. Together with ascorbate and
Fe2+, AKG also regulates the enzyme (prolyl hydrox-
ylase) responsible for conversion of proline to
hydroxyproline. Indeed enteral supplementation of
AKG in pigs increases portal (Kristensen et al., 2002;
Lambert et al., 2006) and arterial proline concentra-
tion (Kristensen et al., 2002). The main substrate for
proline is glutamate. Glutamate receptors, transport-
ers and proteins that regulate glutamate release, are
expressed in bone cells. Moreover, glutamate, being
an excitatory neurotransmitter, is released from ner-
vous ﬁbres penetrating into bone tissue and contrib-
utes to the local regulation of bone cell functions
(Chenu, 2002; Taylor, 2002; Merle et al., 2003).
Thus, it seems likely that AKG as a glutamate pre-
cursor effects anabolic changes on bone cells via
glutamatergic innervation.
Bone geometry, morphology and mechanics
Regardless of mechanism, several studies have docu-
mented a beneﬁcial effect, of enterally administered
AKG, on bone density, mechanical and geometrical
properties (Harrison et al., 2004; Kowalik et al.,
2005b; Tatara et al., 2005a, 2007). The bones investi-
gated in pigs regarding AKG include femur (Kowalik
et al., 2005b) and ribs (Sawa-Wojtanowicz et al.,
2002). Sawa-Wojtanowicz et al. (2002) found that
ribs in pigs given 0.4 g/kg of b.w. per day AKG for
the ﬁrst 56 days of postnatal life, showed a signiﬁcant
increase in such mechanical parameters as maximum
elastic strength and ultimate strength; 90% and 47%
respectively. Perhaps of most importance, for the
current study, however is the fact that AKG treat-
ment early in life, even with a fourfold lower dose
than the study of Sawa-Wojtanowicz et al. (2002),
can have long-lasting effects on rib characteristics in
growing pigs. Furthermore, the reported signiﬁcant

treated with 0.1 g/kg of b.w. per day AKG the ﬁrst

In another investigation 0.4 g/kg of b.w. per day
AKG was given to pigs from birth to the 70th day of
postnatal life, with femur being analysed at days 3,
14, 21, 35, 56 and 70 (Kowalik et al., 2005b). Bene-
ﬁcial effects of AKG, including increased bone
length, cross-sectional area, second moment of iner-
tia and maximal elastic strength, were mainly pres-
ent at day 35 and 56 counteracting negative
inﬂuences of weaning seen in control animals. The
femur cortical density was signiﬁcantly greater in
the AKG group after 56 (approximately 2% increase)
and 70 (approximately 7% increase) days of treat-
ment. At day 70, cortical bone density and maxi-
mum elastic strength were the only parameters,
which differed between the groups. This is to some
extent similar to the present study where few long-
term effects of AKG are found in the weight bearing
bones such as femur and humerus, being only
related to cortical bone density and maximum elastic
strength. At least, the effect of AKG (ﬁrst 21 days of
life) on femur, measured at day 169 was not sub-
stantially different from that after 70 days of AKG
treatment with a fourfold higher dose. It should be
underlined that both these studies were performed
during a period of intensive development of the
skeletal system in pigs, when very rapid processes of
bone modelling and remodelling take place. As a
consequence of both these processes, bone mass
increases rapidly through signiﬁcant changes in the
outer dimensions and through intensive deposition
of bone tissue on the inner and outer bone surfaces.
Furthermore, the trabecular thickness as well as
bone mineral density increase signiﬁcantly during
development of the skeleton at early stages of neo-
natal life. All these factors are important determi-
nants of bone strength throughout life, moreover,
the modelling process is much more effective than
remodelling for increasing bone mass (Rauch, 2007).
Thus, the results of this study clearly indicate beneﬁ-
cial and long-term effects of nutritional intervention
with AKG at an early stage of neonatal life on
skeletal system development. Further investigations
are needed to elucidate whether the periods
before or after weaning are especially susceptible to
dietary AKG interventions with regard to femur
properties.
Mechanical endurance of bone tissue is deter-
mined by mineral density, geometrical properties
and by collagen quality. It seems that as well as
increased proline synthesis and collagen formation,
collagen cross-linkage (collagen maturation) may
also be improved through AKG treatment. Indeed
the data in the present study seem to support this
hypothesis, through observations of a long-term
increase in mechanical characteristics in ribs with no
concomitant increase in cortical bone density.
17 b-Oestradiol
Steroid hormones are known to inﬂuence the skele-
tal system and affect overall body mass and composi-
tion (Turner et al., 1994; Ke et al., 1997). Oestrogen
decreases the development and the activity of bone
clearing osteoclast cells and increases the bone build-
ing ability of osteoblasts (Krassas and Papadopoulou,
2001). Furthermore, women past menopause experi-
ence an accelerated net loss of bone mass due to a
decrease in ovarian produced oestrogen (Richelson
et al., 1984) and elderly men with low oestradiol lev-
els are at increased risk of hip fracture (Amin et al.,
2006). In a study performed on pigs, a signiﬁcant
increase in plasma 17 b-oestradiol concentration of
158% and 121% was observed after 35 and 56 days
of AKG treatment (0.4 g/kg of b.w. per day) respec-
tively (Kowalik et al., 2005a). Soya supplements,
containing isoﬂavones, which share structural simi-
larity with 17 b-oestradiol, reduce both serum testos-
terone concentration in men (Gardner-Thorpe et al.,
2003) and 17 b-oestradiol and progesterone in pre-
menopausal women (Lu et al., 2000). The 17 b-oes-
tradiol concentrations measured in the current study
at day 53 from pigs receiving ad libitum 20.5% crude
protein (CP) diet, containing a total amount of 7%
soyprotein are very comparable to the mean serum
concentration measured from gilts and boars at
70 days of age given ad libitum access to 17% CP
corn–soy meal diet (Clapper et al., 2000). Further-
more, the results of this investigation conﬁrm that
enteral AKG increases plasma 17 b-oestradiol con-
centration, even in young pre-pubertal animals, with
the possibility that this may affect bone.

inhibiting proteolysis and stimulating protein synthe-
sis (Lawrence, 1997). In a study of piglets adminis-
tered AKG during the last 24 days of pregnancy and
the ﬁrst 30 days of postnatal life, a counteractive
effect to a decrease in body weight and serum levels
of GH and IGF-1 at day 30 was observed when the

the male piglets, no differences in live weights
would have been noted if the comparison of treat-
ment had been made on mean values, a valid expla-
nation as to why previous AKG studies performed
on lambs, turkeys and pigs have perhaps not
reported such differences in live weight (Harrison
et al., 2004; Tatara et al., 2004; Kowalik et al.,
2005b).
In males, recent evidence from mice suggests that
oestrogens play an important role in accelerating the
rate of bone maturation and hastening epiphyseal
closure (Gunther et al., 1999), thus, excess oestrogen
may be expected to prematurely reduce the ﬁnal
growth of males as opposed to females. Alternatively,
an explanation to the current ﬁndings might be
found in the research of Prins and Birch (1995), who
showed that in male rats exposed to oestrogens dur-
ing the neonatal period, not only were permanent
reductions in the growth of the prostate gland
observed, but also that permanently decreased andro-
gen receptor levels were noted through to adulthood.
As androgens are well known to promote growth and
the enlargement of skeletal muscle cells, and as the
cross-sectional area of muscle linearly reﬂects bone
mass (Fricke and Schoenau, 2007), it would seem
logical that oestrogen exposure during early life,
reducing the sensitivity of tissues to androgens
through a decrease in androgen receptor numbers in
males, could induce sex-speciﬁc growth retardation
of muscle and bone mass. Another solution may
involve voluntary feed intake. 17 b-Oestradiol is
known to reduce feed intake in many species
(Ganesan, 1994; Lee et al., 2005). The hypophagic
effect of 17 b-oestradiol might have a greater effect on
body weight in male piglets, with a greater general
growth potential, compared to female piglets. How-
ever, these points remain to be investigated in detail.
Conclusion
The mechanism of AKG action on the skeletal sys-
tem is probably multifactorial, and may be associated
with glutamate receptor activation, bone collagen
production via proline and possible anti-catabolic
and anabolic effects of 17 b-oestradiol. This study
shows that AKG administered orally early in life has
a long-term effect on mechanical properties of ribs
in pigs slaughtered 148 days after the end of treat-
ment. Finally, during treatment AKG increased the
growth of female piglets compared to that of con-
trols, whilst for male piglets AKG had the opposite
effect resulting in lighter body weights cf. controls.
Plasma 17 b-oestradiol level increased during AKG
administration, and the effect of AKG on body
weight and plasma 17 b-oestradiol level was tran-
sient. These ﬁndings have signiﬁcance in terms of
enhancing the robustness of livestock, as well as
improving our understanding of the regulation of
bone development.
