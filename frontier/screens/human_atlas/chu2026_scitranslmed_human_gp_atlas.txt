BONE GROWTH
A transcriptional atlas of the pubertal human growth 
plate reveals two populations of stem cells and direct 
effect of growth hormone
Nelson Tsz Long Chu1†, Ostap Dregval1†, Farasat Zaman2, Lei Li1, Xin Tian1, Xin Liu1,  
Dana Trompet1, Baoyi Zhou3, Jussi O. Heinonen3, Claes Ohlsson1, Lars Sävendahl2,  
Igor Adameyko3,4, Andrei S. Chagin1,5*
The cartilaginous growth plate is a critical organ responsible for longitudinal bone growth. It closes after puberty 
in humans but remains open throughout life in mice. Although cartilage stem cells have been identified in murine 
growth plates, their existence in humans and their regulation by growth hormone (GH), the most widely used 
therapy for growth retardation, remain unknown. Here, we characterized the cellular and molecular organization 
of early pubertal human growth plates using unique surgical specimens from growth- restricting procedures and 
examined their direct responsiveness to GH. Single- cell and spatial analyses revealed two distinct stemlike popu-
lations in the resting zone, differing in proliferative activity, molecular identity, and regulatory cues. The root stem 
cells express multiple skeletal stem cell markers but not parathyroid hormone–related peptide and reside in a 
specialized microenvironment low in WNT and TGF- β growth factors. A similar population was identified in tran-
scriptionally profiled unsorted murine growth plates, and clonal lineage tracing demonstrated that these root 
cells, marked by expression of the Prrx1 gene, generate extensive chondrocyte clones and differentiate into stro-
mal and osteoblastic lineages, confirming their stem cell properties. Human growth plate explant cultures showed 
that GH directly activates JAK/STAT, TGF- β, and ERK intracellular signaling pathways, inhibits AKT signaling, and 
stimulates cartilage growth and proliferation of cartilage stem cells and chondrocytes in the proliferative zone. 
Together, these findings uncover a conserved dual stem cell organization in human and mouse growth plates and 
define direct mechanisms of GH action, providing a framework for optimizing growth- promoting therapies.
INTRODUCTION
Linear growth in children is influenced by numerous genetic, physio-
logical, and environmental factors (1). Despite this complexity, these 
factors converge on a single target, the epiphyseal growth plate, a thin 
cartilage structure located near the ends of long bones. This organ 
drives longitudinal bone elongation, and its disruption, as demon-
strated in both human and murine models, results in impaired skeletal 
growth (2, 3). Consequently, a child’s growth rate depends primarily 
on the activity and integrity of the growth plate, and most growth dis-
orders involve its dysfunction, either directly or indirectly.
Traditionally, the growth plate is described as a cartilage disk com-
posed of chondrocytes arranged along the bone’s longitudinal axis ac-
cording to their differentiation stages. Morphologically, the growth 
plate can be subdivided into four zones. The resting zone (RZ) harbors 
rounded progenitor chondrocytes that are recruited into the colum-
nar (or proliferative) zone of dividing flattened chondrocytes arranged 
in longitudinal columns. After several divisions, these cells exit the cell 
cycle; enlarge; and form, first, the postmitotic pre- hypertrophic zone 
and, with subsequent enlargement, the hypertrophic zone (HZ). The 
hypertrophic chondrocytes produce abundant extracellular matrix, 
which serves as a scaffold for bone formation after their death, a pro-
cess known as endochondral ossification (4).
Recent studies have substantially refined this classical view. First, 
many hypertrophic chondrocytes do not undergo apoptosis but instead 
transdifferentiate into osteoblasts and stromal cells, a process discussed 
extensively elsewhere (5). Second, stem cells marked by parathyroid 
hormone–related peptide (PTHrP) expression have been identified in 
the RZ of the mouse growth plate, where they give rise to all other chon-
drocyte populations (6). Activation of these stem cells prolongs bone 
growth (7). Their corresponding stem cell niche is formed postnatally 
by the secondary ossification center (8) and likely provides a low- Wnt 
microenvironment essential for stem cell quiescence (9). However, all of 
these discoveries come from murine models, and whether equivalent 
stem cells exist in the human growth plate remains unknown.
Human growth, however, differs substantially from that of mice. Hu-
mans experience a pubertal growth spurt, an acceleration of growth dur-
ing sexual maturation driven by synergistic actions of growth hormone 
(GH) and sex steroids (10). After puberty, the human growth plate un-
dergoes irreversible fusion, terminating bone elongation (3, 11). In con-
trast, mice lack a pubertal growth spurt, retain open growth plates, and 
continue growing throughout adulthood (12). These fundamental spe-
cies differences underscore the need for human- specific studies to eluci-
date mechanisms of growth regulation. For instance, it remains unclear 
why some children fail to respond to GH therapy or why responsiveness 
declines with age (13). GH influences skeletal growth both systemically, 
by stimulating insulin- like growth factor 1 (IGF1) production in the 
liver (14), and locally, as demonstrated by unilateral GH infusions into 
rodent limbs that induce asymmetric growth (15). In rodent mod-
els, GH also promotes proliferation of RZ chondrocytes (16), suggesting 
a direct action on cartilage stem cells, a concept recently reinforced by

RESUL TS
Initial characterization of the pubertal human growth plate
The pubertal human growth plate is a unique and rare tissue to ob-
tain. Accordingly, we first performed a comprehensive characterization 
of growth plates obtained from pubertal children aged 11 to 14 years 
(Tanner stages B/G2 to B/G4) during epiphysiodesis surgery for idio-
pathic tall stature (Fig. 1, A and B), a condition relatively common in 
Scandinavian countries, which have some of the tallest populations 
worldwide (19).
Cartilage was microdissected from bone marrow under a stereo-
microscope on ice, enzymatically digested for 30 min, and subjected 
to single- cell RNA sequencing (scRNA- seq) without prior sorting. 
After quality control, 23,625 cells from four donors were retained 
(Fig. 1, C and D, and fig. S1, A to D). The initial Uniform Manifold 
Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embedding identified 10 cell 
types, including chondrocytes, endothelial cells, osteoblasts, and sev-
eral hematopoietic- lineage populations (Fig. 1D and fig. S1, A and E). 
Focusing on the chondrocyte population (9507 cells), we confirmed 
the expression of chondrocyte markers SRY- box transcription factor 9 
(SOX9), aggrecan, and collagen 2A1 (COL2A1) (fig. S1, E to I). These 
cells were computationally isolated, regressed for cell cycle and stress 
signatures, and reclustered (fig. S1, I and J). After optimizing cluster 
granularity on the basis of transcriptional differences and literature- 
defined features, we identified five chondrocyte subclusters, termed 
growth plate (GP) clusters GP1 to GP5 (Fig. 1E and fig. S1, J and K). 
All four donors contributed to each cluster, albeit unevenly (fig. S1L); 
total read counts differed across clusters; and normalization to total 
read counts was performed in all subsequent analyses (fig. S1M).
Clusters GP1 and GP2 were both enriched for RZ markers secreted 
frizzled- related protein 5 (SFRP5) and apolipoprotein E (APOE) (20, 21), 
whereas PTHrP- positive cells localized primarily to GP2 (Fig. 1, F to 
H). Cycling cells and those positive for the marker of proliferation Ki- 67 
(MKI67) or cyclin D1 (CCND1) were mostly observed in GP3 and the 
upper GP4, consistent with the proliferative zone (PZ; Fig. 1, I and J, 
and fig. S2, A and B). GP5 expressed the hypertrophic marker collagen 
10 ( COL10A1), whereas pre- hypertrophic markers Indian hedgehog 
(IHH) and myocyte enhancer factor 2C (MEF2C) were enriched in GP4 
and GP5 (Fig. 1, K and L, and fig. S2C). The low representation of hy-
pertrophic chondrocytes likely reflects technical limitations in captur-
ing large cells using the 10x Genomics platform.
Growth plate biopsies were obtained under live x- ray guidance to 
prevent contamination with articular cartilage. Although surgical 
exclusion of perichondrial cells cannot be fully guaranteed, gene ex-
pression analysis for periostin (POSTN) confirmed their absence in 
our dataset (fig. S2D). The distribution of proliferation and matura-
tion markers suggests a differentiation trajectory from GP1 to GP5, 
recapitulating the classical organization of the growth plate into four 
morphological zones: (i) the RZ, which contains progenitor and stem 
cells; (ii) the PZ of flattened, dividing chondrocytes; (iii) the pre- 
hypertrophic zone of postmitotic cells, which sometimes is not dis-
tinguishable morphologically from the HZ (Fig. 1M); and (iv) the 
HZ of terminally differentiated chondrocytes (Fig. 1M and fig. S2, G 
to K). One notable deviation from this canonical structure was the 
presence of two transcriptionally distinct RZ clusters, GP1 and GP2, 
both of which expressed RZ markers. Therefore, to formally define 
the hierarchical relationships among clusters, we performed velocyto 
analysis, which infers lineage trajectories on the basis of the ratio of 
unspliced to spliced mRNA transcripts (22). This analysis revealed a 
differentiation continuum extending from GP1 to GP5, identifying 
GP1 as the root population and GP5 as the terminal end point (Fig. 1, 
N to P , and fig. S2, E and F).
To validate this inferred spatial organization, we visualized expres-
sion of SFRP5 and cyclin D1 proteins on tissue sections (Fig. 1, Q to 
T). SFRP5- positive cells were localized to the RZ, whereas cyclin 
D1–positive cells were situated immediately below, corresponding 
to the PZ and aligning with the expected histological architecture of 
the pubertal human growth plate (Fig. 1, M and Q to T). This spatial 
pattern corroborates the differentiation trajectory predicted from the 
single- cell dataset. The human growth plate displayed a large RZ 
(Fig. 1M and fig. S2G) compared with that of postnatal, sexually im-
mature rodents (23). To assess whether this is a feature of idiopathic 
tall stature, we examined archived growth plates from healthy pubertal 
children who underwent epiphysiodesis to correct for leg- length 
discrepancy (table S1). These samples also exhibited a prominently ex-
panded RZ [fig. S2L; 728 ± 72 μm for patients with leg- length differ-
ence (n = 4 patients) and 711 ± 1 μm for patients with idiopathic tall 
stature (n = 2 patients); P = 0.88 by unpaired Student’s t test], indi-
cating that this feature is a general characteristic of pubertal human 
growth plates. Together, these analyses transcriptionally and spatially 
validate the dataset and reveal two key features of the pubertal hu-
man growth plate: (i) The RZ is proportionally larger than expected 
from rodent data, and (ii) it contains two transcriptionally distinct 
progenitor populations. Building on this framework, we next char -
acterized the molecular architecture of the human growth plate.

Activation of the ATF4 regulon preceded MEF2C along the trajec -

The transition to proliferation was marked by activation of E2F 
family regulons (E2F1, E2F2, E2F3, and E2F7), consistent with cell 
cycle entry and progression (Fig. 2D). The regulon for circadian regu-
lator CLOCK was active early in this domain (Fig. 2D), aligning with 
longstanding discussions of diurnal growth in children (29, 30) and 
adding direct molecular support to the circadian modulation of lon-
gitudinal growth (31). Thus, proliferative progression appears to 
be coupled to both E2F- driven cell cycle control and early circadi-
an input.
Terminal hypertrophy featured activation of Krüppel- like factor 3 
(KLF3), SRF, CEBPD, KLF12, and BACH2 (Fig. 2D and data files S3 
and S4), with a notable enrichment of KLFs, including KLF10, KLF3, 
and KLF12 (fig.  S3J). Among these, KLF10 has been implicated in 
modulating chondrocyte hypertrophy in mice (32), whereas the roles 
of KLF3 and KLF12 in this context remain unexplored. Unexpectedly, 
the KLF family as a whole displayed a structured pattern of expression 
and transactivation along the trajectory, from early progenitors through 
terminal hypertrophy. In total, 8 of 17 known KLFs were present in our 
cross- validated set (12 when considering SCENIC- only hits), indicat-
ing enrichment across the KLF family with stage- specific regulation 
(fig. S3J). This points to a broader, previously underappreciated role for 
KLFs in human endochondral growth.
In this family, the regulon for KLF4, a Y amanaka pluripotency 
factor ( 33), was selectively active in chondroprogenitors (Fig. 2D 
and fig. S3J). Estrogen- related receptor β (ESRRB), another stemness- 
associated TF (34), was likewise active in GP1 and GP2 (Fig. 2D). 
Motivated by these observations, we asked whether additional TFs 
linked to stem- cell regulation or lineage specification were enriched 
in early clusters. The regulons for HLF and MECOM (hematopoietic 
stem cells), ETV5  (neural stem cells), POU3F1 (spermatogonial 
stem cells), and developmental patterning factors HOXA13, FOXC1, 
and TBX18 were selectively active in GP1, GP2, or both clusters (Fig. 2D 
and data files S3 and S4). Thus, early clusters harbor a TF signature 
consistent with multipotency and lineage priming. To test for global 
stemness programs, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) 
and found pluripotency gene enrichment in GP1 and GP2 but not in 
GP3 (proliferative chondrocytes) (Fig. 2E).
Last, we interrogated transcriptional kinetics using the recently 
developed biVI method (35). GP1 and GP2 were dominated by 
actively synthesized transcripts, whereas GP3 was enriched for de-
grading mRNAs (Fig.  2F). GP4 and GP5 showed more balanced 
synthesis and degradation, and genes with high mRNA turnover were 
concentrated in GP1, GP2, and GP5 (Fig. 2F). Together, these kinetic 
features indicate that GP1 and GP2 rely on regulatory strategies dis-
tinct from downstream zones, marked by active transcript production 
and selective high- turnover programs, consistent with progenitor-  
like states.
In sum, this molecular analysis provides a comprehensive char -
acterization of the pubertal human growth plate. It establishes a 
validated, stage- resolved atlas of marker genes and active TFs for 
growth- related studies. It further reveals that GP1 and GP2 (i) are 
enriched for pluripotency- associated genes, (ii) engage distinct reg-
ulatory and kinetic programs, and (iii) activate TFs linked to multi-
potency and lineage specification. Although formal stemness criteria 
(self- renewal and multipotency) cannot be tested in unmanipulated 
human tissue, these convergent findings, together with velocity anal-
ysis (Fig. 1, N to P), support the interpretation that either GP1 or 
GP2 corresponds to the human counterparts of previously identified 
mouse cartilage stem cells (6).
Spatial and transcriptional characterization of two 
chondroprogenitor clusters
The observed enrichment of pluripotency- associated TFs in clusters 
GP1 and GP2, together with their distinct gene- regulatory dynam-
ics, prompted a detailed investigation of the differences between these 
two progenitor populations. Among numerous cluster- specific mark-
ers (data file S1), we selected cytokine- like 1 ( CYTL1) and receptor 
activity–modifying protein 3 ( RAMP3) as representative genes for 
GP1 and GP2, respectively (Fig. 3, A to C), where CYTL1 is a well- 
established marker of mouse skeletal stem cells isolated from the 
growth plate region (36, 37). Among the top 25 genes most specific 
to mouse skeletal stem cells [the “root” population defined in (37)], 
13 were enriched in GP1 or in both GP1 and GP2 (Fig. 3D and fig. S3, 
K to P). This substantial overlap supports the notion of conserved mo-
lecular features between human and mouse skeletal stem cells despite 
differences in developmental timing and sequencing platforms.
Spatially, cells expressing CYTL mRNA were located in the SFRP5- 
positive population in the RZ (Fig. 3, E to I, and fig. S3Q). Dual im-
munofluorescence revealed that CYTL1+ and RAMP3+ cells form two 
partially overlapping subpopulations in the RZ (Fig. 3, J to O, and 
fig. S3R). CYTL1+ and RAMP3+ cells were frequently observed re -
siding in close proximity (Fig.  3, M and N), resembling dyads or 
chondrons, paired chondrocytes that may originate from a single 
progenitor or represent mother- daughter cell pairs and can be 
observed histologically (fig. S2, G, H, and J). Thus, GP1 and GP2 
represent two closely positioned yet transcriptionally distinct pro-
genitor populations in the human RZ.
Differential expression analysis identified 819 up- regulated and 
165 down- regulated genes distinguishing GP1 from GP2 (Fig. 3P). 
Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes 
(KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses revealed glycosaminoglycan 
biosynthesis as the only pathway enriched in GP2, whereas six path-
ways were enriched in GP1, including transforming growth factor–β 
(TGF- β), WNT, and phosphatidylinositol 3- kinase (PI3K)–AKT sig-
naling (Fig. 3Q). The WNT pathway (KEGG:04310), identified here, 
was identical to the WNT- inhibitory environment found for the quies-
cent mouse cartilage stem cells described in prior studies (9). Further-
more, mRNA for the growth arrest–specific 1 (GAS1) gene, a marker of 
quiescent mouse epiphyseal stem cells (9), was specifically expressed 
in GP1 (Fig. 3D). Among 174 genes of the KEGG:04310 pathway, GP1 
cells expressed four ligands and nine soluble inhibitors (Fig. 3R).

Moreover, the absence of β- catenin–related TF activity in GP1 (data 
files S4 and S5) further suggests a WNT- low state similar to that ob-
served in quiescent cartilage stem cells in mice (9). This prompted us 
to compare proliferative activity between the clusters. It revealed that 
GP1 cells exhibited the lowest proliferative index among GP1, GP2, 
and GP3 (Fig. 3S), further reinforcing parallels between the GP1 clus-
ter and the reported quiescent mouse stem cells (9).
The TGF- β signaling pathway displayed an even greater differen-
tial between GP1 and GP2 clusters than the WNT pathway (Fig. 3Q). 
Given the well- established role of this pathway in skeletal and stem 
cell biology (38), we examined it in detail. Manual annotation sepa-
rated KEGG TGF- β–pathway genes into bone morphogenetic protein 
(BMP)–related (activating Smad1/5/8 intracellular signaling cascade) 
and TGF- β–related (activating Smad2/3) modules (fig. S4, A and B). 
TGF- β–related components were visually observed to be enriched in 
GP1, whereas BMP pathway genes were more abundant in GP2 and 
GP5 (fig. S4, A and B). GP1 exhibited strong up- regulation of several 
soluble TGF- β inhibitors, including thrombospondin 1  ( THBS1), 
THBS2, THBS4, and DCN (fig. S4A), suggesting that TGF- β signaling 
may be actively suppressed in this cluster.
To validate this interpretation, we examined TF activity. Among 
SMAD family members, only SMAD2 showed detectable regulon 
activity, which was lowest in GP1 and highest in GP2 (fig. S4, C to 
E). Furthermore, the SOX4 regulon, a downstream effector of Smad2/3 
signaling, was active only in the hypertrophic cluster GP5 but ab-
sent in GP1 and GP2 (data file S3). These observations collectively 
indicate inhibition of TGF- β signaling in GP1. Supporting this, ad-
ditional expression analysis confirmed elevated expression of TGF- β 
inhibitors in GP1: THBS2 [7.5- fold; adjusted P (Padj) = 2.11 × 10–115], 
THBS4 (5.7- fold; Padj = 1.33 × 10–4), and THBS1 and DCN (2.6- fold 
each; Padj = 9.57 × 10–50 and Padj = 1.03 × 10–28, respectively) (fig. S4F). 
Analysis of actual gene expression [normalized to sequencing depth 
but not scaled between genes as in fig. S4 (A and B)] confirmed that 
the inhibitors THBS1, THBS2, and DCN were the most highly expressed 
TGF- β–related genes in GP1 (fig. S4G), underscoring active repres-
sion of this pathway in the GP1 cluster.
Together, these observations indicate that both GP1 and GP2 re-
side in the RZ but differ markedly in their transcriptional profiles, 
signaling environments, and proliferative behaviors. GP1 is charac-
terized by low WNT and TGF- β signaling, high expression of soluble 
inhibitors, and minimal proliferation, suggesting a stable homeostatic 
or stemlike state. In contrast, PTHrP- positive GP2 cells showed par-
tial activation of these pathways and higher proliferative activity. Con-
sidering these observations and velocity analysis (Fig. 1, N to P) and 
the gradual activation of transcriptional machinery along the GP1- to- 
GP2 continuum (Fig. 2D and data file S3), it is plausible to anticipate 
that GP1 represents a parental, dormant stem cell population in the 
human growth plate. At the same time, it is important to emphasize 
that stemness is a functional property, and therefore, the above analy-
ses provide only indirect indications of stem cell identity.
The mouse growth plate harbors two populations of 
cartilage stem cells
These findings raised the question of whether the observed two-  
cluster organization of chondroprogenitors is a unique feature of the 
human growth plate. To address this, we applied the same unbiased 
single- cell strategy to the mouse growth plate. Growth plates were 
carefully microdissected from 1- month- old mice, washed in ice- cold 
phosphate- buffered saline to remove blood cells, and enzymatically 
digested in their entirety, and the resulting single- cell suspensions 
were subjected to scRNA- seq without any prior sorting or enrichment.
After quality control, 4534 cells from four growth plates (two mice, 
proximal tibias) were retained for analysis. Initial UMAP embedding 
identified six cell types, one corresponding to chondrocytes express-
ing aggrecan and Col2a1 (fig.  S5, A to D). The chondrocyte cluster 
(1459 cells) was computationally isolated, regressed for cell cycle ef-
fects, and showed consistent gene counts, sample representation, and 
expression of Sox9  and cartilage matrix genes Col2a1 and Col9a1 
(fig. S5, D to H). After optimizing clustering granularity, we defined 
five subclusters, designated mouse growth plate 1 (mGP1) to mGP5, 
with mGP2 expressing Pthlh (RZ and stem cell marker), mGP3 and 
mGP4 harboring numerous cycling cells (PZ), and mGP5 expressing 
the HZ marker Col10A1 (Fig. 4, A to D, and fig. S5, I and J).
Several reported RZ markers, including Apoe , Clu, Pthlh, Sfrp5, 
Gas1, and Grem1 (6, 9, 20, 21, 39, 40), were enriched in either mGP1, 
mGP2, or both (Fig. 4E). Combined with expression of Sox9, Col2a1, and 
Col9a1 in both clusters (fig. S5, D, G, and H), these observations sug-
gest that both clusters may correspond to the RZ of the growth plate. 
At the same time, adjusting the UMAP resolution did not collapse the 
distinction between mGP1 and mGP2 (fig. S5I; observed as early as 
Leiden 0.2), indicating substantial transcriptional divergence. RNA 
velocity analysis (velocyto) showed a trajectory extending from mGP1 
(root) to mGP5 (terminal hypertrophic cells) (Fig. 4, F and G, and 
fig. S5, K to N). Differential expression analysis highlighted several 
KEGG pathways distinguishing mGP1 from mGP2, including 
TGF- β, focal adhesion, and PI3K- AKT signaling (fig. S5, O and P , and 
data file S5), the same pathways enriched in the human GP1 cluster 
(Fig.  3Q). These parallels suggest a conserved transcriptional pro-
gram between mouse and human GP1- like progenitors.
To spatially validate mGP1 and mGP2, we selected paired related 
homeobox 1 (Prrx1) and Cytl1 as mGP1 markers and Pthlh (gene 
name for PTHrP) and Sfrp5 as mGP2 markers (Fig. 4E). RNAscope 
analysis confirmed that cells expressing these markers localized to 
the RZ of 1- month- old mouse growth plate (Fig. 4, H to J, and fig. S6, 
A to D). These markers rarely colocalized within single cells, indi-
cating that mGP1 and mGP2 represent two distinct, spatially adja-
cent cell populations (Fig. 4, H to J, and fig. S6, A to D). To assess the 
functional properties of mGP1, we selected Prrx1 as an mGP1 marker 
(Fig. 4E and fig. S6E) and used the Prrx1- CreERT2 mouse strain for 
clonal lineage tracing by crossing it with the R26- Confetti reporter 
line and pulsed with tamoxifen at postnatal day 30 (P30; Fig. 4K). 
Three days postinduction, rare recombined cells or small clusters were 
observed in the RZ, typically near the secondary ossification center 
(Fig. 4L and fig. S6F). Over time, labeled clones expanded through 
the growth plate (Fig. 4, M to O, and fig. S6, G and H), with some 
reaching up to 400 cells after 60 days (Fig. 4O and fig. S6H). These 
large clones were not reported for Pthrp- derived lineages (6) nor ob-
served by us previously (7), indicating the superior clonogenic po-
tential of Prrx1+ cells.
Subsequent formation of osterix- positive osteoprogenitors, COL1+ 
osteoblasts, and early B cell factor 3 (EBF3)+ stromal cells from ini-
tially labeled Prrx1+ cells (Fig. 4, P to S, and fig. S6, I to R) supports 
their multipotency, encompassing chondrogenic, stromal, and osteo-
genic fates. Together, these findings demonstrate the presence of two 
distinct populations in the mouse RZ: a Pthrp+ population, previously 
shown to represent skeletal stem cells (6), and a Prrx1+ population, 
identified here as the root cluster computationally and validated func-
tionally as highly clonogenic cells with in vivo multipotency.

Inferred paracrine regulation of the human growth plate
The stage- specific expression patterns of multiple hormone receptors 
and morphogen pathways, particularly in the GP1 and GP2 human 
clusters (Fig. 2A and data file S1), suggested that these cells may be 
subject to complex hormonal and paracrine control. We therefore ex-
amined in detail the signaling mechanisms potentially regulating pu-
bertal children’s growth.
To infer paracrine mechanisms, we examined ligand- receptor 
(LR) interactions between clusters in the human growth plate. Al-
though LR predictions can be inherently noisy and of limited predic-
tive value, we conducted this analysis given the unique nature of the 
tissue under study. Specifically, we used the Rank Aggregate method 
from the LIANA package, which integrates and cross- validates pre-
dictions from multiple tools, including CellChat, CellPhoneDB, Cell-
SignalIR, Connectome, and NATMI (41). From the human growth 
plate dataset, 1000 potential LR pairs were identified. These pairs were 
filtered to exclude collagen- dependent interactions and scored for 
specificity and magnitude. The top 60 highest- ranked LR pairs for 
each score were visualized (fig. S7, A and B), and the top 160 pairs 
for both specificity and magnitude are listed in data file S4. The classical 
PTHrP- IHH feedback loop was among those identified in specificity- 
scored but not magnitude- scored interactions (fig. S7, A and B), sup-
porting the validity of the specificity- based predictions.
Hypertrophic cells (GP5 cluster) exhibited the highest secretory 
activity, expressing a wide range of morphogens. GP1 cells showed 
the second- highest activity, whereas GP3 and GP4 were the least 
active and displayed no potential interactions when scored by speci-
ficity (fig. S7A). Among the predicted interactions, IHH from the 
HZ targeted both the GP1 and GP2 clusters (fig. S7A). In addition, 
BMP8A and BMP8B were predicted to target the GP2 cluster by 
three potential receptors and the GP1 cluster by one potential recep-
tor (fig. S7A). Expression data showed that, in addition to BMP8A 
and BMP8B, hypertrophic cells expressed BMP2, BMP4, BMP6, and 
BMP7 (fig.  S4B). BMP4 was predicted to target GP2 but appeared 
among magnitude- scored rather than specificity- scored pairs (fig. S7B). 
This aligns with the expression of BMPR1A, BMPR1B, and BMPR2 re-
ceptors in the GP2 cluster (fig. S4B). The BMP2 and BMP7 were pre-
dicted to act in an autocrine manner, expressed by hypertrophic cells 
and target these same cells (fig. S7B). Thus, the LIANA predictions align 
reasonably well with the expression patterns, as would be expected. Be-
yond BMP signaling, the algorithm identified additional potential 
interactions involving fibroblast growth factors, WNTs, Ephrins, 
platelet- derived growth factor, and Notch signaling pathways (fig. S7, 
A and B). Expression values for selected ligands and receptors of these 
pathways were also presented for direct comparison (fig. S7C).
Although these predictions remain speculative, they offer valu-
able insight into potential paracrine networks that shape growth 
plate organization and function, highlighting the GP1 and GP5 clus-
ters as principal sources of paracrine signals. The predominance of 
interactions between hypertrophic (GP5) and stem cell (GP1/GP2) 
clusters likely reflects an active and complex feedback loop between 
differentiated progeny and their parental stem cells.
Inferred endocrine regulation of the human growth plate
To explore the potential hormonal regulation of the human growth 
plate, we mapped the expression of major hormone receptors across 
human growth plate clusters. Beyond the established regulators of the 
pubertal growth spurt, such as sex steroids and GH (10), several ad-
ditional hormone receptors were detected (Fig. 5A), suggesting a 
broader endocrine influence on longitudinal bone growth. Compara-
tive analysis of their abundances (mRNA expression levels were nor-
malized to sequencing depth only) revealed the PTH receptor as the 
most abundantly expressed (Fig. 5B), consistent with the central role 
of the PTHrP- IHH feedback loop in chondrocyte regulation (4). Oth-
er receptors with substantial expression included GH receptor (GHR), 
IGF1 receptor (IGF1R), estrogen receptor α (ESR1), androgen receptor 
(AR), glucocorticoid receptor  (NR3C1), thyroid hormone receptor β 
(THRB), and adiponectin receptor 2 (ADIPOR2). Except for ADIPOR2, 
all are established regulators of skeletal growth (10), supporting the 
physiological relevance of our dataset and extending these pathways 
to the human context.
Among these, GHR and IGF1R showed gradual but opposite 
expression gradients, with GHR highest in GP1 and IGF1R highest 
in GP5 (Fig. 5A). GH is the principal endocrine driver of longitudi-
nal bone growth, acting both directly on the growth plate and indi-
rectly by stimulating hepatic IGF1 production (10). The reciprocal 
expression of GHR and IGF1R suggests that these two hormonal arms 
act on distinct cellular compartments, potentially coordinating pro-
liferation and differentiation. Whereas IGF1R signaling in chondrocyte 
hypertrophy is well established (10, 42), GH- mediated regulation of 
stem cells is only beginning to emerge (17). The regulons for key 
downstream transcriptional mediators, signal transducer and activator 
of transcription 5 ( STAT5), STAT3, and STAT1, were active in the 
GP1/2 clusters (Fig. 5, C to F), supporting the idea that GH may 
directly act on growth plate stem cells.
Together, these observations suggest that the pubertal human 
growth plate is subject to complex, integrated hormonal regulation. 
Among these regulators, the localized expression of GHR and the ac-
tivation of its downstream TFs highlight an intriguing possibility that 
GH directly modulates stem cell activity in the human growth plate. 
Given the global therapeutic use of GH in pediatric growth disorders 
and the limited understanding of its underlying mechanisms in the 
growth plate, we next focused on elucidating GH action in the human 
growth plate.
GH directly stimulates the growth of the human pubertal 
growth plate

Given that GH is a potent anabolic hormone known to promote 
mitochondrial biogenesis (45), we examined the levels of mitochon-
drial leucyl- tRNA synthetase 2 (LARS2), as a marker of mitochon-
drial activity. GH up- regulated LARS2 levels in long- term cultures 
of human pubertal growth plates in the HZ (P = 0.04) (fig. S8, R and 
S), confirming growth plate responsiveness to GH and suggesting 
that enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis may contribute to its direct 
anabolic effects on growth plate chondrocytes.
Together, these data provide proof of principle that GH directly 
targets the human pubertal growth plate. Although augmented pro-
liferation and mitochondrial biogenesis may be contributing mech-
anisms, long- term ex vivo culture could introduce nonphysiological 
effects. Therefore, to minimize confounding influences, we next used 
short- term culture models to dissect the cellular and molecular mecha-
nisms underlying GH action.
Early cellular responses to GH in the human growth plate
To characterize the early cellular responses of the human growth plate 
to GH, we used the same ex vivo growth plate slice model, applying a 
24- hour treatment period (Fig. 6A). A 24- hour culture period was 
selected to capture initial cellular and transcriptional responses while 
minimizing the artifacts associated with prolonged ex vivo culture.
To validate GH responsiveness in this setting, we first examined 
two well- characterized GH target genes, insulin- like growth factor 
binding protein 3 (IGFBP3; generally up- regulated by GH) and B cell 
lymphoma 2 (BCL2) interacting protein 3 (BNIP3; down- regulated by 
GH to promote mitochondriogenesis) (46, 47). Consistently, IGFBP3 
immunostaining revealed a higher proportion of IGFBP3- positive 
chondrocytes in GH- treated explants compared with vehicle con-
trols (P = 0.0237) (Fig. 6, B to D), whereas BNIP3 expression was 
significantly (P = 0.0021) reduced by GH (Fig. 6, E to G). Treatment 
with GH significantly (P  < 0.001) increased the overall proportion 
of cells in the S phase of the cell cycle (Fig. 6H). The phosphoryla-
tion of STAT5, a direct indicator of GH signaling, was increased pre-
dominantly in the RZ (P = 0.034) upon this short- term GH treatment 
(Fig. 6, I to K). These results confirm that the cultured human growth 
plate exhibits a canonical GH response.
The highest levels of phospho- STAT5 protein observed in the RZ 
(Fig. 6K) prompted us to assess whether GH influenced chondropro-
genitor subpopulations corresponding to the GP1 and GP2 clusters. 
CYTL1 and RAMP3, representing GP1 and GP2 markers, respectively, 
were expressed after 24 hours of culture in both control and GH- treated 
samples, and their abundances were not significantly (P > 0.05) altered 
by GH (fig. S9, A to F). However, in contrast with uncultured growth 
plates, we observed that in cultured samples, CYTL1 and RAMP3 pro-
teins were sometimes coexpressed in the same RZ cells and that these 
markers were also observed in the hypertrophic layer (fig. S9, G to I), 
probably reflecting early changes induced by ex vivo conditions. Cyclin 
D1–positive cells were significantly (P  = 0.0101) increased after 
24 hours of GH exposure (fig. S9, J to L), confirming the proliferative 
response observed using scRNA- seq (Fig. 6H). The number of MEF2C- 
positive pre- hypertrophic cells remained unchanged (fig. S9, M to O).
These analyses revealed that early and direct cellular responses to 
GH include increased STAT5 phosphorylation in the RZ and enhanced 
chondrocyte proliferation in the proliferative layer. Furthermore, the 
results demonstrate that short- term–cultured human growth plate 
slices preserve their zonal architecture, respond appropriately to GH 
stimulation, and thus constitute a robust ex vivo platform for dis-
secting the direct molecular mechanisms of GH action.
Molecular mechanisms of GH action in the human 
growth plate
To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying direct GH action, 
we performed scRNA- seq of human growth plate slices cultured for 
24 hours with either vehicle or GH (Fig. 6A). First, to validate the model 
transcriptionally, we compared scRNA- seq profiles of 24- hour–cultured 
growth plate slices with those of uncultured samples. Cultured slices 
retained most cell clusters observed in native growth plates, with the 
notable exception of cluster GP1 (fig. S10, A to H). mRNA markers 
associated with GP1, including CYTL1 and IGF1, were either mark-
edly reduced (from 175 to 4 reads per positive cell for CYTL1; compare 
fig. S10I to Fig. 3B) or absent (fig. S10J) in cultured samples. Despite 
this difference, the PTHrP- IHH feedback loop, a critical regulatory 
mechanism, remained intact (fig. S10, G and H). Rates of proliferation 
were slightly reduced in cultured samples compared with uncultured 
ones (P < 0.0001), whereas the morphology of the RZ was histologi-
cally normal (fig. S10, K and L).
To address the difficulty of detecting GP1 cells in cultured samples, 
we used a semisupervised neuronal network–based model, scANVI 
(48). This method, applied to the combined vehicle-  and GH- treated 
samples, predicted the presence of GP1 cells in cultured samples on the 
basis of their uncultured counterparts (fig. S10, M to P). Although GP1 
cells were detected, they were extremely scarce (fig. S10N). Albeit the 
underlying reasons for the scarcity of GP1 cells are unclear, the observed 
overlap between CYTL1-  and RAMP3- expressing cells in cultured but 
not uncultured samples (Fig. 3, M and N, and fig. S9, G to I) suggests an 
altered transcription profile in culture conditions, which may hinder 
transcriptional separation between cultured GP1 (c- GP1) and c- GP2 
clusters. Consequently, the c- GP1 cells were not further evaluated.
Among the cultured samples, cluster c- GP2, positive for SFRP5 
and PTHrP, was identified as the one corresponding to the uncultured 
GP2 cluster (fig. S10, Q and R); clusters c- GP4 and c- GP5, marked by 
IHH, corresponded to pre- hypertrophic and hypertrophic cells, re-
spectively; and cluster c- GP3, located between c- GP2 and c- GP4, cor-
responded to flat proliferating chondrocytes (fig.  S10S). Treatment 
with GH increased (P < 0.0001) the proportion of cells in the S phase 
of the cell cycle in the c- GP3 cluster (fig. S10T).
Transcriptional analysis revealed that GH exposure for 24 hours 
resulted in 144 significantly (P < 0.0001) up-  and 120 down- regulated 
genes (DEGs) in the c- GP2 cluster (Fig. 7A) and 1640 up-  and 818 
down- regulated DEGs in the c- GP3 cluster (fig. S10U). KEGG path-
way analysis of DEGs showed that GH treatment activated several 
pathways in the c- GP2 cluster, including the TGF- β signaling pathway 
(Fig. 7B) and mostly pathways for cell cycle and DNA replication in 
the c- GP3 cluster (fig. S10V). Together, the transcriptional and cellu-
lar analyses indicate that GH directly stimulates proliferation of flat 
chondrocytes in the human growth plate. However, the response of 
RZ cells proved more intriguing, particularly in light of the TGF- β 
signaling pathway, which was found to be actively suppressed in na-
tive GP1 cartilage stem cells (Fig. 3Q and fig. S4).

Species- specific differences in postnatal growth regulation under-
score the importance of directly studying the human growth plate. Al-
though human embryonic limb development has been thoroughly 
characterized (50), postnatal growth plate biology may differ substan-
tially (8) and remains poorly understood, providing a rationale for in- 
depth characterization. Our transcription atlas identifies known and 
previously unexplored marker genes across chondrocyte differentiation 
stages and provides a detailed view of the regulatory programs govern-
ing postnatal human chondrogenesis. By integrating two complemen-
tary methods, SCENIC (27) and decoupleR (26), our dataset provides a 
robust, cross- validated map of TF activity across the growth plate. 
Known regulators of hypertrophic differentiation, including ATF4 and 
MEF2C, were activated at the proliferative- hypertrophic transition, 
mirroring findings in mice (28, 51). In addition, this analysis revealed 
numerous additional TFs previously not linked with hypertrophy, as 
well as transcriptional machinery underlying resting- to- proliferative 
transition. Together, these findings provide a valuable resource for un-
derstanding human postnatal chondrogenesis and for future studies of 
skeletal dysplasias and tissue engineering.
A notable feature of human pubertal growth plates is the large RZ, 
which comprises nearly half of the structure. This challenges the long- 
standing hypothesis that growth ceases because of the exhaustion of 
chondroprogenitors (52). Instead, the presence of stem cells, as shown 
in mice (6, 53) and proposed here for humans, suggests that growth 
cessation might involve active remodeling of the stem cell niche, for 
example, by elevating hormones such as estrogens and GH during pu-
berty. Although technical and ethical limitations preclude formal 
functional testing of human RZ cells for stemness, numerous indirect 
lines of evidence support their stemness, including differentiation tra-
jectory analysis, distinct transcriptional dynamics, the presence of stem 
cell markers and pluripotency- associated genes, slow cycling activity, 
and transcriptional similarity to mouse skeletal stem cells. In contrast, 
our mouse data provide direct functional support: Clonal lineage 
tracing of Prrx1+ cells, corresponding to the GP1 cluster, demonstrat-
ed self- renewal and multipotency, with progeny contributing to chon-
drocytes, osteoblasts, and stromal cells.
We identified two transcriptionally distinct progenitor popula-
tions in the RZ in both species. In humans, an independent spatial 
transcriptomic study supports this dual organization (54). In mice, 
our data place GP1 cells at the apex of the lineage hierarchy, up-
stream of the PTHrP + GP2 population, consistent with a previous 
report of hierarchically distinct FoxA2+ cartilage stem cells (53), al-
though we did not detect FoxA2 expression in either GP1 or GP2 
clusters in both species. Although both populations may coexist 
long term, our analyses suggest that GP1 cells represent a quiescent, 
TGF- β– and WNT- low stem cell state, whereas GP2 cells are more 
proliferative and column generating. This dual stem cell organiza-
tion, comprising one quiescent state and one active state, is common 
in other self- renewing tissues, including intestine, skin, hematopoietic 
system, and hair follicles (55). Alternative explanations remain possible. 
For example, label- retaining cells in the mouse growth plate express 
markers of both GP1 and GP2 (9), suggesting that the dormant state 
may encompass both populations, whereas these populations perform 
complementary functions, analogous to the dual stem cell systems 
described in skull sutures (56). Nonetheless, the notable cross-  
species conservation strongly supports a fundamental role for these 
cells in longitudinal growth.
GH is widely used to treat short stature (13, 18), yet its direct ef-
fects on the growth plate have remained understudied, particularly 
in humans. Using organ cultures of human pubertal growth plates, 
we demonstrated that GH can directly stimulate growth, which can 
be seen after 2 months of treatment. Notably, in a typical clinical 
setting, children are treated with GH for several years. It has to be 
emphasized that responsiveness to GH is acquired postnatally, at 2 
to 3 years of age in children (10) and 2 to 3 weeks in rodents (57). 
Neonatal rat metatarsal bones do not respond to GH ex vivo (unpub-
lished observation), highlighting the importance of using pubertal 
growth plates for testing direct GH action. Although this organ cul-
ture model has been validated in previous studies (43,  58), it has 
limitations. Cartilage, as an avascular structure, typically performs 
well in vitro, as shown in 5- month- long cultures of rat metatarsal 
bones (44). However, the subchondral bone does not form ex vivo, 
as seen in these metatarsal cultures (44). Furthermore, serum- free 
conditions may lack the nutrients required for sustained cartilage 
growth. Perhaps the most significant limitation is the inability to 
replicate the pulsatile secretion of GH, which may lead to receptor 
down- regulation and reduced responsiveness with time. Although 
three of five patients showed a growth- promoting response, the ab-
sence of a response in two patients may reflect either intrinsic patient 
variability or the limitations of the model. Despite these challenges, 
our findings provide proof of principle for a direct GH effect inde-
pendent of systemic IGF1. Short- term GH exposure increased chon-
drocyte proliferation and activated canonical JAK/STAT signaling 
(18), predominantly in cartilage stem cells, suggesting that these 
cells are specific GH targets. This finding aligns with previous stud-
ies in rats, in which GH’s effects are localized to RZ cells (16) and 
recent mouse genetic data showing direct GH regulation of PTHrP+ 
stem cells (17). Beyond canonical signaling, we uncovered nonca-
nonical GH effects, including activation of ERK1/2 and inactivation 
of AKT signaling pathways not previously linked to GH- mediated 
longitudinal growth but regulated by GH in other systems (59). 
These findings are particularly relevant in the context of emerging 
therapies, such as c- type natriuretic peptide analogs, which modulate 
ERK1/2 (60). A particularly intriguing observation was GH- induced 
activation of the TGF- β signaling pathway in human cartilaginous 
stem cells, given that quiescent stem cells were observed to reside in 
a TGF- β–low microenvironment. Considering the established role 
of TGF- β in maintaining stem cell quiescence, GH- mediated modu-
lation of this pathway may represent a mechanism for stem cell acti-
vation or niche remodeling. This interaction has potential clinical 
relevance, given that TGF- β–targeting therapies are currently under 
investigation for skeletal disorders (61).
This study is limited by the rarity of human pubertal growth plate 
samples, restricting cohort size and precluding stratification by sex, 
pubertal stage, or GH responsiveness. The samples in this study were 
isolated from children undergoing epiphyseodesis surgery. This pro-
cedure is performed predominantly in Sweden and the Netherlands, 
nations among the 10 tallest globally (19), where surgical termination 
of normal growth is sometimes considered. Our clinical center serves 
all of Scandinavia, yet we can obtain only three to five samples annually. 
This scarcity highlights the uniqueness of the tissue studied but 
imposes certain constraints. For instance, we combined data from

tecture of the human pubertal growth plate, identifies two conserved 
populations of cartilage stem cells, and uncovers direct, noncanoni-

stem cells and uncovering TGF- β pathway involvement, our find-

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study design
The primary objective of this study was to investigate whether puber-
tal human growth plate chondrocytes can respond directly to GH 
treatment. In addition, the study aimed to identify the cellular and 
molecular targets of GH. This objective further motivated an extensive 
characterization of the native pubertal human growth plate because it 
had not been comprehensively characterized in previous studies. Single- 
cell sequencing, bioinformatic analysis, and molecular biology tech-
niques were used to analyze the human growth plate. The surgical 
removal of the growth plates, known as epiphysiodesis, was performed 
on healthy adolescent children with predicted tall stature in accordance 
with local pediatric guidelines. Because of the limited availability of 
fresh adolescent growth plate samples, 4 of 10 patients were analyzed 
using the 10x Chromium platform, whereas the remaining samples 
were used for explant experiments and validation analyses. Quantified 
histological and validation experiments were performed using samples 
from at least three patients (table S1). No data were excluded from sta-
tistical analyses, and no outliers were removed. The study cohort 
included 10 participants aged 11 to 14 years, encompassing both sexes. 
Sex was determined on the basis of self- reporting. Consent was obtained 
for sharing individual- level data. Detailed patient information is pro-
vided in table S1. All patients were monitored, and all surgeries were 
conducted at Karolinska University Hospital, Sweden. The collection of 
human growth plate biopsies postsurgery was approved by the local hu-
man ethics committee (permission number is 97- 214, Karolinska 
Institutet Research Ethics Committee North, Karolinska University 
Hospital, Stockholm, Sweden). Written informed consent was obtained 
from all patients and their parents, and the process was documented 
in hospital records. The adolescent patient samples, due to difficulty in 
their availability, were limited to patients with tall stature and a need 
for epiphysiodesis surgery. Patient samples were given random num-
bers during the experiment. Race and ethnicity were not considered 
in this study. All the patient material was collected in Karolinska 
University Hospital, Stockholm, Sweden. All animal experiments 
were approved in advance by the Stockholm North Ethical Committee 
or the Gothenburg Animal Ethics Board (Göteborgs djurförsöksetiska 
nämnd, 4030/21) and conducted in accordance with the regulations 
of the Swedish Board of Agriculture. In mouse experiments, both 
sexes were considered.
Statistical analysis
All values are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise stated. Com-
parisons between two groups (such as vehicle- treated and GH- treated 
slices) were performed using either a paired or unpaired Student’s t 
test, depending on whether the samples were paired or independent. 
Whereas experiments were performed in a way that growth plate slic-
es from each patient were cultured with either vehicle or GH (paired 
samples), during the analysis pipeline and blinding of the samples, the 
analyzed histological sections were not always from the same patients 
and were regarded as independent samples in this case. For grouped 
data, one- way analysis of variance (ANOV A) with Tukey’s correction 
for multiple comparisons was applied.
All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (ver-
sion 8.0) unless otherwise specified. The normality was tested in Graph-
Pad Prism before applying the t test or ANOV A. A minimum of three 
independent biological replicates was included in each statistical analy-
sis. Details regarding the specific statistical tests used for individual 
experiments are provided in the corresponding figure legends. The 
following P value thresholds were used to denote statistical significance: 
*P < 0.05, **P < 0.005, ***P < 0.0005, and ****P < 0.00005.
To statistically estimate the proliferation score in scRNA- seq data, 
a chi- square contingency test was used. Each cell was assigned a phase 
score based on the expression of known cell cycle genes. Cells identi-
fied as being in the S or G2- M stages were classified as cycling, render-
ing the parameter binary. For each cluster, the absolute number of 
cycling and noncycling cells was calculated. A pairwise chi- square 
contingency test was conducted to compare gp1 against all other clus-
ters. The null hypothesis (H0) posited that the groups compared had 
the same effect on proliferation, and this hypothesis was tested. All 
data are provided in data files S1 to S7.

A transcriptional atlas of the pubertal human growth plate reveals two populations
of stem cells and direct effect of growth hormone
Nelson Tsz Long Chu, Ostap Dregval, Farasat Zaman, Lei Li, Xin Tian, Xin Liu, Dana Trompet, Baoyi Zhou, Jussi O.
Heinonen, Claes Ohlsson, Lars Sävendahl, Igor Adameyko, and Andrei S. Chagin
Sci. Transl. Med. 18 (845), eadw3590.  DOI: 10.1126/scitranslmed.adw3590
Editor’s summary
Human longitudinal bone growth is organized from the cartilaginous growth plate. The cellular mechanisms underlying
growth disorders and the targets of growth hormone therapy have remained poorly defined for human tissues. Here,
Chu et al. conducted cellular and molecular analyses of the early pubertal human growth plate to identify two stemlike
populations in the growth plate resting zone, similar to populations identified in the murine growth plate. Explant
cultures of the pubertal human growth plate further elucidated growth hormone–related signaling providing insight for
therapeutic intervention in growth disorders. —Molly Ogle
