ABSTRACT. Oherger, E., Engstrom, I. and Karlberg, J. (Department of Paediatrics, 
Karolinska Institute, Stockholm and Departments of Pediatrics I and Anatomy, Gothenburg 
University, Gotehorg, Sweden). Longterm treatment with glucocorticoids/ACTH in asthmatic 
children. 111. Effects on growth and adult height. Acta Paediatr Scand 79: 77, 1990. 
The effect on growth of long-term treatment with prednisolone and/or ACTHI-24 (tetracosac- 
trin) depot preparation was studied in 40 children with severe bronchial asthma. Height 
velocity was subnormal before treatment. During treatment the group of 17 children prima- 
rily treated with ACTH showed a moderate increase in mean velocity. Their height was not 
significantly altered, and neither was the age at peak height velocity nor adult height. In the 
group of 23 children treated with prednisolone the mean velocity decreased, resulting in a 
relative decrease in height. Peak height velocity was delayed by about 2 years in the boys hut 
occurred at the expected time in the girls, as did menarche. Mean adult height was lower than 
expected after adjustment for mid-parenteral height. In 10 children ACTH was substituted 
for prednisolone, and their height velocity increased but not enough to affect adult height, 
which was just as low as in the patients treated with prednisolone only. Key words: bronchial 
asthma, children, adolescents, growth, peak height velocity, adult height, glucocorticoids, 
corticotrophin. 
Inhibition of growth is a well-known side effect of long term systemic glucocorticoid 
treatment and occurs in children with bronchial asthma whether receiving alternate day (I, 
2) or daily administration (3-7). After withdrawal of such treatment, growth accelerates (3, 
6,7), in particular during therapy with ACTH (8,9). There are no reports on effects of long 
term treatment with glucocorticoids and/or ACTH on adult height. The aim of the present 
study was to investigate the immediate and the long term effect of glucocorticoids and/or 
ACTH on growth and timing of pubertal maturation. Longitudinal growth data were 
collected in children with severe bronchial asthma treated with prednisolone and/or 
ACTH1-24 (tetracosactrin) depot preparation and followed through the treatment period 
until the end of growth. 
MATERIAL AND METHODS 
Our series consist of 40 children, born in 1947-1974, with bronchial asthma severe enough to require 
long term treatment with glucocorticoids or ACTH (Table 1). Seventeen children were given ACTH 
without prior treatment with prednisolone (group A). Twenty-three children were given prednisolone 
(groups P and P+A). Ten of these were transferred to ACTH after a period of combined treatment 
(group P+A). 
During the period of study ACTH was withdrawn in all children except one (in group P+A) and 
prednisolone was withdrawn in 8 of 13 children (in group P). All children in whom the treatment was 
withdrawn were followed further for 31 year, 32 until final height.

RESULTS 
Growth. In the whole series (n=40) the mean height SDS was below the reference mean 
(1 1) (p<O.OOl) at start of treatment, and the mean height velocity SDS (n=30) was below 
the reference mean (p<O.OOI) during the year immediately preceding the treatment. Height 
velocity increased in all children but three during the first year of treatment with ACTH 
and decreased in all children during the first year with prednisolone (Fig. I). Height 
velocity SDS remained at the same level during treatment with ACTH and decreased after 
its withdrawal (p<O.OI) but the mean posttreatment value did not differ significantly from 
the reference mean (Table 2). No significant bivariate correlation was found for height 
velocity during the first year of treatment with the dose of ACTH, age, duration of the 
disease or height or height velocity before treatment. 
During treatment with prednisolone, height velocity remained low, resulting in a pro- 
gressive decrease in height SDS (Table 2). In the ten patients transferred from predniso- 
lone to ACTH (group P+A) (Table I) growth accelerated, but 1 year after withdrawal of 
prednisolone the mean height SDS still remained below the reference mean. Growth was 
retarded on doses of prednisolone as small as around 0.1 mglkglday (Fig. 2). No significant 
dose-response relationship was present. Furthermore, height velocity did not show any 
significant bivariate correlation with age, duration of the disease, or height or height 
velocity before treatment.

DISCUSSION 
Severe asthma as such may have a negative influence on growth (7, 16, 17). This effect of 
the disease as such was evident in the present group of children. The acceleration of 
growth during the first year of ACTH treatment did not reflect pubertal growth spurt, as no 
child showed any sign of pubertal maturation. The dose did not correlate with height 
velocity. A few of the children requiring very high doses had lower growth rates, which 
might reflect the severity of asthma rather than the dose of ACTH. Thus the acceleration 
might involve catch-up growth (18) due to better control of the disease. Alternatively, it 
could result from growth-promoting effect of the ACTH treatment, presumably mediated 
by an increased secretion of adrenal androgens (19). The fact that adrenal androgens may 
accelerate growth is evidenced by the association of the “mid-growth spurt” (20) at about 
6-8 years of age with normal adrenarche (21). 
During the first year of prednisolone treatment all children improved clinically, but their 
height velocity was even lower than before. Despite the fairly low dosage the growth- 
suppressive effect was strong during the whole period of treatment, leading to a progres- 
sive decrease in height SDS. Growth was already retarded at a dose of 0.1 mg predniso- 
lone/kg/day, which is well below previously reported limits in asthmatic children (2, 3. 4). 
As a consequence no dose effective in the treatment of asthma can be considered safe with 
regard to growth. 
Long-term glucocorticoid treatment is reported to reduce the levels of testosterone in 
adult men (22). This might be relevant to the mechanisms behind the delayed pubertal 
growth spurt in the prednisolone-treated boys as the pubertal growth spurt depends on an 
increase in testicular androgens. However, Hauspie et al. (16) found a delay in puberty of

Martin et al. (17) reported normal adult height in patients with severe asthma, despite a 
delay in growth at around the age of 14. In the present group the pronounced growth 
retardation before puberty was not compensated for by the pubertal spurt. A period of 
increased height velocity before puberty is apparently necessary in order to reach the 
expected adult height. According to Prader (18) the possibility of complete or near 
complete catch-up growth diminishes as puberty approaches. Consequently, the age at 
withdrawal of glucocorticoids appears important to the final outcome of growth. In the 
present group no such relationship was found. However, in the four children who reached 
adult heights above - 1 SDS, prednisolone had been withdrawn before PHV. No child on 
prednisolone at the time of PHV reached that level of final height. Thus, we conclude that 
there is probably less risk of growth stunting if glucocorticoids can be avoided during the 
period immediately preceding the pubertal growth spurt. 
The acceleration of growth after ACTH is substituted for prednisolone is in agreement 
with previous findings (8,9, 23) but was not sufficiently pronounced to compensate for the 
lost height potential. 
It is apparent that both systemic prednisolone and ACTH interfere with the hormonal 
regulation of growth. ACTH might induce an enhancement of growth rate, but this seems 
to be transient, and adult height is not perceptibly affected. In contrast, treatment with 
glucocorticoids, if not terminated well in advance of puberty, entails a considerable risk of 
stunting.
