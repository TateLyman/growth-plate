Submicroscopic 9q22.3 Deletion

TO THE EDITOR:
Haploinsufﬁciency of the PTCH1 gene at 9q22.3 leads to Gorlin
syndrome. The characteristic features of Gorlin syndrome include
relative macrocephaly, multiple basal cell carcinoma, keratocysts
of the jaw, palmar/plantar pits, spine and rib anomalies, and
calciﬁcation of the falx cerebri [Kimonis et al., 1997]. Recently,
Redon et al. [2006] reported two patients with comparable 6.5-Mb
long submicroscopic deletions spanning the PTCH1 gene who
exhibited overgrowth; they suggested the existence of a gene whose
haploinsufﬁciency contributes to prenatal overgrowth. Further-
more, Shimojima et al. [2009] reported a patient with a 2.3-Mb
deletion spanning PTCH1 who exhibited overgrowth. Since the
2.3-Mb deleted interval was completely included within the 6.5-Mb
deleted interval deﬁned by Rendon et al., the putative gene whose
haploinsufﬁciency leads to overgrowth should reside within the
2.3-Mb interval. Here, we report on a patient with overgrowth
and a 2.4-Mb deletion, of which 1.4 Mb (94,882,075 –97,322,234)
overlapped the deleted region reported by Shimojima et al. Our
observation further narrows the critical interval where the putative
growth suppressor gene is likely to reside.
The propositus was a Japanese-Chinese boy who was referred to
our clinic at the age of 2 months for the evaluation of multiple
congenital anomalies. The parents were nonconsanguineous and
phenotypically normal and had a normal stature (paternal height of
175 cm and maternal height of 166 cm). The patient’s prenatal
history was remarkable for hydronephrosis of the right kidney
(grades 3–4) and multiple dysplastic cystic kidney of the left kidney,
which had been detected at 35 weeks. Oligohydramnios was not
observed.
The propositus was delivered vaginally at 37 weeks of gestation.
His Apgar score was 6 at 1 min and 7 at 5 min. His birth weight was
3,833 g ( þ2.0 SD), and his crown-to-heel length was 53.5 cm
(þ2.1 SD). At birth, he was noted to have bilateral auricular pits
and preaxial polydactyly of the left hand. The patient was not
edematous.
He exhibited a global developmental delay and hypotonia: he
gained head control at around 12 months of age and started rolling
over at the age of 17 months. At 25 months of age, he was able to sit
without support but was unable to stand even with support and had
never uttered any meaningful words.
His growth was characterized by sustained overgrowth since
birth (Fig. 1). At an age of 23 months, he weighed 15.28 kg
(þ3.5 SD) and was 95.5 cm tall ( þ3.8 SD); his OFC 56.6 cm
(>90th centile). He exhibited dysmorphic features including
apparent hypertelorism, frontal bossing with a low nasal bridge,
epicanthal folds, low-set ears, bilateral auricular pits, a long phil-
trum, micrognathia, a high palate, a short and webbed neck, a sacral
dimple, and preaxial polydactyly of the left hand. These physical
features were not suggestive of any speciﬁc syndromic diagnosis.
The bone age as determined according to the Greulich and Pyle
method was age appropriate, and there were no signs of rib
anomalies or calciﬁcation of the falx cerebri.
His G-banded karyotype was normal. Array comparative
genomic hybridization using the Agilent 180 K format CGH array
designed by the International Standards for Cytogenomic Arrays
Consortium (backbone resolution of 25 kb and 500 targeted

We have reported here on a patient with overgrowth of prenatal
onset who had a submicroscopic 2.4-Mb deletion at chromosome
9q22.3. Even in retrospect, once we had obtained the array CGH
results indicating the PTCH1 haploinsufﬁciency of the propositus,
the patient did not fulﬁll the diagnostic criteria for Gorlin syndrome
proposed by Kimonis et al. [1997], presumably because of the
patient’s young age. The appearance of many of the critical features
of Gorlin syndrome, such as multiple basal cell carcinoma,
keratocysts of the jaw, and palmar/plantar pits, is age-dependent
and occurs rather late in life. We suggest that the possible presence
of a submicroscopic 9q22.3 deletion should be considered when
evaluating patients with multiple malformations including over-
growth with a prenatal onset.
Together with the ﬁndings of three previously reported cases of
submicroscopic 9q22.3 deletion involving PTCH1 [Redon et al.,
2006; Shimojima et al., 2009], the present ﬁndings suggest that a
growth suppressor gene(s) may reside at this location. The obser-
vation that two cases with a relatively small but microscopically
visible deletion have been reported to exhibit prenatal overgrowth
supports this notion [Farrell et al., 1991; Chen et al., 2006; Yama-
moto et al., 2009]. The lack of overgrowth in several patients with a
large deletion that was detected by a G-band analysis might indicate
that the larger deletions tend to suppress growth in a nonspeciﬁc
manner [Shimojima et al., 2009]. The observation that relative
macrocephaly is a frequent feature of Gorlin syndrome and that the
adult height of patients with Gorlin syndrome is statistically higher
than that of the general population [Kimonis et al., 1997] suggests
that the haploinsufﬁciency of PTCH1 may be responsible for
accelerated growth. Nevertheless, the fact that overt overgrowth
with a prenatal onset is not considered a typical feature of patients
with Gorlin syndrome arising from the intragenic mutation of
PTCH1 suggests the possibility that a growth suppressor gene(s)
other than PTCH1 may exist in the proximity of PTCH1. The most
direct proof of the existence of a putative growth suppressor gene
apart from the PTCH1 gene would be the documentation of
patients with prenatal overgrowth who have a deletion of 9q22.3
that does not include PTCH1. We wait further studies.
A comparison of previously reported patients with prenatal
overgrowth and the 9q22.3 submicroscopic deletion [Redon
et al., 2006; Shimojima et al., 2009] narrowed the critical interval
for the putative growth suppressor gene to between 95.9 and
97.3 Mb in the hg18 coordinate (Fig. 3), based on the concept of
inclusion mapping. This region extends 1.4 Mb from the PTCH1
gene towards the centromere.

Included within the minimal overlap are 11 genes: PTPDC1,
ZNF169, FAM22F, AK095188, HIATL1, CR593779, FBP2, FBP1,
C9orf3, FANCC, and PTCH1. FBP1 and FBP2 (fructose 1,6-
bisphosphatase) are associated with glucose metabolism. FBP2,
which encodes a gluconeogenesis regulatory enzyme that cata-
lyzes the hydrolysis of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate to fructose
6-phosphate and inorganic phosphate, is one of the eight genetic
loci associated with human height [Cho et al., 2009] and thus is a
fair candidate gene that may be responsible for overgrowth. As
its name indicates, PTPDC1 (protein tyrosine phosphatase
domain containing 1) is hypothesized to act in the MAP kinase
pathway and thus may play a regulatory role in growth. APO
encodes a digestive enzyme aminopeptidase O and is an unlikely
candidate. FANCC (Fanconi anemia, complementation group C) is
the causative gene responsible for Fanconi anemia, and hetero-
zygotes for FANCC mutations are asymptomatic; thus, this gene is
unlikely to be associated with overgrowth. The function of
FAM22F, ZNF169, AK095188, CR593779, and C9orf3 are unknown
at present, and thus their potential role in overgrowth is yet to be
evaluated.
In summary, we suggest that the submicroscopic 9q22.3 deletion
should be included in the differential diagnosis of overgrowth of
prenatal onset and multiple malformations. The identiﬁcation of
9q22.3 and an accompanying PTCH1 haploinsufﬁciency may
enable appropriate anticipatory management, including tumor
surveillance and the avoidance of irradiation, which could predis-
pose the individual to tumor formation.
