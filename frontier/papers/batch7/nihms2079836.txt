Abstract
Gorlin-Goltz Syndrome (GGS) is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder encompassing a 
diverse range of clinical manifestations, including congenital anomalies and predisposition to 
cancer. Pathogenic variants in PTCH1 and SUFU account for up to 79% and 6% of cases, 
respectively. Currently, an estimated 15%−27% of individuals with a clinical diagnosis of GGS 
do not have a pathogenic variant identified in either gene. We report on a 17-year-old female 
referred to the Undiagnosed Disease Network with a clinical diagnosis of Gorlin-Goltz Syndrome 
that manifested as both classic and unusual findings, including isolated hypogonadotropic

Introduction
Gorlin-Goltz syndrome (GGS), also known as Basal Cell Carcinoma Nevus Syndrome 
(BCCNS), is an autosomal dominant condition with a variable phenotype of major 
and minor anomalies as well as tumor predisposition. Penetrance is high, but clinical 
expressivity is variable. First delineated by Gorlin and Goltz in 1960, GGS was established 
clinically as a classical triad of multiple basocellular epitheliomas, odontogenic keratocysts 
(OKCs), and bifid ribs.(Gorlin & Goltz, 1960) Over time, the diagnostic criteria were refined and 
formalized with major and minor criteria, (Evans et al., 1993; Kimonis et al., 1997) last revised in 
2005.(Bree, Shah, & Group, 2011) According to this framework, a diagnosis can be established in 
the presence of 1) one major criterion and molecular confirmation; 2) two major criteria; or 
3) one major and two minor clinical criteria. Major criteria include a basal cell carcinoma 
(BCC) with onset in individuals ≤20 years old or multiple BCCs; odontogenic keratocyst 
of the jaw with onset prior to 20 years; palmar/plantar pitting; lamellar calcification of the 
cerebral falx; medulloblastoma; and a first-degree relative with GGS. Minor criteria include 
rib anomalies; other specific skeletal malformations and radiologic changes; macrocephaly; 
cleft/lip palate; ovarian/cardiac fibroma; lymphomesenteric cysts; and ocular abnormalities.
(Bree, Shah, & Group, 2011)
Clinical criteria are particularly relevant for the estimated 15–27% of individuals who are 
clinically diagnosed but lack detected pathogenic variants in either PTCH1 or SUFU, the 
two main genes associated with GGS .(Evans et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2014) In the absence of 
these variants, making a definitive diagnosis of GGS becomes more challenging, particularly 
when an individual presents with unusual or novel manifestations. Nonetheless, establishing 
a diagnosis is crucial for its significant implications for management, especially tumor 
surveillance and prevention.(Bree, Shah, & Group, 2011)
To illustrate these points, we present a challenging case of a 17-year-old woman referred to 
the Undiagnosed Disease Network (UDN) with a clinical diagnosis of GGS but with unusual 
findings and non-diagnostic genetic testing that included targeted gene panel, genomic

Case Report
A 17-year-old female with clinically suspected GGS was referred to the University of Utah 
Intermountain West clinical site of the Undiagnosed Disease Network (UDN) for diagnostic 
evaluation because of her unusual findings and non-diagnostic extensive genetic testing.
Following an uncomplicated term pregnancy and delivery, she was diagnosed at birth with 
cleft palate and bilateral cleft lip. The staged cleft repair was completed at age 4 years. The 
associated velopharyngeal dysfunction, hypernasality, and speech articulation issues were 
treated with speech therapy and, eventually, pharyngoplasty. At age 2 years, her mother 
noticed a mass just left of her nose, firm and bone-like to the touch, which on computed 
tomography (CT) was diagnosed as a large expansile cyst extending to the left maxillary 
sinus. The cyst, excised during the hard palate repair surgery, had histological features 
consistent with an odontogenic keratocyst (OKC). Because of the well-known association 
of OKC with GGS, she was referred to dermatology. The cutaneous examination at that 
time did not identify any manifestations of GGS. A further CT failed to identify a calcified 
cerebral falx but showed non-union of the posterior elements of the third thoracic vertebra, 
which has been described in GGS. (Kimonis et al., 1997)
Over the years, the patient continued to be followed in the cleft palate/craniofacial clinic 
for the development of multiple OKCs in her mandible and maxilla, associated with 
pain, swelling, and tooth bud displacement. She underwent several surgical interventions, 
including rotary curettage for OKCs, application of Carnoy solution, cyst marsupialization, 
and a bone graft to treat a left maxillary cleft. A CT of the brain at the age of 11 years 
identified calcification of the cerebral falx, and at this time she was referred to genetics for 
diagnostic evaluation.
At 7 years of age, her height was tracking at the 3rd percentile. Additionally, she had 
relative macrocephaly, with an occipital frontal circumference of 55 cm (90th percentile), 
non-familial facial traits, including orbital hypoplasia with mild exophthalmos, unfurled 
superior helix in her right ear (figure 1), underdeveloped bilateral malar regions, high 
nasal bridge, shortened philtrum with cleft lip repair scars, and compensated class III 
malocclusion with a left posterior open bite. Additional eye findings included intermittent 
exotropia treated subsequently with bilateral lateral rectus recession surgery. She had palmar 
pits, but no signs of basal cell carcinomas.
Based on these physical and imaging findings, which included three major diagnostic 
criteria, a clinical diagnosis of GGS was established. Recommendations at the time included 
the diligent use of sun protection for the risk for basal cell carcinoma, annual dermatological 
examinations, and radiation avoidance (e.g., radiographs only when strictly necessary).

Genetic testing was initiated to confirm and characterize the clinical diagnosis. However, 
targeted sequencing and deletion/duplication analysis of the PTCH1 and SUFU genes were 
negative, as was a chromosomal microarray analysis.
The patient was also found to have mixed hearing loss on the right side on a school 
screening, which was confirmed on audiological evaluation. Subsequent imaging identified 
bilateral hypoplasia of the posterior semicircular canals.
On follow-up, her height had fallen off the growth curve. At 14 years and 5 months, 
her height was 141.9 cm (z-score: −3 SD). An endocrine evaluation for short stature 
identified early signs of adrenarche (minimal axillary hair growth and Tanner stage 2 for 
pubic hair), but no signs of central puberty (no breast development). Hormonal testing 
showed normal thyroid axis (TSH: 1.82 µIU/mL, free T4: 0.95 ng/dL), growth hormone axis 
(IGF-1: 166 ng/mL, IGF-BP3: 2830 ng/mL), and prolactin (6.4 ng/mL), with no signs of 
adrenal insufficiency. She had prepubertal levels of LH (0.1 mU/mL), FSH (0.5 mIU/mL) 
and estradiol (3.0 pg/mL). Her bone age consistent with 13 years and 6 months was 
approximately 2 years behind her chronological age. Finally, she reported that she could not 
smell. Given her report of anosmia along with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, she was 
diagnosed with Kallmann syndrome and estradiol therapy was initiated. Additional findings 
at that time included mild obstructive sleep apnea that did not require continuous positive 
airway pressure (CPAP), simple cysts in the left kidney, and a Chiari type I malformation. 
Because of these unusual clinical manifestations (including Kallmann syndrome and 
abnormal SCC) and the negative testing for GGS-related genes despite a clinically consistent 
presentation, she was referred to the UDN for further diagnostic evaluation.
Material and Methods
Editorial policies and ethical considerations.
Written informed consent was obtained from the patient to publish clinical information.
Genetic analyses.
A targeted gene panel for Gorlin-Goltz Syndrome was negative for sequencing and 
copy number variants in PTCH1 and SUFU. Genomic microarray was also negative. 
Clinical exome sequencing reported a maternally-inherited missense variant of uncertain 
significance (VUS) in CHD7 (c.8077G>C; p.Gly2693Arg, NM_017780.3), but no variants 
of interest in PTCH1 or SUFU. Pathogenic variants in CHD7 cause autosomal dominant 
CHARGE syndrome, known to include abnormalities in the SCC and, rarely, Kallmann 
syndrome. However, the evidence for pathogenicity for the CHD7 variant (p.Gly2693Arg) 
was unconvincing. The patient’s mother reported having a normal sense of smell and no 
external manifestations of CHARGE syndrome (though she declined imaging to assess the 
semicircular canals) or puberty issues. Methylation analysis for the epigenetic signature 
associated with CHARGE syndrome was performed on the proband at a commercial 
laboratory and was not consistent with CHARGE syndrome. (Aref-Eshghi et al., 2018; Sadikovic, 
Levy, & Aref-Eshghi, 2020; Sadikovic et al., 2021) . No additional epigenetic signatures were 
analyzed.

Chromosome analysis and karyotype generation were performed on metaphase preparations 
from the patient’s peripheral blood using standard procedures.(Marilyn S. Arsham, March 2017) 
Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) studies were performed on peripheral blood 
samples from the patient and an unrelated healthy control using fluorescently labeled 
bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clones (Empire Genomics, Buffalo, NY). A single 
red fluorescent BAC probe (RP11–404F11 at 9q22.33) was designed to hybridize within 
the inverted segment, and a green fluorescent BAC probe (RP11–974F22 at 9q34.3) was 
designed to hybridize outside the inversion. Metaphase cells were scored for the number and 
location of red and green signals.
DNA was extracted from the patient’s peripheral blood sample. The genomic regions 
flanking the proximal and distal breakpoints of inv(9)(q22.32q34.2) were amplified by PCR 
and Sanger sequenced. PCR was performed using M13-tailed primers (Table S1), Premix 
D (Lucigen), and AccuStart II Taq DNA Polymerase (Quantabio). Bidirectional Sanger 
DNA sequencing was performed using BigDye Terminator Cycle Sequencing Kit (Life 
Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with M13 primers (IDT), and sequencing products 
were electrophoresed on an Applied Biosystems 3730 DNA Analyzer. Data analysis was 
performed using QIAxcel Advanced System (Qiagen), Sequence Scanner Software (Life 
Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and NCBI BLAST.
Genome Sequencing and Reanalysis Results
Genome sequencing (GS) was performed on peripheral blood samples from the patient, 
her parents, and an unaffected full sister. The report from UDN’s contracted laboratory 
noted a deep intronic variant in PTCH1 (c.3+314G>T; NM_0010083602.2) in addition to a 
previously reported VUS in the CHD7 gene (c.8077G>C; p.Gly2693Arg, NM_017780.3). 
The intronic PTCH1 variant was considered likely benign as it was present in the unaffected 
father and sister.
Our team reanalyzed the GS data through the Utah Center for Genetic Discovery (UCGD) 
research workflows and through ARUP’s workflows (Emedgene, Illumina Inc.). Structural 
variants were evaluated using the software tools RUFUS and Smoove (Layer et al., 2014), 
both of which called a 38.3 Mb de novo balanced inversion, inv(9)(q22.32q34.2), with 
the proximal breakpoint located in intron 2 of the PTCH1 gene (GRCh38 chr9:95503702–
133803027). This inversion is predicted to result in either a truncated protein or mRNA 
subject to nonsense-mediated decay, leading to PTCH1 haploinsufficiency. The distal 
breakpoint disrupts the V A V2 gene, for which no disease association has been documented. 
This structural variant was confirmed by chromosome analysis, metaphase FISH, and Sanger 
sequencing (Figure 2 and Figure S1).
Discussion
Gorlin-Goltz syndrome (GGS) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder with an estimated 
occurrence of 1 in 40,000–60,000 individuals.(Spiker, Troxell, & Ramsey, 2024) A primary clinical 
concern in GGS is its associated risk for both malignant and non-malignant tumors, 
including medulloblastoma, odontogenic keratocysts (OKCs), and widespread basal cell

carcinomas (BCCs).(Bree, Shah, & Group, 2011) Early diagnosis is critically important for 
management, which includes tumor surveillance and risk reduction measures (e.g., sun 
protection and minimizing exposure to radiation). The diagnosis of GSS is primarily 
clinical, and current criteria do not require molecular confirmation. However, molecular 
confirmation can provide additional information and can be essential for optimal counseling 
and family risk assessment. Our patient met clinical criteria of GGS at four years of age, 
based on the combination of OKCs, vertebral anomalies, cleft lip and palate, and strabismus. 
The subsequent identification of palmar pits and calcified falx cerebri further strengthened 
the diagnosis. OKCs are particularly helpful in pointing to a diagnosis as they occur early 
and often (75%–90%) in GGS cases.(Scully, Langdon, & Evans, 2010)
However, the patient had unusual manifestations rarely seen in GGS, including abnormal 
semicircular canals (SCC) and Kallmann syndrome. Combined with the inability of clinical 
molecular testing to identify a causative mutation in known GGS genes (mainly PTCH1 and 
SUFU), her clinical manifestations raised the possibility of a novel gene-disease association, 
a phenotype expansion, or dual diagnoses. Research reanalysis of genome sequencing data 
identified a de novo inversion disrupting PTCH1, which explains her GGS phenotype. 
PTCH1 encodes the patched 1 protein, a transmembrane receptor for sonic hedgehog 
(SHH) and other hedgehog ligands.(Gailani et al., 1996) PTCH1 appears to function as a tumor 
suppressor in basal cell carcinoma and OKCs, whereas congenital malformations such as 
cleft lip and palate appear to be related to alterations in the SHH developmental pathway 
(Evans et al., 1993). The molecular pathology of PTCH1 in GGS indicates loss-of-function of 
PTCH1 and pathogenic variants include deletions, nonsense, missense, splice-site mutations, 
and, occasionally, deep intronic variants.(Evans et al., 1993)
The clinical and genomic evaluation noted several findings with implications for the family 
and more generally for clinical practice. First, the large (38.3 Mb) balanced inversion on 
chromosome 9 (inv(9)(q22.32q34.2)) was not identified by targeted sequencing of PTCH1, 
clinical exome sequencing, or genomic microarray testing. The inability to detect balanced 
chromosome abnormalities is a known limitation of these tests, though the inversion was 
clearly evident in a subsequent chromosome analysis and confirmed by targeted metaphase 
FISH analysis. The balanced inversion was also missed by copy number variant detection 
tools routinely used in clinical genome analysis. It was identified, however, by the structural 
variant detection tools Smoove and RUFUS(Layer et al., 2014), which detect novel chromosome 
breakpoints. This diagnostic challenge underscores the value of in-depth evaluation of 
balanced structural chromosome variants, especially when the analysis can be guided 
by the clinical presentation. Although an earlier karyotype analysis in the workup could 
have expedited the identification of the underlying molecular etiology for GGS, karyotype 
resolution is insufficient to have precisely localized the inversion breakpoint within the 
PTCH1 gene. Additional molecular assessment would have been required to confirm that 
PTCH1 was disrupted. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first reported 
instance of a balanced inversion disrupting the PTCH1 gene leading to GGS. The variant 
occurred as a de novo event, which has been reported in 35–50% of GGS cases.(Evans et al., 
1993)

From a clinical perspective, the patient’s presentation highlights rare manifestations 
that may be underdiagnosed or underappreciated in GGS, including hypogonadotropic 
hypogonadism and anosmia (Kallmann syndrome). Although both findings were reported 
by Ward et al. in 1960 in one of the initial families diagnosed with GGS,(Ward, 1960) these 
features have been reported subsequently in only a few families, with anosmia being more 
frequently diagnosed in family members than hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.(Barraud et al., 
2021; Wallace et al., 1973)
At the same time, the presence of a CHD7 variant in our patient raised the possibility of 
a dual diagnosis that could account for Kallmann syndrome, abnormal SCC, and perhaps 
the cleft palate, as all these findings have been reported in patients with a CHD7-related 
disorder without the full CHARGE syndrome phenotype.(Jongmans et al., 2009; Raivio, 2012) 
Chd7 haploinsufficiency in mice has been shown to cause genital hypoplasia and delayed 
puberty, with reduced expression of Fgfr1 in the olfactory placodal region and fewer 
hypothalamic GnRH neurons(Layman, Hurd, & Martin, 2011), similar to Kallmann syndrome 
in humans. Conversely, whereas abnormal SCC are a well-known hallmark of CHARGE 
syndrome and related phenotypes, they have also been reported rarely in GGS.(Villavicencio, 
Walterhouse, & Iannaccone, 2000) In animal studies, the sonic hedgehog (Shh) pathway has been 
shown to play a role in the development and specification of the inner ear ventral (auditory) 
and dorsal (vestibular) structures. Specifically, the Shh receptor Ptch1 is expressed in the 
otic epithelium and periotic mesenchyme, with lower expression in the dorsal vestibular 
organs and progressively higher expression towards the ventral cochlea. (Riccomagno et al., 
2002; Shin et al., 2017) In summary, it is still possible that the PTCH1 disruption could account 
for the patient’s entire phenotype.
On the other hand, the patient also fits the criteria for CHARGE syndrome, with two 
major criteria (hypoplastic SCC canal, oral cleft) and additional minor criteria (anosmia, 
hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, Chiari type I malformation, and renal cyst). Such dual 
diagnoses are increasingly reported, particularly in cohorts evaluated by undiagnosed disease 
programs, where they may occur in 5 to 8% of study participants.(Posey et al., 2017) However, 
the genomic evidence for a role of the CHD7 variant in the phenotype is not conclusive. The 
variant (c.8077G>C) is not present in population databases (gnomAD v2-v4, ExAC, 1000 
Genomes), nor has it been reported in individuals with CHD7-related disease. Computer 
modeling is conflicting on the question of a deleterious effect on the protein. This CHD7 
variant is maternally inherited, and the mother is apparently asymptomatic, with reportedly 
average age at puberty, though she declined inner ear imaging or further evaluations 
including formal anosmia testing. There was no evidence of maternal mosaicism in the 
genome sequencing data on blood (21 reference allele reads and 27 alternative allele reads), 
but mosaicism in other tissues cannot be ruled out and could conceivably explain maternal 
non-penetrance for Kallman syndrome manifestations. Targeted methylation testing on 
blood in the proband (EpiSign, Greenwood Genetic Center -ggc.org/episign) was reported 
as normal. While CHD7 episignatures theoretically demonstrate high sensitivity (70–100%), 
their clinical utility is limited by variability in methylation profiles and occasional discordant 
results.(Husson et al., 2024) No other epigenetic signatures were analyzed.

Therefore, a dual diagnosis is possible but not conclusively demonstrated, as several 
manifestations (e.g., Kallman syndrome, orofacial clefting) have been described both in 
patients with PTCH1-related GGS and CHD7-related conditions, albeit with different 
frequency (see supplementary table 2). Nevertheless, if a role of CHD7 is discounted, this 
would be the first report of abnormal SCC in GGS.
In summary, this phenotype-driven evaluation of a patient with a clinical diagnosis of 
GGS and additional unusual clinical manifestations identified a novel balanced de novo 
chromosome inversion leading to loss of function of PTCH1, thus resolving her primary 
diagnosis. In addition, a dual diagnosis that includes a partial CHD7-related phenotype may 
explain what would otherwise be a rare novel finding in GGS, namely, the presence of 
abnormal semicircular canals. More broadly, her diagnostic journey highlights the strengths 
and limitations of different genetic testing modalities, the value of pursuing diagnostic 
confirmation with a combination of clinical and research testing that can resolve structural 
chromosome variants, and the role of phenotype in guiding genomic analyses. For the 
family, the information helped target and improve management and counseling, especially 
with respect to clinical surveillance, endocrinologic treatment, and reproductive risk.
