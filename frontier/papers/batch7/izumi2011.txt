CLINICAL REPORT
Familial 9q22.3 Microduplication Spanning PTCH1
Causes Short Stature Syndrome With Mild Intellectual
Disability and Dysmorphic Features
Kosuke Izumi,1,2,3* Amanda Hahn, 2 Laurie Christ,2 Christine Curtis,1,2 and Derek E Neilson 1,2,3
1Department of Genetics, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, Ohio
2Center for Human Genetics, University Hospitals Case Medical Center, Cleveland, Ohio
3Department of Pediatrics, Rainbow Babies and Children’s Hospital, Cleveland, Ohio
Received 30 December 2009; Accepted 26 January 2011
Partial trisomy 9q involving the duplication of band 9q22 is
manifested by a constellation of symptoms including short
stature, intellectual disability, microcephaly, pyloric stenosis,
facial dysmorphism, and various defects of the heart, distal
extremities, eyes, thyroid, and esophagus. In three family mem-
bers with growth retardation, mild intellectual disability, and
mild facial dysmorphism, array-based comparative genomic
hybridization analyses showed a familial microduplication at
9q22.3. On the basis of the described functions of the duplicated
genes, PTCH1 represents a candidate gene that may be respon-
sible for the phenotypic ﬁndings, although the 14 other genes in
this duplicated segment may also contribute to the phenotype.
The current report provides evidence to support a speciﬁc
phenotype associated with a 9q22.3 microduplication and con-
ﬁrm localization of a subset of the trisomy 9q phenotype to this
chromosomal region. /C2112011 Wiley-Liss, Inc.

INTRODUCTION
‘‘Partial trisomy 9q’’ syndrome comprises a clinically recognizable
group of heterogeneous chromosomal rearrangements, involving,
at a minimum, the duplication of the 9q22.1 –q31 region [Heller
et al., 2000]. Clinical ﬁndings attributable to these varied duplica-
tions include short stature, intellectual disability, microcephaly,
pyloric stenosis, facial dysmorphism, and developmental defects
involving the heart, distal extremities, eyes, thyroid, and esophagus
[Heller et al., 2000; Hengstschlager et al., 2004]. We report on a
familial case of partial trisomy 9q in which array-based comparative
genomic hybridization (CGH) and ﬂuorescence in situ hybridiza-
tion (FISH) analyses show a set of 15 duplicated genes contributing
to a phenotype consistent with prior reports.

3 years.

Sister of the Propositus
The younger full sister of the propositus had a prenatal course

7 months of gestational age. Delivery was by cesarean due to breech

of 2.0 kg, <5th centile) and has remained so, with her height,
weight, and head circumference persistently below the 3rd centile.
Hypertonicity of the upper limbs and neck was present from
birth. She maintained an abducted and hyperextended arm posi-

could bring her arms to the midline, but because of the hyperto-
nicity, the gross motor milestones related to the arms were
delayed; she held objects at the age of 1 year and transferred
an object from the left to the right hand at 1.5 years of age. The
rest of her development was normal. She rolled at an age of
6–7 months, walked at 13 months, and used three word sentences
at 2 years.
On physical exam at the age of 2 years, she was small and slender.

Mother of the Propositus
The mother of the propositus was 30 years old at the time of the
initial evaluation. She was short (height: 150.5 cm, <5th centile)
and borderline microcephalic (head circumference: 52 cm,
/C24 3rd centile). Earlier growth parameters were normal; at the age
of 10 years, her height was 143 cm (/C24 75th centile) and she weighed
37 kg (/C24 75th centile). Developmentally, she showed relatively poor
performance in school, but was able to complete high school and
attend a technical college. As an adult, she had been treated with
dextroamphetamine for attention deﬁcit disorder and learning
disabilities.
Her past medical history was signiﬁcant for the co-occurrence
of endocrinopathies such as precocious puberty, which started
at 5 years of age, type 2 diabetes mellitus, polycystic ovarian
syndrome, and hypercholesterolemia. At the age of 10 years, she
had a history of abdominal fullness, caused by hematocolpos from
an imperforate hymen. Because of this, the timing of menarche
could not be established. She was diagnosed with late-onset adrenal
hyperplasia with elevated levels of androgen and 17-hydroxypro-

on dexamethasone and spironolactone treatment. Despite these

The adult heights of the rest of the family members were

METHODS
Cytogenetic and Molecular Studies
Array-based CGH analyses of the patients’ peripheral blood sam-
ples were performed at Signature Genomic Laboratories with
SignatureChip V4.0, which contains over 1,887 genomic bacterial
artiﬁcial chromosome (BAC) clones with a resolution of 100 kilo-
bases (kbs). The results of the array-based CGH analyses were
conﬁrmed using iterative FISH experiments, which were performed
to further deﬁne the chromosomal duplication using multiple BAC
clones. The FISH studies were performed using standard protocols
with BAC probes. In these FISH assays, both metaphase and
interphase cells were examined.

Using FISH analyses, the proximal breakpoint was localized
within or near the outer edge of the BAC RP11-1007A10

breakpoint was localized within or near the outer edge of the BAC

physical length of the duplication between 2.32 and 2.70 megabases
(Mbs). Genes fully contained within the region include WNK2,
C9orf129, FAM120AOS, FAM120A, PHF2, BARX1, PTPDC1,
ZNF169, FAM22F, HIATL1, FBP2, FBP1, C9orf3, FANCC, and

DISCUSSION
The described family manifests an apparently de novo duplication
of 9q22.3 detected by array-based CGH analysis, which allows
for improved genotype/phenotype correlations. The phenotypic
characteristics of the partial trisomy 9q syndrome ascribed to this
acquired genetic material include microcephaly, developmental
delay, short stature, and facial dysmorphism. The duplicated
genomic region contains 15 genes, all of which represent possible
candidate genes for these phenotypes.
This family demonstrates striking similarities to a previous
report of a mother and son with a 360 Kb microduplication
of 9q22 that contained only the PTCH1 gene and the noncoding
exon 1 of FANCC [Derwinska et al., 2009]. Shared clinical
ﬁndings in the two kindreds include microcephaly, facial
dysmorphism, and mild developmental delay (Table I). The dupli-
cation reported here is apparently de novo, and the breakpoints of
the duplication are distinct; this excludes the possibility of a
shared familial duplication in the two affected kindreds. Similar
cases of duplications were not found in the Database of
Genomic Variants (http://projects.tcag.ca/variation/). A 0.82-Mb
microduplication of 9q22.32 was reported as a familial variant
in the DECIPHER database (https://decipher. sanger.ac.uk/
application/), but the phenotypic information of that individual
(248456) is unavailable. The current report provides evidence
to support a speciﬁc phenotype associated with a 9q22.3
microduplication.
Gain of function mutations in PTCH1 have been postulated
to result in holoprosencephaly [Ming et al., 2002], leading to
the suggestion that PTCH1 duplication be considered in cases
of holoprosencephaly through attenuation of sonic hedgehog
signaling [Derwinska et al., 2009]. Holoprosencephaly did not
occur in these three patients, nor in the previous report of
PTCH1 microduplication, suggesting that duplication of the
regulatory elements may reduce this potential effect. In contrast,
central nervous system malformations of the posterior fossa, are
relatively common in duplications of 9q [Sepulveda et al., 2003].
The vermian hypoplasia present in the propositus represents
the smallest 9q duplication in association with this ﬁnding,
but genes other than PTCH1 may have contributed to the
malformation.

Microdeletions of the paternal allele of 9q22.3 are associated
with overgrowth [Shimojima et al., 2009]. The family members
in the current case study and one patient previously
reported demonstrate short stature [Derwinska et al., 2009].
This provides the interesting speculation that deletions and
duplications of 9q22.3 may demonstrate reciprocal effects on
height.
In summary, we present a case of familial 9q22.3 duplication,
including PTCH1, which was associated with microcephaly, devel-
opmental delay, short stature, and facial dysmorphism in the
affected individuals. Our report has conﬁrmed the ﬁndings of
recent case reports and has expanded the phenotype to include
vermian hypoplasia and short stature.
