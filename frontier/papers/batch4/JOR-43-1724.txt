ABSTRACT
Growth plate injury, which may trigger growth plate fusion or arrest, is a common occurrence in pediatric orthopaedics that can
lead to angulation of the limb and limb length discrepancy. Growth plate injuries are currently treated palliatively, with surgical
intervention when severe limb length discrepancy or angulation is found during follow ‐up visits. The cellular changes in the
chondrocytes in injured growth plate have not been fully elucidated, and understanding these factors will provide important
insights for the development of therapies that promote growth plate repair and prevent growth arrest. In this study, we refined a
mouse growth plate injury model and demonstrated that needle ‐induced growth plate injury resulted in different healing
outcomes depending on the size of the needle used. The 30G needle ‐induced injury was repaired without significant sequelae,
while 25G injury induced bone bridge and limb length discrepancy. In the non ‐healing model, the growth plate showed severely
reduced bone‐forming activity rapidly after the injury, with a decreased number of osteoclasts in the chondro ‐osseous junction
in the metaphysis. We found that the injured growth plate stimulated retinoid signaling evidenced by the upregulation of
retinoid target molecules, and that treatment with 7C, a retinoic acid receptor gamma antagonist, ameliorated decreases in bone
forming activity and osteoclast formation, and partially rescued injury ‐induced growth inhibition. The results of this study
suggest that changes in the growth plate immediately post ‐injury should be considered as a therapeutic target for growth plate
repair and that retinoid antagonists may be viable pharmacological interventions for this purpose.
1 | Introduction
Fractures are common orthopaedic problems in children, with
approximately 15 ‐30% involving growth plate (GP, also called
physis) [ 1, 2 ]. The GP is the hub of endochondral ossification
and is responsible for the growth of the long bones [ 3, 4 ];
however, an injury to the GP may impair its function [ 1, 5]. The
failure of functional recovery in the injured GP may trigger
premature arrest or fusion, resulting in angulation of the limb
and/or a limb length discrepancy (LLD) [ 5, 6]. These conditions
can subsequently impair smooth limb movement associated
with pain, and further increase the risk of osteoarthritis [ 6].
The location of fracture within the GP is a key factor in the
prognosis of growth disturbances [ 1, 5 ]. Fractures across a
portion or full length of GP with propagation through the epi-
physeal ossification center and into the metaphysis result in a
higher risk of growth arrest [ 5]. In such cases, fracture
communication between the epiphysis and metaphysis may
allow for the formation of an osseous bridge (bone bridge or

The treatment of GP injuries is initially palliative noninvasive
procedures, and then during follow ‐up of GP injuries, surgical
interventions may be required to reduce LLD and restore ana-
tomical alignment of the joints, epiphysis, and growth plates
[6]. Correction of limb angulation or LLD can be a significant
burden for patients. To date, most clinical and preclinical
studies have primarily focused the bone bridge as a therapeutic
target [ 7–9]; however while the surgical removal of the bone
bridge may ameliorate growth arrest, the outcome may depend
on how well the GP retains its function. Understanding the
cellular changes in chondrocytes in early stages of GP injury is
important for stimulating GP repair and preventing growth
arrest.
Chondrocytes in the GP undergo a series of functional changes,
organizing distinct layers named resting, proliferating, pre ‐
hypertrophic and hypertrophic zones [ 3, 4 ]. GP chondrocytes
undergo active proliferation, the abundant production of carti-
lage matrix, and terminal differentiation characterized by
hypertrophy, matrix degradation and calcification. Terminally
differentiated chondrocytes and their calcified matrix are
eventually invaded by blood vessels and osteoclasts, and sub-
sequently replaced with bone. The rate and extent of phenotype
changes in GP chondrocytes are sophisticatedly controlled by
various factors including vitamins, hormones, growth factors
and cytokines [ 10, 11 ].
When the GP is injured, chondrocytes respond to the injury and
attempt to repair the defect. In prior studies, the GP was
shortened 10 ‐12 weeks post ‐injury in the proximal tibia of
rabbits [ 12] and 7 weeks post ‐injury in the distal femur of rats
[13] as determined by radiographical or histological evaluation.
In contrast, Drenkard et al. [ 14] reported that the GP was locally
thickened at the site of the injury in the distal femoral GP as
early as 1 week post ‐injury, and the thickening was corelated
with the disturbance of bone growth. Histological evaluation in
the aforementioned study revealed thickening of the hyper-
trophic and resting zones, with a simultaneous decrease in the
ratio of the proliferating zone [ 14]. Recent studies have dem-
onstrated that skeletal stem cells in the resting zone propagate
3 days post ‐injury and contribute to the repair process in a
repairable growth plate injury model in mice [ 15]. However, it
remains unclear which changes in molecular pathways cause
the disorganization and dysfunction of injured GPs, leading to
growth arrest. To answer this question, we need to determine
the spatiotemporal changes of injured GPs at the cellular and
molecular levels using a rigorous and reproducible GP injury
model. In this study, we refined a murine GP injury model that
minimizes damage to surrounding tissues to reduce the com-
plexity of the surgical procedure and to mitigate the indirect
effects of other tissue reactions, and investigated early changes
in the injured GP.
Controlling retinoid signaling is important for the regulation of
cartilage development and GP function [ 16]. RAR γ dominantly
mediates retinoid signaling in the GP [ 17]. Retinoid signaling
activity is low in most regions of the GP while it is elevated in
the terminally differentiated chondrocytes in the hypertrophic
zone [ 18]. Excessive activation of retinoid signaling in the GP
impairs proliferation [ 19] and accelerates hypertrophy of
chondrocytes [ 20]. Of note, an association was found between
the upregulation of retinoid signaling and injury in a variety of
tissues, including the skeletal muscle, heart, liver, and lungs
[21, 22 ]. We, here, examined whether retinoid signaling was
also upregulated in the injured GP. As we previously demon-
strated that a retinoid antagonist had an inhibitory action on
growth disturbance caused by a cancer drug [ 23], we aimed to
determine whether retinoid antagonists also have an inhibitory
effect on GP injury ‐induced growth inhibition.
2 | Methods
2.1 | Animals
All animal experiments were conducted according to the pro-
tocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use
Committee of the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia and
University of Maryland, Baltimore. A total of 201 mice were
used: 39 male and 47 female C57BL/6J mice for characteriza-
tion of GP injury, and 45 male and 70 female C57BL/6 J mice
for 7C treatment experiments. GP injuries were induced in
the right proximal tibial GP in 3.5 to 4 ‐week‐old C57BL/6 J mice
(Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Mice received
intraperitoneal injections of 5 ‐ethynyl‐20‐deoxyuridine (EdU,
50 mg/kg, Sigma ‐Aldrich Inc. St. Louis, MO, USA) 2 h before
euthanasia to monitor cell proliferation and/or alizarin com-
plexon (30 g/kg, Sigma ‐Aldrich Inc.) 2 days before euthanasia.
The tibias were harvested 1 day to 5 weeks after surgery, with
the mice randomly divided into control and drug groups. Drug
response was assessed based on the differences in bone length
(primary outcome), bone formation activity, bone bridge for-
mation (secondary outcome), and cellular changes. Animal
treatment and sample analyses were performed in an unblinded
manner.
2.2 | Preparation and Systemic Administration of
Retinoids
Retinoic acid receptor gamma (RAR γ) antagonist 7C was
synthesized by Atomax Chemicals (Shenzhen, China) ac-
cording to the world patent application (WO 2005/066115 A2).
Stock solutions of 7C in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, D2650;
Sigma‐Aldrich Inc.) were stored at −30°C under argon. Before
administration, aliquots of the stock solution were mixed with
at h r e e‐fold volume of corn oil (C8267; Sigma ‐Aldrich Inc.)
and orally administered to the mice (1 mg/kg). Vehicle control
mice received 10 μL DMSO plus 30 μL corn oil in the same
manner. Treatment was started 1 day after the induction of the
GP injury and continued daily for 7 days.
2.3 | Statistical Analysis and Sample Size
Sample size was calculated using Power & Sample Size Calcu-
lator (null ‐hypothesis statistical test) provided by GIGA calcu-
lator ( https://www.gigacalculator.com/) with power 0.80,

3 | Results
3.1 | The GP Injury Model
Animal models for GP injuries usually involve creating a drill ‐
hole defect throughout the full depth of the GP in the tibia or
femur of rabbits [ 12], rats [ 8, 13], or mice [ 14]. This injury may
be made from the diaphysis or metaphysis to the end of the
epiphyseal bone [ 8, 12, 13, 24 ] or the articular cartilage to the
metaphysis [14]. These models create a clinical mimic of the GP
and are classified as Salter ‐Harris type IV [ 5], leading to bone
bridge formation and bone growth disturbances. However, this
procedure requires special skills to control the location, length,
and angle of the drill ‐holes. Surgery may produce inconsisten-
cies such as varying degrees of size and location of damage to
the articular cartilage, GP, and bone, making it difficult to
elucidate the detailed cellular and molecular changes which
occur during GP injury, repair, and arrest. To minimize damage
to the bone, articular cartilage and joint tissues, and to create a
reproducible defect on the GP, we created a transverse defect by
penetration of a needle into the GP of the proximal tibia from
the medial to lateral site (Figure 1A,B and Figure S‐1).
Figures 1C and 1D show images of the proximal tibial GP before
and after needle insertion. Changing the size of the needle
allowed us to control the size of the defect. The penetration of a
25‐gauge (25 G) needle made a full thickness defect in the
proximal tibial GP (Figure 1E), and induced bone bridge for-
mation (Figure 1F, histology; Figure 1G,H, μCT, red).
The effects of the different needle sizes were also investigated,
showing that the injuries made with the 18 G and 25 G needles
inhibited the growth of the affected bone (Figure 1I, 18 G and
25 G) and induced consistent and significant LLDs (Figure 1J,
18 G and 25 G) whereas the injuries made with the 30 G needles
did not (Figure 1I,J, 30 G). The outer diameters of the 18 G,
25 G, and 30 G needles are approximately 1.2, 0.5, and 0.3 mm,
respectively. The width of the GP of 3 ‐4 week old mice is
approximately 0.3 ‐0.4 mm [ 25]. Therefore, the 30 G needle was
not large enough to create a full ‐thickness defect in the GP that
induced growth disturbance, meaning the affected GP was
likely repaired. Injuries with a 25 G needle resulted in LLDs in
both females and males (Figure 1K), and sex differences in the
extent of bone shortening were not detected (Figure 1L).
Figure 2 shows the histology of the GP injured with a 25 G needle.

3.2 | Early Changes in the Injured GP
Animal studies on GP injury have largely focused on bone
bridge formation, which occurs within a certain period of time
post‐injury [ 7, 8 ]. Little information is available on how GPs
rapidly change their functions. Using our growth injury model,
we investigated the early changes in the injured GP. Prolifera-
tion was assessed by analyzing EdU uptake 1 and 3 weeks post ‐
injury. No significant difference in EdU uptake was detected
1 week post ‐injury (Figure 3A,B, and E, 1 week), whereas
3 weeks post ‐injury, EdU uptake decreased in the injured GP
(Figure 3C,D, and E, 3 weeks). The GP chondrocytes except for
chondroprogenitors in the resting zone, are rapidly transitioned
from a proliferating phase to a hypertrophic phase and disap-
pear from the GP. To examine differences in cell turnover,
proliferating GP chondrocytes were labeled with EdU before
surgery, and the distribution of the EdU ‐labeled cells was ex-
amined 4 days post ‐injury. The number of EdU ‐labeled cells in
the hypertrophic zone was significantly higher in the injured
than that in the contralateral GP (Figure 3F‐J), suggesting that
cell turnover was slower in the injured GP.
This finding suggests that the GP function is acutely impaired by
injury. To examine bone forming activity in the primary spon-

4 | Discussion
4.1 | GP Injury Mouse Models
In this study, we refined the GP injury mouse model and
demonstrated that injuries in the GP induced with various
needle sizes produced different healing results. The injury cre-
ated with a 30 G needle was repaired without significant
sequelae while the injury created with a 25 G needle resulted in
bone bridge and LLD. The use of these two models would be
helpful in studying differences in the mechanisms underlying
GP repair and growth arrest, as the healing results were likely
dependent on whether the needle diameter exceeded the width
of the GP. Connecting the epiphyseal bone marrow to meta-
physeal bone marrow may prevent integration of the edges of
the damaged GP and lead to bone bridge formation. Therefore,
the needle size should be adjusted for each mouse strain
depending on the width of GP. Additionally, age at the time of
surgery is important. At younger ages, when the secondary
ossification center has not yet been established, complete repair
is likely, even with a larger needle size. However, a needle
smaller than the width of the GP may cause growth arrest at
older ages because GP activity declines with age. The 25 G
injury model has an injury line penetrating from epiphysis to
metaphysis and consistently induces bone bridge and LLD. It is
difficult to define this model according to the Salter ‐Harris
classification: induction of consistent LLD is a feature of a
Salter‐Harris type IV, but the fracture line resembles a Salter ‐
Harris type II. In contrast, the 30 G injury model confines the
injury within the growth plate and may be considered a Salter ‐
Harris type I fracture. Compared to the GP injury models pre-
viously reported [ 8, 12–14, 24], our GP injury model minimized
damage to surrounding tissues such as the diaphysis of bone
and articular cartilage, and thereby may not mimic the true
clinical situation, which is a limitation of this model. However,
due to the small size of mouse bones, it is difficult to replicate
drill holes in the GP and surrounding tissues at exactly the same
location and angle, which can be a biasing factor in experi-
ments. Our models may be a valuable tool for the precise and
detailed analysis of injury ‐induced functional and molecular
changes in GP chondrocytes.
Sun et al. [ 30] have recently reported a sophisticated mouse GP
injury model using compound tricolor reporter transgenic mice
that can locate collagen 2 ‐expressing and collagen 10 expressing
areas. They used 2 ‐week‐old mice and made a defect, 0.5 mm in
diameter and depth, from the metaphyseal bone to the hyper-
trophic zone [ 30]. Both our 25 G injury model and the tricolor
model limit damage to the GP and consistently induce bone
bridge formation and LLD induction. Our model minimizes
damage to surrounding tissues and focuses on studying the
direct response of growth plate chondrocytes to injury, and may
be less clinically relevant than the tricolor model, which mimics
a Salter‐Harris type 2 fracture [ 30]. Relying on visual inspection
of the growth plate without being guided by reporter indicators
may result in less accurate positioning, but instead, any mice
can be used in our model. The growth plate in 3 ‐4 week old
mice was clearly detected under the stereomicroscope
(Figure S‐1). We used 3 ‐4 week old mice while a tricolor
mouse model used 2 week old mice. One of the reasons we
chose 3 ‐4 weeks of age instead of 2 weeks of age is maturity of

4.2 | Growth Arrest Caused by GP Injuries
This study demonstrated that the GP changes rapidly post ‐
injury, preceding bone bridge formation. The changes found
1 week post ‐injury include slower cell turnover as well as
decreased bone ‐forming activity and osteoclast number in the
chondro‐osseous junction in the metaphysis. These results
indicated that the endochondral ossification process was
impaired by the injury. In particular, a decrease in bone form-
ing activity is directly associated with growth arrest [ 26]. The
decrease in bone‐forming activity continued 3 weeks post ‐injury
(Figure S‐1). Although bone formation was histologically evi-
dent 3 weeks post ‐injury, the connectivity of the bone bridge to
the trabecular bones in the metaphysis was less evident than
that 5 weeks post ‐injury (Figure 2E,G). These findings
suggest that the inhibition of longitudinal bone growth occurs
before the physical block of growth by the bone bridge. Since
proliferative activity was not reduced 1 week post ‐injury, the
reason for the decreased cell turnover is likely a delay in events
required for bone replacement, including the terminal differ-
entiation of chondrocytes, vascular invasion, absorption of cal-
cified matrix and differentiation of osteoprogenitors. Another
possibility is the inactivation of resting chondrocytes/chon-
droprogenitors that supply new chondrocytes to the GP, as
previously suggested [ 15].
The initial treatment for GP injury currently monitoring injury
healing, pain and growth imbalance [ 33]. If growth imbalance
in a bone or between the affected and unaffected bones is
predicted to cause severe LLD or skeletal deformity, temporal
or permanent epiphysiodesis is a surgical option to slow or
stop unaffected GP function [ 6, 9, 34 ]. Clinical and preclinical
studies have explored intervention techniques and materials to
stimulate GP repair and prevent LLD. Successful reduction in
bone bridge formation by quick release of the antibody against
vascular endothelial growth factor has been reported; how-
ever, the inhibition of growth disturbance by this treatment
has not been identified [ 8]. The restoration of the GP injury ‐
induced defect with the poly(ethylene glycol) ‐based resin with
hydrogels containing glycosaminoglycans or TGFb3 represents
a positive action on the improvement of LLD [ 35]. The
application of mesenchymal stem cells into injured GP has
been studied clinically and preclinically; however, their
effectiveness has not been concluded [ 36]. Our findings sug-
gest the possibility that the amelioration of early impairment
of the GP function may effectively prevent future growth arrest
and imbalance.
4.3 | Upregulation of Retinoid Signaling in the
GP Repair
We observed that retinoid signaling was acutely increased in
injured GP. The activation of retinoid signaling in injured tis-
sues is generally a pro ‐action for tissue repair, though not
always [ 21]. Retinoid signaling supports the proliferation and
differentiation of progenitor cells and/or reactivation of cell
programs in various injured tissues [ 37]. The expression of
ALDH1A3, a rate ‐limiting enzyme in the production of retinoic
acid from vitamin A, increases in myofibroblasts surrounding
acutely injured tubules in the kidney, leading to the stimulation
of retinoid signaling that supports tubule repair for a short
period [38, 39]. Upregulation of retinoid signaling has been also
found immediately post ‐muscle injury [ 22, 40 ], and the
administration of agonists for RAR γ stimulates muscle repair
[22] and inhibits fat infiltration [ 40] in animal models. Fur-
thermore, the clinical benefits of vitamin A derivatives in skin
repair have been demonstrated [ 37].

that the injured GP having hyper activation of retinoid signaling
showed a decrease in new bone formation and osteoclast for-

In contrast, treatment with retinoic acid strongly enhances the
expression of hypertrophic traits and matrix mineralization in
cultured avian chondrocytes [ 45]. Beta ‐carotene, a precursor of
vitamin A and low concentration of retinoic acid stimulate the
early differentiation of osteoblasts in culture [ 46]. The
inactivation of RAR signaling in osteoblasts induces craniofacial
deformities [ 47] and osteopetrotic conditions [ 48] in mice.
Furthermore, retinoic acid has been reported to enhance
RANKL‐induced osteoclast formation [ 49], and RARα‐mediated
signaling in bone marrow stromal cells supports osteoclast
formation in culture [ 47]. These findings highlight the physio-
logical importance of retinoid signaling in skeletal growth and
maintenance. It is likely that retinoid signaling activated in the
injured GP should be considered a pathological dysregulation
but not an enhancement of its endogenous role, as seen in other
tissues [ 37].
Our results suggest that 7 C, a RAR γ antagonist, may be useful
in suppressing the hyperactivation of retinoid signaling in
injured GPs, attenuating GP arrest and bridge formation, and
correcting bone growth pathology. The 7 C was administered
systemically for the first 7 days post ‐injury but did not com-
pletely block the injury ‐induced pathology. The 7 C regimen
was set for 7 days, as there was no information on its toxicity
beyond this period. Future studies should be conducted to
optimize the treatment frequency, duration, and dosage to
maximize therapeutic efficacy without causing significant side
effects. Furthermore, targeted applications of 7 C should be
investigated, for example, by experiments that demonstrate the
complementary effect of 7 C loaded into polylactide nano-
particles with BMP2 on endochondral ossification in a mouse
spinal fusion model [ 50]. The therapeutic action of the RAR γ
antagonist (CD2665) has been also demonstrated by preventing
the arrest of mouse GP induced by the antagonist of the
hedgehog pathway, LDE225 (sonidegib), which has been de-
veloped for the treatment of variety of cancers including basal
cell carcinomas and medulloblastomas [ 23]. On the other hand,
the RARγ agonist (palovarotene) has been approved as a clinical
drug to inhibit heterotopic ossification in fibrodysplasia ossifi-
cans progressiva, a fatal genetic skeletal disease [ 51]. Our
findings and those of others indicate that pharmacological
control of retinoid signaling may be an effective treatment for a
variety of skeletal disorders involving the endochondral ossifi-
cation process.
