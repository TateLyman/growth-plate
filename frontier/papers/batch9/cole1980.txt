summary 
We measured inorganic sulfate in 100 ~1 of deproteinized serum by micro- 
assay, using the 133Ba precipitation method. Infants and children under 3 yr 
(n = 46), children from 3 to 9 yr of age (n = 27) and adolescents (lo-18 yr, 
n = 12) in a hospitalized population, and healthy adults (n = lo), were studied. 
An age-dependent curvilinear regression for serum sulfate characterizes the first 
group: the mean serum sulfate value falls from 0.47 mmol/l on the first day of 
life to 0.33 mmol/l at 36 months. Thereafter the mean value is about 0.33 
mmol/l. The 95% confidence intervals were defined for each group. 
Introduction 
Most methods for the measurement of serum sulfate require a large sample 
volume which precludes their use in the pediatric population. Altered serum 
and extracellular levels of this anion in states of variable nutritional and renal 
function can only be evaluated if normal values have been established. We have 
adapted the radio-labelled barium method [l] for microassay of sulfate in 
100 ~1 of serum [2] and report here, for the first time, the age-dependent 
serum sulfate concentrations in hospitalized pediatric subjects and healthy 
ambulant adults.

Materials and methods 
Serum samples 
Venous blood samples were taken from in-patients at the Montreal Children’s 
Hospital without concern for time of day or relation to meals. The blood was 
drawn into tubes without heparin, the serum separated at room temperature, 
then frozen at -2O”C, and analyzed within three to four weeks. Only samples 
from patients without clinical evidence of renal dysfunction or with normal 
values for serum urea nitrogen and creatinine were used. Samples were also ob- 
tained from healthy adult laboratory personnel, and from eight children of 
varying age with established renal failure. 
Serum sulfate assay 
Our modification of the barium precipitation method for microanalysis of 
serum sulfate has been described elsewhere [ 23. In brief, 100 ~1 of serum is 
treated with 50 ~1 of TCA (20% v/v) in methanol (50% v/v) and centrifuged 
briefly to sediment precipitated protein. To 100 1.11 of supernatant is added 
10 ~1 of 5 mmol/l Na,SO, followed by 15 ~1 of 10 mmol/l BaCl, containing 
approximately 2 X lo4 dpm 133Ba. The solution is held at 4°C for 3 h then spun 
in the cold at 9600 X g X 1 h. An aliquot (75 ~1) of supernatant is counted for 
y-emission. The quantity of sulfate in serum bears a stoichiometric relation- 
ship to the decrease in radioactivity of the supematant. 
Statistical 
Each value is the average of an assay in triplicate or quadruplicate. We used 
the Student t test to analyze differences between male and female values, and 
log transformation to derive homogeneous distributions among groups [ 51; the 
means and confidence limits for the various age groups were then calculated. 
Log transformation allowed us to calculate a curvilinear regression equation 
and 95% confidence limits in the O-36 months age group. 
Results 
We analyzed the sera from 85 children (47 female, 38 male) ranging in age 
from newborn to 18 years, and from 10 adults (2 female, 8 male). Serum sul- 
fate was not sex dependent in any age group and the values for male and female 
subjects have been combined for the analysis of age dependence, as shown in 
Fig. 1 and Table I. 
Serum sulfate values are higher in the infant than in later life. Curvilinear 
regression analysis reveals that the decline in serum sulfate concentration 
between birth and 3 years of age is significant (F1,44 = 4.93, p < 0.05). The pre- 
dicted mean value on the first day of life is 0.47 mmol/l with 95% confidence 
limits between 0.295 mmol/l and 0.945 mmol/l; by 3 years of age the predicted 
mean value is 0.33 mmol/l for which the 95% confidence limits are 0.225 
mmol/l to 0.675 mmol/l. Thereafter, the observed mean value is approximately 
0.33 mmol/l in the child, adolescent and adult, in accordance with previous 
values reported for the adult [ 1,6].

Discussion 
Free sulfate anion in body fluids is derived chiefly from the oxidation of 
organic sulfur, while a small fraction is contributed by intestinal absorption of 
sulfate and hydrolysis of sulfoesters. Sulfate is excreted from the body largely 
in the urine. Serum sulfate thus reflects the equilibration between intake, 
distribution in tissue pools, and renal excretion. The latter seems to be of par- 
ticular importance. With progressive renal failure, sulfate levels rise as the rate 
of glomerular filtration declines [3,4,7,8]. We found serum sulfate concentra- 
tions in eight children with varying’degrees of renal failure to be above the age- 
specific 95% confidence limit in keeping with previous observations in adults 
[7,8]. Thus, in moderate to severe uremia, a considerable fraction of the 
increased anion gap may be accounted for by sulfate and with the development 
of long-term management of renal failure in man, interest in sulfate homeosta- 
sis has been rekindled. 
The elevated serum sulfate values of young infants are of special interest, 
because glomerular filtration rate has long been known to be low in young 
infants [9], and because of the analogy to serum phosphate levels which are 
highest in the young infant [lo]. The finding suggests that the renal contribu- 
tion to sulfate homeostasis parallels that for phosphate anion. However we 
know from studies in progress that parathyroid hormone does not have a con- 
trolling effect on sulfate levels in serum (D.E.C. Cole and C.R. Striver, unpub- 
lished observations). 
Availability of free inorganic sulfate influences in vitro incorporation of the 
anion into biologically important sulfate esters, in particular the sulfated muco- 
polysaccharides [ll] and sulfated phenols [12,13]. It may be that elevated 
serum sulfate levels in the newborn have an important biological function in 
the development of competent mechanisms for connective tissue synthesis and 
drug metabolism. The observed inter-individual variation in serum sulfate is 
greater than that for serum phosphorus. There are three factors which may con- 
tribute to this finding: (1) the precision of the microassay for sulfate (CV = 
7%, ref. 2) is less than that for phosphorus (CV = 2.7%, Dr. Claire DuPont, 
personal communication); (2) the normal fluctuations in sulfate influx into the 
intravascular compartment are likely to be much larger than the analogous 
fluxes for phosphate; (3) there is no evidence for rapid feedback regulation of 
elevated sulfate. Further studies of sulfate metabolism will be aided by the 
method used here to define control values and, in turn, such studies should 
enhance our understanding of sulfate metabolism and homeostasis in man.
