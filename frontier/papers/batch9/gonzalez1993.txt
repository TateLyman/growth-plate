Effect of Inorganic Sulfate and Sulfur Amino Acids on 
Bone Mineralization in Broilers1 
N. J. GONZALEZ,2 T. W. SULLIVAN, J. H. DOUGLAS, and M. M. BECK 
Department of Animal Science, Institute of Agriculture and Natural Resources, 
University of Nebraska, Lincoln, Nebraska 68583-0908 
ABSTRACT Two experiments with a factorial arrangement of treatments 
were conducted to determine the effect of inorganic sulfate and sulfur amino 
acids (SAA) levels on bone mineralization and Ca excretion in broilers from 1 to 
21 days of age. Experiment 1 included two levels of Ca (.6 and 1.0%), two sulfate 
sources [copper sulfate (CuS04-5H20), and potassium sulfate (K2S04)], and 
three sulfate levels (.024, .034, and .044%). Experiment 2 included two Ca levels 
(.6 and 1.0%), two CP levels (18 and 22%), and three SAA levels (.93,1.23, and 
1.53%). Body weight gain, feed:gain, feed consumption, and tibia variables 
were measured in both experiments. In Experiment 2, Ca retention was also 
assessed. In Experiment 1, birds fed potassium sulfate and .6% Ca gained 
significantly more weight than birds fed copper sulfate and 1.0% Ca, respec­
tively. As sulfate level increased, tibia ash percentage increased and decreased 
linearly in birds fed copper sulfate and potassium sulfate, respectively (P < 
.012). In Experiment 2, birds fed 1.0% Ca, .93% SAA, and 22% CP performed 
better than the birds receiving the other levels. A low Ca level (.6%) was more 
detrimental for bone mineralization in birds fed 22% CP compared with birds 
fed 18% CP. There was no effect of SAA on tibia ash and Ca percentages. Fecal 
Ca excretion decreased linearly with SAA level (P < .01). The results suggest 
that inorganic sulfate from the potassium salt may adversely affect Ca deposi­
tion in bone. However, SAA supplementation up to 65% in excess of the 
requirement in starter broiler diets did not affect bone mineralization. 
(Key words: sulfate, sulfur amino acids, tibia ash, calcium, protein)

INTRODUCTION 
In spite of much effort, leg weakness 
continues to be a significant problem for 
the commercial poultry industry. Sulfate 
salts, including calcium sulfate, potassium 
sulfate, magnesium sulfate, and sodium 
sulfate, are utilized in broiler diets to meet 
mineral requirements. Copper sulfate is 
often used as a growth promotant (Scott et 
ah, 1982). However, limited attention has 
been given to the effects of sulfate from

that excess dietary protein and inorganic 
sulfate increase urinary Ca (Whiting and 
Draper, 1980, 1981; Zemel et al, 1981; 
Greger et ah, 1991) and appear to have an

In poultry, excess dietary protein and 
inorganic sulfate have been associated 
with increased incidence of leg abnormali­

linear increase in the incidence of rotated

fed protein levels from 24 to 39%. More­
over, an increased incidence of valgus-

were fed a high-protein diet from 3 to 20

Because protein level did not significantly 
affect BW gain at 20 wk, the authors 
hypothesized that the acid load per se due 
to the increase in catabolism of sulfur 
amino acids (SAA) may have induced the 
leg abnormalities. Stevens and Salmon 
(1989) also determined that turkey poults 
fed from 0 to 4 wk of age diets containing 
.558% DL-Met supplemented with .327% 
of sulfate as calcium sulfate (CaS04-2H20) 
and magnesium sulfate (MgS04-7H20) had 
an increased incidence of leg abnormali­
ties, especially at marginal dietary levels 
of Ca and P. 
Using broiler chickens, Skinner et al. 
(1991) observed that increasing the level of 
total amino acids up to 120% of the 
requirement significantly decreased per­
centage tibia ash in birds fed .5 and 1.0% 
Ca diets; however, the decline in tibia ash 
was more severe for birds fed .5% Ca. 
Halley et al. (1987) observed an increased 
incidence of varus deformation in broilers 
fed 1.8% sulfate from 1 day to 3 wk of age, 
but no effect on other variables such as 
bone ash. Edwards (1984) observed that 
although .31% sulfate in broiler diets 
significantly increased bone ash percent­
age over the control, the incidence of tibial 
dyschondroplasia (TD) was not affected. 
Unfortunately, all of these studies were 
conducted using levels of sulfate that 
greatly exceed the levels found in practical 
broiler diets. To date, experiments have 
not been performed to study the effect of 
excess SAA increasing total sulfur or acid 
load on bone mineralization in broilers, 
even though liberal use of SAA is com­
monly practiced in formulating commer­
cial diets. The objective of this study was 
to determine the effect of inorganic sulfate 
level derived from different sources, pro­
tein level, and excess SAA on bone 
mineralization in broilers.

RESULTS 
Experiment 1 
Only main effect treatment means for 
BW gain, feed:gain, and feed consumption 
at 21 days are presented because no signifi­
cant interactions were observed (Table 3). 
Birds receiving potassium sulfate had sig­
nificantly greater BW gain at 21 days than 
those receiving copper sulfate. The source 
of sulfate did not affect feed:gain at 21 days. 
Birds fed a marginal Ca level (.6%) gained 
significantly more weight and had better 
feed:gain at 21 days compared with birds 
fed 1.0% Ca. Body weight gain and feed: 
gain were not significantly affected by 
dietary sulfate level at 21 days of age. None 
of the treatments affected feed consump­
tion. 
The effect of sulfate source, and Ca, and 
sulfate level on tibia variables and leg 
weakness are presented in Table 4. As 
expected, percentage tibia ash and percent­
age tibia Ca were significantly greater for 
birds fed 1.0% Ca in relation to birds fed .6% 
Ca. There was a significant interaction 
between sulfate source and sulfate level for 
percentage tibia ash. Tibia ash increased 
linearly when increasing levels of sulfate 
were given in birds fed copper sulfate. 
However, when birds were fed potassium 
sulfate, tibia ash decreased linearly with the 
level of sulfate (P = .011). Increasing the 
level of sulfate in the diet decreased 
percentage tibia Ca (P = .067), but no 
significant interactions were observed for 
this variable. 
There were no significant effects of 
sulfate source on percentage tibia Ca. Tibia 
dry weight and the incidence of weak legs 
were not significantly affected by the differ­
ent dietary treatments, and no significant 
interactions were observed.

Experiment 2 
Body weight gain, feed consumption, 
and feed:gain data are presented in Table 5. 
Body weight gains at 21 days were signifi­
cantly greater and feed:gain significantly 
better for birds fed 22% CP than for birds 
fed 18% CP. Moreover, birds fed 1.0% Ca 
gained significantly more weight and con­
sumed significantly more feed than birds 
fed .6% Ca. Increasing the level of SAA 
linearly decreased BW gain and feed con­
sumption at 21 days. A significant CP by 
SAA interaction was observed for feed:gain 
at 21 days. When birds were fed 18% CP, 
increasing the level of SAA supplementa­
tion was detrimental for feed:gain. How­
ever, increasing the level of SAA did not 
affect feed:gain of birds fed 22% CP. 
The effect of treatment on tibia variables 
and Ca excretion are presented in Table 6. A 
Ca by CP level interaction (P = .054) was 
observed for tibia ash percentage. Tibia ash 
was lower in birds fed .6% Ca compared 
with birds fed 1.0% Ca. However, tibia ash 
decreased more with Ca level when birds 
were fed 22% CP. There was no effect of 
SAA on percentage tibia ash. Percentage 
tibia Ca was not significantly affected by 
either SAA, CP, or Ca level. 
Tibia dry weight was significantly 
greater for birds fed 1.0% Ca compared 
with birds fed .6% Ca. Increasing the level 
of SAA decreased tibia dry weight linearly. 
However, neither an effect of protein nor 
significant interactions were observed for 
tibia dry weight. A significant Ca by SAA 
interaction was observed for percentage Ca 
excretion. As SAA level increased in birds 
fed .6% Ca, Ca excretion did not change 
significantly. However, when birds were 
fed 1.0% Ca, Ca excretion had a quadratic (P 
< .003, not shown) response to SAA level.

known to increase acid excretion, which is 
accompanied by hypercalciuria and an 
increase in bone resorption (Whiting and

on Ca metabolism may explain the de­

creased in birds fed potassium sulfate. 
Sulfate anion can exert its effect through 
changes in blood acid-base balance toward

Results from the second experiment 
show that although all essential amino 
acids were fed at adequate levels, the 
chicks fed 18% CP had lower BW gain and 
feed:gain as compared with those fed 22% 
CP. These results agree with the findings 
of others (Fancher and Jensen, 1989). 
The detrimental effect of excess SAA on 
feed intake and performance is more 
likely a consequence of amino acid im­
balance. The imbalance was stronger in 
birds fed 18% CP compared with birds fed 
22% CP. The 18% CP diet was sup­
plemented with Lys, Arg, and Thr to meet 
or exceed the NRC (1984) guidelines for 
all essential amino acids. However, Han et 
al. (1992) determined that a 19% CP-corn-
soybean meal diet is also deficient in Val. 
A Val deficiency may have contributed to 
the apparent Met imbalance and conse­
quently lower performance of birds fed 
18% CP.
