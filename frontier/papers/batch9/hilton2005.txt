Abstract
Multiple Hereditary Exostoses (MHE) is an autosomal dominant skeletal disorder most frequently caused by mutations in the EXT1 gene.
MHE affects proper development of endochondral bones, such that all affected individuals present with exostoses adjacent to the growth
plate of long bones, while some individuals exhibit additional bone deformities. EXT1 functions as a heparan sulfate (HS) co-polymerase,
and when defective causes improper elongation of glycosaminoglycan side chains on core proteins of HS proteoglycans. Although analysis
of heterozygous EXT1-deficient mice has failed to reveal any significant gross morphological variations in skeletal development, significant
alterations in molecular signaling occur in the developing long bones. Our results indicate that defects in EXT1 and the resulting reduction in
HS lead to enhanced Indian Hedgehog diffusion causing an increase in chondrocyte proliferation and delayed hypertrophic differentiation.

Introduction
Multiple Hereditary Exostoses (MHE) is an autosomal
dominant skeletal disorder that is caused by mutations at
one of at least two distinct loci: 8q24.1 ( EXT1) [1] and
11p11–12 (EXT2) [2]. Most cases of MHE, about 70%, are
caused by mutations in the EXT1 gene. Individuals with
MHE present with exostoses, or cartilaginous benign bone
tumors, located at the growth plates of endochondral bones,
and additionally can display bone deformities, joint fusions,
and short stature (OMIM 133700). EXT1 is a member of the
EXT-related gene family that is composed of EXT1 [3],
EXT2 [4], EXTL1 [5], EXTL2 [6,7], and EXTL3 [7,8]. The
EXT1 gene encodes a type II transmembrane protein with
glycosyltransferase activity, and specifically functions as a
heparan sulfate (HS) co-polymerase with EXT2 in a hetero-
oligomeric complex [9,10]. The EXT1/EXT2 complex
elongates glycosaminoglycan (GAG) side chains on core
proteins of heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs).
Cell surface HS and HSPGs are known to play critical
roles in the extracellular matrix as co-receptors for signaling
molecules, modulators of protein transport, stabilizers for
cell–cell adhesion, and participate in many other aspects of
cellular communication [11]. For example, HS and HSPGs
are known to be important modulators of FGF/FGF receptor
interaction and specificity [12,13], the organization of Wnt
proteins at the cell surface [14], the interactions between
BMP dimers and their receptors [15,16], the interaction
between Noggin and the cell surface [17], and in maintain-
ing the proper morphogen gradient and diffusion of
Hedgehog (Hh) during development [18].
Since a haploinsufficiency of EXT1, and the correlated
reduction of HS, are implicated in the bone disorder of
MHE, impairments in one or more of the aforementioned

signaling pathways could be responsible for some or all of
the phenotypes associated with MHE. The process of
endochondral ossification is tightly regulated by many of
these signaling pathways. Indian hedgehog (Ihh) is a master
regulator of chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation
during endochondral bone development. It is expressed
early near the center of skeletal elements, and later becomes
restricted to prehypertrophic and hypertrophic chondrocytes
[19–21]. Ihh signals regulate the onset of hypertrophic
differentiation via an indirect regulation of parathyroid
hormone-related peptide, PTHrP , expressed in the periartic-
ular region. High concentrations of PTHrP maintain
immature chondrocytes in a proliferative state. When PTHrP
concentrations fall below a threshold level, chondrocytes
initiate hypertrophic differentiation and begin to express
Ihh. This indirect regulation controls the distance from the
periarticular region at which hypertrophic differentiation
occurs [21]. Ihh has also been shown to positively regulate
chondrocyte proliferation independent of its role in control-
ling differentiation by acting directly through proliferating
chondrocytes [22].
In order to understand the molecular details of MHE, Lin
et al. [23] disrupted the primary locus for MHE, EXT1,
through homologous recombination in mouse embryonic
stem (ES) cells. Since the EXT1 null ( EXT1 /C0//C0) mice are
embryonic lethal at E8.5, skeletal analysis could not be
performed. However, initial observations by Lin et al. [23]
demonstrated that heterozygous EXT1 mice (EXT1+//C0) live
to adulthood and display no gross skeletal phenotype during
post-natal development. Subsequent analyses of these adult
heterozygous mice have demonstrated mild reductions in
humerus and femur bone density as compared to wild-type
bones (unpublished data). In addition, cytological examina-
tion of the growth plates of adult EXT1+//C0long bones show
an increase in chondrocyte numbers within the columnar
growth plate, although the overall length of long bones are
comparable to those of wild-type mice (unpublished data).
Given the mild skeletal phenotype in the adult EXT1+//C0
mice, we examined embryonic bone development for
significant phenotypic changes, as well as changes in the
molecular mechanisms regulating endochondral ossifica-
tion. To examine the role EXT1 plays during endochondral
bone development, we have analyzed bone length and
morphology, chondrocyte proliferation, the expression of
specific markers of chondrocyte differentiation, as well as
the involvement and interaction of signaling pathways,
specifically the Ihh/PTHrP negative feedback loop.
Materials and methods
Animal husbandry
EXT1 mouse strains were previously described [23].
Mice were used in accordance to guidelines established by
the animal care facilities at the University of Houston.
Skeletal preparations
Skeletal preparations were performed on E14.5 and
E18.5 mouse embryos according to a modified protocol
based on McLeod [24]. Briefly, embryos were fixed in 95%
ethanol, treated with acetone, stained with an alazarin red/
alcian blue mixture (1 volume 0.3% Alcian Blue 8GS; 1
volume 0.1% Alizarin Red S type; 1 volume glacial acetic
acid; 17 volumes 70% ethanol), cleared in 0.5% or 1%
potassium hydroxide, and transferred through 20%, 50%,
and 80% glycerol solutions in 0.5% or 1% potassium
hydroxide.
In situ hybridization
Paraffin-embedded tissue sections (6 Am) were prepared
from wild type and EXT1+//C0embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5)
mouse limbs after overnight fixation in 4% paraformalde-
hyde at 4 8C. Antisense digoxygenin-labeled riboprobes
were generated using the MaxiScript In Vitro Transcription
kit (Ambion) with a digoxigenin RNA labeling mix
(Roche). Plasmid constructs used for probe preparation
were as follows: collagen 2a1 [25], collagen 10a1 [26],
indian hedgehog [19], and patched [27]. Prior to hybrid-
ization, sections were deparaffinized in xylene, taken
through an ethanol rehydration series, postfixed in 4%
paraformaldehyde, rinsed in PBS, and treated with 10 Ag/ml
Proteinase K and 0.1 M triethanolamine with acetic
anhydride for 10 min each followed by PBS rinses. Slides
were prehybridized at 65 8C [50% formamide, 5 /C2SSC, 2%
SDS, 2% Boehringer Blocking Reagent (BBR), 250 Ag/ml
tRNA, and 100 Ag/ml heparin] for 3 h and hybridization was
then carried out at 65 8C for at least 14 h. Following washes
and a 1-h blocking step in 20% heat-inactivated sheep serum
and 2% BBR, slides were treated with 1:2500 dilution of
anti-digoxigenin-AP fab fragments (Roche) overnight at
48C. Slides were washed and finally incubated in a NBT/
BCIP solution (125 Ag/ml BCIP and 250 Ag/ml NBT in
NTM) for 3–5 h at room temperature in order to detect a
color reaction.
Immunohistochemistry and BrdU analysis
Slides containing comparable sections from E16.5 mouse
limbs were used in the immunohistochemistry procedures
according to the Zymed HistoMouse-SP Kit (AEC) and
Zymed BrdU staining protocols with minor modifications.
Prior to peroxidase quenching, slides used for antibody
staining of Ihh were incubated with 1 mg/ml hyaluronidase at
378C for 15 min. On the day of harvesting E16.5 embryos for
BrdU analysis, the mother was given an intraperitoneal
injection of 0.1 ml per 10 g of body weight of a 10 mg/ml
bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) (Sigma): 1.2 mg/ml fluorodeox-
yuridine (FdU) (Sigma) stock solution. At least three different
sections were counted for four wild type and four EXT1+//C0
E16.5 mouse tibias in order to calculate BrdU indices.

Results
Skeletal analysis of EXT1 heterozygous mutant mouse
embryos
Wild-type and EXT1+//C0mouse embryos were cleared
and stained with alcian blue and alizarin red in order to
analyze skeletal morphology, bone length, and percent
ossification of endochondral bones during two stages of
embryonic skeletal development, E18.5 and E14.5. E18.5
embryos were analyzed primarily for phenotypes that would
be consistent with MHE. EXT1+//C0skeletons did not
display any observable bone deformities, occurrence of
exostoses, or changes in axial or appendicular skeletal
structure. Overall, the EXT1+//C0skeletons do not display a
significant change in skeletal morphology as compared to
wild-type embryos (data not shown). In addition, E14.5
embryos were similarly analyzed in order to detect potential
changes in proliferation during early stages of chondro-
genesis, prior to ossification and calcification of the
endochondral bones. Again, the EXT1+//C0embryos showed
no significant differences in their skeletal morphology at
this early stage of chondrogenesis when compared to wild-
type mice (data not shown).
To further analyze the size and degree of ossification of
long bones for EXT1+//C0and wild-type mice, E18.5
humeri, femurs, and tibias were dissected from mice used
for skeletal preparations. These bones were catalogued
using digital imaging equipment and measured with
Image-Pro Express software. EXT1+/ /C0long bones dis-
play no significant change in length or ossification as
compared to wild-type bones, although EXT1+//C0bones
show greater variation in both length and percent
ossification ( Table 1 ).
Proliferative and hypertrophic marker analysis in EXT1
heterozygous mutant long bones
In order to investigate potential changes in proliferation
or hypertrophic differentiation, in situ hybridization was
performed using riboprobes for collagen 2a1 , a marker for
proliferating chondrocytes, and collagen 10a1 , a marker for
hypertrophic chondrocytes. At E16.5, EXT1+//C0proximal
tibia sections exhibit a moderate increase, 6.6 F 1.1% ( n =
8; P b 0.05) in the expression domain of collagen 2a1 as
compared to wild-type littermates. The collagen 2a1
expression domain was measured from the periarticular
region to the final collagen 2a1 expressing cells near the
hypertrophic region ( Figs. 1 A,B). EXT1+//C0embryos also
display a reduction in the collagen 10a1 expression domain
in proximal tibia growth regions ( Figs. 1 C,D). At least
three sections were analyzed from eight animals of each
genotype ( n = 8). Similar results were also obtained from
forelimb growth plates of E16.5 EXT1+//C0and wild-type
animals (data not shown). In addition, quantitative RT-PCR
results demonstrate a near 44.2 F 3.0% ( n =7 ; P b 0.01)
reduction in collagen 10a1 expression in E18.5 whole
tibias. As a positive control, EXT1 expression was
quantified in wild-type and EXT1+//C0tibias. EXT1+//C0
tibias show over a 35.1 F 5.6% (n =7 ; P b 0.01) reduction
in EXT1 expression as compared to wild-type tibias
(Fig. 2 ). Similar results were also obtained when analyzing
E18.5 humeri from both EXT1 F and wild-type mice (data
not shown).
An increase in the proliferative region, as seen in the
expanded collagen 2a1 expression domain, suggests that
EXT1+//C0embryos display an increase in chondrocyte
proliferation and/or delay in hypertrophic differentiation.
The reduction in the collagen 10a1 expression do-
main, which is also located at a greater distance from
the periarticular region, provides further support for
an apparent change in the maturation of EXT1+//C0
chondrocytes.

EXT1 heterozygotes display altered Indian Hedgehog
signaling during endochondral bone development
To determine the molecular mechanisms responsible for
increased proliferation and delayed hypertrophic differ-
entiation of chondrocytes in EXT1+//C0embryos, we first
examined potential defects in Ihh signaling. Given the
well-documented role Ihh plays in chondrocyte prolifer-
ation and differentiation and the effect ttv, the Drosophila
EXT1 homologue, has on Hh diffusion and signaling [18],
we performed immunohistochemical and in situ hybrid-
ization analyses in order to determine the exact effect
EXT1 has on Ihh signaling during endochondral bone
development.
First, wild-type and EXT1+//C0E16.5 proximal tibia
sections were incubated with the 5E1 monoclonal anti-
body against the N-terminal peptide of Ihh. Comparison
of wild-type and EXT1+//C0sections generally show a
more diffuse staining pattern in EXT1+//C0sections for
Ihh.( Figs. 4 A,B). Despite the increased domain of Ihh
staining in EXT1+//C0sections, in situ hybridization data
revealed that Ihh expression is restricted to a more
discrete domain within the prehypertrophic region ( Figs.
4C,D). This reduction in the Ihh expression domain of
EXT1+//C0sections makes the increased Ihh diffusion more
significant.
Increased Ihh activity has been shown to increase
chondrocyte proliferation and delay the initiation of
hypertrophic differentiation [20–22]. To further address
the effect of EXT1 on Ihh signaling, in situ hybridization
was performed for the Ihh transcriptional target gene,
patched. Consistent with the observations of increased
Ihh diffusion in EXT1+//C0embryos, patched expression
is enhanced in the perichondrium flanking the prehy-
pertrophic region, and also expanded to proliferating
chondrocytes very near the periarticular region of E16.5
proximal tibia sections ( Fig. 5 ). The increase in Ihh
diffusion, enhanced and expanded patched expression
suggests a molecular mechanism to explain the increased
proliferation and delayed hypertrophic differentiation seen
in EXT1 +//C0endochondral bones during embryonic
development. However, alterations in other signaling

Discussion
Mutations in both EXT1 or EXT2 have been shown to
cause MHE. Since both EXT1 and EXT2 function as HS co-
polymerases, it is believed that defects in HS biosynthesis
cause the characteristic skeletal phenotypes associated with
the disease. The process of endochondral bone development
is partially controlled by signaling molecules that utilize HS
and HSPGs to facilitate ligand–receptor interactions, control
proper protein diffusion, and maintain association of proteins
at the cell surface or within the extracellular matrix. Since
EXT1 and EXT2 are expressed in proliferating chondrocytes,
prehypertrophic chondrocytes, and osteoblasts [28], poten-
tial defects in signaling might occur at almost any stage of
endochondral ossification. To address the effect EXT1 has
on endochondral bone development, we analyzed EXT1+//C0
mutant mice for defects in skeletal morphology, chondrocyte
proliferation and differentiation, and alterations in gene
expression and protein distribution for molecules of the Ihh
signaling pathway during embryonic development.

Similar results in delayed differentiation and increased
proliferation are seen when Ihh is misexpressed within
endochondral bones using viral infection or genetic manip-
ulation [20,22]. Additionally, the expanded collagen 2a1
expression, restricted and reduced collagen 10a1 expres-
sion, expanded patched expression, and increased BrdU
indices found in EXT1+//C0embryos are consistent with
various experiments describing the affects of ectopic Ihh
signaling on endochondral bone development [20–
22,29,30] . We believe that reduced HS biosynthesis
increases Ihh diffusion and signaling throughout prehyper-
trophic/hypertrophic and many immature chondrocytes
causing increased proliferation and delayed differentiation
seen in EXT1+//C0embryonic long bones.
Studies using the Drosophila EXT1 homologue, ttv,
mutant suggested that Hh diffusion and signaling were
diminished due to complete disruption of HS biosynthesis
[18,31]. Additionally, Lin et al. [23] demonstrated an
inability of Ihh to associate with the cell surface of the
visceral endoderm in EXT1/C0//C0embryos. These data are in
contrast to our results showing an increase in Ihh diffusion
and signaling during endochondral ossification due to the
haploinsufficiency of EXT1. The most likely explanation
for this discrepancy is the clear differences in the overall
levels of HS production. Our data and recent data generated
by Andrea V ortkamp [32] has lead us to suggest that Ihh
requires HS for association at the cell surface, as well as for
transportation across multiple cell diameters, but might also
act to limit Ihh diffusion in normal concentrations. There-
fore, EXT1 +//C0mice show regions of expanded Ihh
diffusion and activity due to a near 50% reduction in HS,
while ttv mutants and early EXT1/C0//C0embryos (no HS
production) show restricted Hh and Ihh diffusion and a
complete lack of association at the cell surface. Another
mutant mouse carrying hypomorphic alleles for EXT1
produces only 18% of the normal levels of HS. Analyses
of these mice have demonstrated even greater range of Ihh
diffusion and signaling as compared to EXT1+//C0mice [32].
Additionally, many of the findings we have reported here
describing the effect EXT1 has on Ihh signaling is supported
by recent data generated from the aforementioned EXT1
hypomorph [32].
Molecular consequences of EXT1 haploinsufficiency and
MHE pathology
Signals between the perichondrium and chondrocytes
from areas flanking the hypertrophic and periarticular
regions provide positional cues establishing the vertical
columns of chondrocytes, which ensure proper longitudinal
bone growth. Disruptions in communication between the
perichondrium/periosteum, or bone collar, and chondrocytes
can drastically alter endochondral bone development.
Stickens et al. [28] proposed a model to explain the
molecular mechanisms underlying exostosis formation. In
this model, individuals haploinsufficient for either EXT1 or
EXT2 would obtain a second somatic mutation in either an
EXT gene, or perhaps a third related gene. The subsequent
mutant clone would proliferate giving rise to many mutant
borderline chondrocytes (near the perichondrium) or mutant
perichondral cells. These mutant cells would disrupt signal-
ing between the perichondrium and chondrocytes due to the
complete loss of HS biosynthesis. The localized mutations
