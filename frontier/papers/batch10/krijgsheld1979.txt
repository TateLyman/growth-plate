ABSORPTION, SERUM LEVELS AND URINARY EXCRETION OF 
INORGANIC SULFATE AFTER ORAL ADMINISTRATION OF SODIUM 
SULFATE IN THE CONSCIOUS RAT 
KLAAS R. KRIJGSHELD a, HENNY FRANKENA b, EGBERT SCHOLTENS a, 
JACOBUS ZWEENS b and GERARD J. MULDER a 
Departments of a Pharmacology and b Chemical Physiology, State University of 
Groningen, Bloemsingel 1, 9713 BZ Groningen (The Netherlands)

Key words: Sulfate absorption; Sulfate metabolism; Drug conjugation; Laxative action; 
(Rat) 
Summary 
The absorption of inorganic sulfate after ingestion was investigated in rats. 
After oral administration of Na23SSO4, 3sS radioactivity was measurable in 
plasma already after 15 min and its plasma concentration reached a peak after 
about 1.5--2 h. The 35S-radioactivity excreted in urine during 24 h after inges- 
tion of Na23SSO4 together with varying amounts of unlabelled Na2SO4 (0.25-- 
5.0 mmol Na2SO4 per rat) indicated an almost complete absorption of inor- 
ganic sulfate from the gastrointestinal tract. Determination of the inorganic sul- 
fate concentration in rat serum 2 h after oral administration of 5.0 mmol 
Na2SO4 revealed a three-fold increase in serum sulfate concentration. The data 
suggest a rapid and almost complete absorption of inorganic sulfate after oral 
administration in the rat. Its importance in relation to the sulfate availability 
for sulfate conjugation of drugs is discussed. 
Introduction 
Many xenobiotics with an appropriate acceptor group are conjugated in 
mammals with sulfate, by which they generally loose their pharmacological 
properties and can easily be eliminated in bile or urine. For sulfation, inor- 
ganic sulfate is needed, which is converted with ATP to the co-substrate for sul- 
fation, adenosine 3'-phosphate 5'-sulfatophosphate. Inorganic sulfate in blood 
is immediately available for sulfate conjugation in the liver [1]. Recently it 
has been shown that at the physiological plasma sulfate concentration the rate 
of sulfation is not yet at its maximum [2]. 

493 
It has been suggested that drug that are mainly sulfated in the body can 
exhaust the directly available sulfate pool when given in high doses to animals 
and man [3,4]. Under those conditions, orally administered Na2SO4 increased 
the in vivo sulfation, supposedly by compensating the shortage of sulfate 
[5,6]. In several animal species inorganic sulfate has been shown to be well 
absorbed after oral administration; however, the available data are incomplete 
because they have been obtained using very different experimental conditions 
[7--10]. 
Surprisingly, many textbooks in pharmacology state that inorganic sulfate is 
poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract (see, for example, Refs. 11 and 
12) and use this postulate to explain the laxative action of Na2SO4 and other 
sulfate salts. 
Because of this discrepancy we decided to reinvestigate to what extent orally 
administered sulfate is absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract in conscious 
rats. To quantitate accurately the sulfate absorption, 3SS-labelled Na2SO4 was 
used, with or without unlabelled Na2SO4. These are the first data on oral 
sulfate absorption in the rat, giving the rate of absorption, as well as the result- 
ing inorganic sulfate concentrations in serum and the sulfate excretion in urine. 
Materials and Methods 
Animal experiments 
Male Wistar rats {300--330 g body weight) were used for the experiments. 
They had free access to food and water. 
The rate of appearance of [3SS]sulfate in blood after its oral administration 
was studied in rats with a permanent catheter as described by Steffens [13] in 
the right atrium, which allows the sampling of blood from conscious unre- 
stricted animals. Under light ether anesthesia the animals received 600 gCi/kg 
body wt. Na~3SSO4 in 2 ml water by gastric tube. When only a tracer dose was 
given, the 2 ml water also contained 0.3 mmol NaC1 (NaC1, 9 g/l); when, in 
addition to the tracer, unlabelled sulfate was administered, the solution con- 
tained 1.0 or 5.0 mmol Na2SO4. Blood was collected in heparinized microtubes 
(Caraway Micro Blood Collecting Tubes, Sherwood Medical Inc. St. Louis, MO, 
U.S.A.) at various time intervals, to determine the plasma 3sS concentration. 
In normal rats, without a permanent catheter, the urinary excretion of sul- 
fate was studied following administration by gastric tube under light ether 
anaesthesia of Na235SO4 (50 ~Ci/kg body wt.) in 2 ml water with NaC1 (0.3 
mmol) or with Na2SO4 in increasing amounts (0.25 to 5.0 mmol). In these 
experiments the 3SS-radioactivity is concentrated in urine; therefore, a lower 
dose of radioactivity was given than in the experiments in which 3sS-radioac- 
tivity was measured in a small sample of plasma. The rats were placed in metab- 
olism cages designed to allow the separate collection of urine and faeces. Urine 
was collected up to 7 days after oral sulfate administration. Rats receiving the 
two high sulfate doses (2.5 and 5.0 mmol) were afflicted with diarrhoea, which 
started about 4 h after sulfate administration and lasted for about 4 h. To 
prevent contamination of urine by faeces, the watery stools were removed 
immediately after they were produced. This, however, inevitably resulted in

When unlabelled sodium sulfate was added to the administered solution, the

Excretion of [3SS]sulfate in urine after oral administration of Na2S04 
When the labelled sodium sulfate (50 gCi/kg body wt.) is administered in a 
saline solution or with 0.25 or 0.5 mmol of Na2SO4 per rat, about 90% of the 
administered radioactivity is recovered in urine within 24 h (Table I). In the 
following days only a small additional amount is excreted, so that after a week 
a little more than 90% of the 3SS-radioactivity is recovered in urine. When the 
dose of unlabelled Na2SO4 was increased to 1.0, 2.5 and 5.0 mmol per rat, the 
percentage of the dose recovered in urine after 24 h was decreased to 73, 67 
and 56%, respectively. However, the recovery data at these higher sulfate doses 
underestimate the actual excretion because of the urine loss as a consequence 
of diarrhoea and because of the decreasing efficiency of 3s S-radioactivity count- 
ing caused by high concentration of inorganic sulfate in the urine sample. Since 
we did not measure the sulfate concentration in urine, it was not posible to cor- 
rect for the decreased efficiency of counting. In order to determine the size of 
this correction, we have administered 5 mmol labelled sulfate to an additional 
group of rats (n = 4) and determined the 24 h urinary excretion of 3SS-radioac- 
tivity. Without correction we recovered 41.4 ± 5.1% of the dose (mean ±S.E.), 
but after dilution of urine to maximal efficiency of counting we determined a 
recovery of 47.3 ± 5.1%. This shows that the high concentration of sulfate in 
urine does decrease the recovery by about 15%. 
Serum sulfate concentrations 2 h after oral administration of inorganic sulfate 
Our experiments with 3SS-labelled sulfate indicated that about 2 h after oral 
sulfate administration, a peak in plasma sulfate concentration was reached.

A dose of 0.5 mmol Na:SO4 caused a significant increase :in serum sulfate 
concentration compared with the control situation (nothing administered). 
Raising the sulfate dose to 2.5 or 5.0 mmol, Na2SO4 increased serum sulfate 
even more. Because the sulfate solutions administered were strongly hyper- 
tonic, NaC1 solutions with a comparable osmolarity were given as a control. 
We found that, already at an oral dose of 0.3 mmol NaC1 in 2 ml water, NaC1 
decreased the serum sulfate concentration (Table II). Oral administration of 
2.5 or 7.5 mmol NaCI caused, both to the same extent, a more profound 
decrease in the serum sulfate concentration, whereas oral administration of 
water had no influence on the serum sulfate concentration. 
To obtain more information about possible shifts of body water to the 
gastrointestinal tract as a consequence of the administration of a hypertonic 
solution, we determined osmolality, chloride concentration and total protein in 
serum, along with inorganic sulfate (Table III). Oral administration of 2 ml 
distilled water had no influence on the sulfate concentration, chloride concen- 
tration and osmolality in serum as compared with values from control rats, 
while they were elevated by 5 mmol of sodium sulfate. An NaC1 dose of the 
same osmolarity as the highest Na2SO4 dose caused increases in serum osmo- 
lality and chloride, but a decrease in serum sulfate. Total protein did not differ 
significantly from control in either group.

Discussion 
The absorption of orally administered Na2SO4 in freely moving rats is very 
rapid. 3SS-radioactivity was detected in plasma soon after administration. When 
a tracer dose was given, a peak in radioactivity was reached after 1.5--2 h. With 
larger amounts of Na2SO4 the level of 3SS-radioactivity remained for longer near 
the peak level. When 5 mmol of Na2SO4 was given, a steady state was almost 
reached between the absorption from the gastrointestinal tract and the elimina- 
tion from the blood. 
From these experiments no information was obtained about the quantities 
of sulfate absorbed. This information came from the results of the experiments 
on urinary excretion of orally administered Na23SSO4 together with increasing 
amounts of unlabelled sulfate. At the lower doses about 90% of the radioactiv- 
ity was excreted in urine, indicating almost complete absorption of the dose 
from the gut. The loss of about 10% of the total [3SS]sulfate dose can in part 
be explained by the incorporation of 3ss into macromolecules in the body. Al- 
though the percentage recovered in urine decreased at higher sulfate doses, the 
absolute amount of sulfate absorbed strongly increases. Since the highest doses 
of sulfate caused diarrhoea it is to be expected that sulfate is lost in the faeces 
and recovery in urine must be lower because less time is available for absorp- 
tion. 
From the data in Table I it is obvious that in the case of the highest sulfate 
dose, half of the total radioactivity excreted in urine in 24 h had already been 
excreted after 8 h. After 24 h little additional 3SS-radioactivity was excreted. 
This emphasizes the rapidity of sulfate absorption. 
As for the high urinary recovery of orally administered [3SS]sulfate, the 
results of Berry et al. [8] point in the same direction. They found in swine, 
after oral administration of sodium [3SS]sulfate, a peak level of radioactivity 
in serum after 3 h. After 7 days 58% of the dose was excreted in urine against 

499 
only 5% in the faeces. A high recovery of oral sulfate was also found by Dzie- 
wiatkowski [7] in the rat and more recently by O'Connor et al. [9] in the dog. 
In order to evaluate the increase in the serum sulfate concentration and the 
possible concomitant water shifts, we have measured osmolality, inorganic 
sulfate, chloride and protein concentration in serum after oral Na2SO4 adminis- 
tration (Table III). If we assume the distribution volume for chloride and sul- 
fate ions to be 33% and 25% of body weight, respectively [17,18], and total 
distribution volume of osmotically active particles (total body water) to be 
67% of body weight [18], the following calculation can be made for the rats 
of 300 g used in these experiments. The increase of the mean serum sulfate 
concentration from 0.8 to 2.4 mmol/1 after sulfate administration (Table III) 
corresponds to the addition of 0.12 mmol sulfate to the sulfate distribution 
volume, which increases osmolality of the body water by 1.8 mosmol/kg. The 
increase of chloride in plasma from 104 to 106 mmol/1 most likely is due to a 
shift of body water to the gastrointestinal tract. It can be calculated that this 
shift amounts to 2.4 ml water, which raises the osmolality of serum with 3.6 
mosmol/kg. Thus, sulfate absorption and water movement together can 
account for the observed change in osmolality of serum from 296.2 to 302.0 
mosmol/kg. 
The increase of osmolality after oral administration of NaC1 (Table III) can 
be completely accounted for by the increased serum chloride concentration, 
which implies that, on the moment of blood sampling, NaC1 had not caused a 
net water movement from the body fluids to the gastrointestinal tract. It is 
remarkable that oral administration of NaC1, even physiological saline 
decreased serum sulfate. Further investigations are required to solve the under- 
lying mechanism. 
Clearly the laxative action of sulfate salts cannot be due to their lack of 
absorption in the intestine. An alternative explanation of this action is that the 
high sulfate concentration irritates the intestinal mucosa, as is suggested by the 
hyperemic state of intestine which we observed, which causes an accelerated 
transit of intestinal contents. A rapid accumulation of water in the intestinal 
lumen by osmotic action may add to the laxative effect. 
The toxicological implication of these findings are obvious: since many toxic 
compounds are detoxified by sulfate conjugation, the availability of sulfate 
may become decisive in this process. Oral supplementation of sulfate under 
conditions of sulfate depletion at high doses of toxicans clearly relieves this. On 
the other hand, some compounds containing an N-hydroxy group become more 
toxic by sulfation [19]. High inorganic sulfate in the food therefore might 
enhance the toxicity of this type of compound, since (in the rat) sulfation is 
not yet saturated in respect to inorganic sulfate at physiological plasma sulfate 
levels [2].
