bone mass acquisition has little effect on adult bone density

ABSTRACT 
 
It is often assum ed that bone m ineral accre tion should be optim ized throughout childhood to 
maximize peak bone m ass. In contrast, we hypothe sized that bone m ineral acquisition early in 
life would have little or no effect on adult bone mass because many areas of the juvenile skeleton 
are replaced in toto through skeletal growth. 
 
To test this hypothesis, we induced osteoporosis  by administering dexamethasone to 5-week-old 
rabbits for 5 weeks and then allowed them  to recover for 16 weeks. Tibial bone m ineral density 
(ash weight/volume) was decreased in the dexamethasone-treated animals at the end of treatment 
but recovered com pletely. Bone structure in  the fem ur was assessed by histom orphometry. 
Trabecular and cortical bone in the distal m etaphysis was made osteoporotic by dexam ethasone, 
but was then replaced through endochondral bone formation and recovered. Periosteal bone 
formation rate in the diaphysis was decreased during dexam ethasone treatm ent but afterwards 
rebounded above controls and norm alized cortical  width. Our data suggest that bone m ineral 
acquisition early in life has little effect on adult bone  density because the juvenile bone is largely 
replaced through growth. If this concept generali zes, then interventions to m aximize peak bone 
mass should be directed at adolescents rather than young children. 
 
Key words: child • glucocorticoids • dexamethasone • adolescence • bone density 
B 
 
one mass increases during childhood, peaks in late adolescence or early adulthood, and 
then may decrease with age (1), som etimes eventuating in osteoporosis. The childhood 
increase in bone mass is due prim arily to an increase in bone size, with less change in 
volumetric bone density (2). Individuals who attain a higher peak bone m ass may be at 
decreased risk for the development of osteoporosis later in life (3). 
 
  
  
Although an individuals peak bone m ass is determined primarily by genetics (4), environmental 
conditions can also influence bone mineral acquisition and, thus, peak bone mass. Consequently, 
it is often assum ed that environm ental factors, such as dietary calcium  intake, should be 
optimized throughout childhood to maximize peak bone mass (1, 5, 6). This assum ption is based 
primarily on studies that do not distinguish betw een factors present in early childhood and those 
present in adolescence, during the years im mediately preceding the attainm ent of peak bone 
mass. The supposition, that bone mineral acquisition even in early childhood contributes to adult 
bone density, however, may not be consonant with the biology of skeletal growth. 
 
In the growing m ammal, skeletal growth occurs through a com bination of processes. At the 
growth plate, new trabecular bone is produ ced through endochondral bone form ation. As the 
bone elongates, the medullary cavity also lengthens and the innermost trabeculae are resorbed by 
osteoclasts. In the diaphysis, cortical bone is cr eated directly by periosteal bone form ation (7). 
As the m edullary cavity expands radially, corti cal bone at the endosteal surface is resorbed by 
osteoclasts. Therefore, trabecular and cortical bone formed early in life is gradually resorbed and 
replaced by new bone as the bone and the m edullary cavity enlarge. W e hypothesized that bone 
mineral acquisition early in life would have little  or no effect on peak bone m ass because bone 
formed early in childhood is replaced through skel etal growth. Consequently, transient exposure 
to bone-promoting or bone-inhibiting conditions ear ly in childhood would not affect peak bone 
mass and would thus not alter the risk of osteoporosis later in life. 
 
To test this hypothesis, we adm inistered dexamethasone (dex) to young rabbits to induce severe 
osteoporosis. The dexamethasone treatment was then discontinued. After the anim als had grown 
to near-final size, we assessed bone mineral density, strength, and structure to determine whether 
the juvenile osteoporosis had been reversed through skeletal growth. 
 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
 
Animals 
 
Animals were housed and cared for in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) 
Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Anim als (8). They were fed Open Form ula Rabbit 
Diet (NIH-32), which contains a m inimum of 1.15 g calcium  and 220 IU Vitam in D per 100 g. 
The experim ental procedures were approved by the National Institute of  Child Health and 
Human Development Animal Care and Use Committee. 
 
Five-week-old male New Zealand White rabbits (Covance Research Products, Denver, PA) were 
treated with supraphysiological doses of de xamethasone (Phoenix Pharm aceutical, St. Joseph, 
MO), 0.5 mg/kg/day subcutaneously for 5 weeks.  Male New Zealand white rabbits begin sexual 
maturation at approxim ately two m onths of ag e (9) and reach adult size by approxim ately 6 
months of age (10). W e began treatment at 5 weeks of age to allow sufficient tim e for induction 
of osteoporosis and recovery before com pletion of growth. Five-week-old m ale rabbits have 
achieved approxim ately 60% of adult length (11). Untreated anim als served as concurrent 
controls. One day after discontinuation of dexam ethasone, all rabbits began receiving 
oxytetracycline (Phoenix Pharm aceutical), 10 m g/kg i.m . weekly to label newly form ed bone. 
We administered the last dose one day before  killing. Animals were weighed weekly throughout 
  
  
the study. Rabbits were killed by pentobarbital overdose at 2, 9, 16, 30, and 114 days (expressed 
as 0, 1, 2, 4, and 16 weeks for convenience) after the discontinuation of dexam ethasone (eight 
animals in each treatment group per time point) and the femurs and tibias were analyzed. Two of 
the dexamethasone-treated anim als died bef ore scheduled killing and were not included in the 
analysis. Because dexam ethasone not only induces osteoporosis, but also inhibits growth plate 
chondrogenesis, we used these sam e rabbits to explore the growth plate m echanisms responsible 
for catch-up in linear growth (12). 
 
Tibial dry and ash weights 
 
Following removal of soft tissue, tibial volum e was determ ined by underwater weighing. Each 
tibia was suspended in water by a thread. W e reported volume as the weight of the bone and 
water minus the weight of the water alone. Maxi mal tibial length was also m easured by using a 
digital caliper (Mutitoyo Corp., Kawasaki, Japan). The tibias were then stored at 80 °C. The 
right tibias were later thawed and then dehydrat ed and defatted with five washes of acetone 
(Mallinckrodt Baker, Inc., Paris, KY), f ollowed by 5 washes of  diethyl ether (Mallinckrodt 
Baker, Inc., Paris, KY). The bones were air-dri ed overnight. Dry weights were m easured, and 
dry bone density was calculated as the dry weight divided by bone volum e (g/cm3). The tibias 
were then ashed in a m uffle furnace (B arnstead Therm olyne, Bubuque, IA) at 105 ßC for 2 h, 
followed by 580ßC for 22 h. Tibial ash was weighed and the bone mineral density was calculated 
as the ash weight divided by bone volume (g/cm3). 
 
Biomechanical testing 
 
Biomechanical strength was assessed in the left  tibias from  the 0- and 16-week groups. W e 
performed a three-point bending test by using a m echanical testing m achine (MTS Bionix 858; 
MTS, Edin Praire, MN) (13). The bones were th awed in saline and positioned on the two m etal 
supports (6 m m in diam eter with a 50 m m span ) with the cranial surface facing the loading 
striker (6 m m in diam eter). The loading point was located three-fifths of the distance from  the 
proximal to the distal end of the bone. Loadi ng speed was 1 mm/min. Ultimate load was defined 
as the m aximum load applied until f ailure. Stif fness was m easured as the slope of  the initial 
linear portion of the load-displacement curve. 
 
Diaphyseal histomorphometry 
 
Using a bone saw (Marmed, Inc., Cleveland, OH), we cut a 23 mm thick cross section from the 
mid-diaphysis of each left fem ur. The m arrow was removed, and the diam eter of the m edullary 
cavity was m easured in duplicate by using a di ssecting m icroscope with m icrometer by an 
observer blinded to treatm ent. We measured the width of the cortical bone, form ed before and 
after stopping dexamethasone, by using a fluorescen ce microscope, a video cam era, a computer, 
and computer software (Oncor Im age, Version 2.0, Oncor Im aging, Gaithersburg, MD). Cortex 
formed before cessation of dexamethasone was assessed by measuring the width of the unlabeled 
bone adjacent to the endosteal surface. Cort ex form ed after stopping dexam ethasone was 
assessed by m easuring the fluorescent oxytetracyc line-labeled bone adjacent to the periosteal 
surface. Cortical measurements were made at the anterior and posterior aspects of the fem ur and 
summed for each specim en; the sum  of the labeled and unlabeled bone represented the 
  
  
diaphyseal cortical width. W e determ ined the total diam eter of the fem oral diaphysis by 
summing the marrow diameter and the diaphyseal cortical width. 
 
Metaphyseal and epiphyseal histomorphometry 
 
The right distal fem urs were dehydrated in se rial solutions of graded ethanol followed by 
clearing in xylene and inf iltration with methyl methacrylate. Serial 5-µm sections were cut near-
parallel to the long-axis of the fem ur in the co ronal plane and stained with m odified Goldners 
Trichrome. 
 
Using com puter-assisted histom orphometry (Ost eomeasure, version 2.2, Osteom etrics, Inc., 
Decatur, GA), an observer blinded to treatment group measured static histomorphometric indices 
(bone volume/total tissue volum e = BV/TV; tr abecular number = Tb.N; trabecular thickness = 
Tb.Th) (14) in a rectangular area (0.5 × 2 mm), 1 mm interior to the i nner edge of the cortex in 
the epiphysis and m etaphysis. In the epiphysis, th is area was located 2 m m distal to the resting 
zone of the growth plate; in the m etaphysis, this area was 1 m m proximal to the hypertrophic 
zone of the growth plate. The cortical width of the distal fem oral metaphysis was measured 57 
mm below the growth plate, where trabecular bone was no longer present. 
 
For dynamic histomorphometry, unstained 8-µm sections cut near-parallel to the long-axis of the 
undecalcified right fem urs were prepared as described above. W e used com puter-assisted 
fluorescence histom orphometry (Osteom easure, ve rsion 2.2) to exam ine epiphyseal trabecular 
bone in the sam e location used for the static  indices. Mineral apposition rate in epiphyseal 
trabecular bone double-labeled with oxytetracycline was m easured in the control and treated 
groups killed two weeks after stopping dexam ethasone. Dynam ic histom orphometry was not 
performed in the m etaphyseal spongiosa because the pattern of tetracycline labeling was too 
irregular to apply standard techniques. 
 
Statistical analysis 
 
Data are presented as m ean ± SE. W e analyzed the rates of  change by using m ultiple linear 
regression. For analyses of the recovery period, we excluded the 0-week tim e point to avoid the 
residual effects of dexam ethasone treatm ent. We com pared tim e points by using a two-way 
ANOVA followed by a Tukey test for posthoc pair wise m ultiple com parisons. Ultim ate load, 
stiffness, and mineral apposition rate were com pared between groups at different tim e points by 
using the Students t-test. 
 
RESULTS 
 
Body weight  
 
During the 5-week treatm ent period, dexam ethasone-treated animals gained weight less rapidly 
than control anim als (P < 0.001). After stopping dexam ethasone, treated animals gained weight 
more rapidly than controls ( P < 0.05). However, differences in weight were still present at the 
end of the study (P < 0.005). 
 
  
  
Tibial bone density 
 
At the end of the treatment period, tibial length of the dexamethasone-treated animals was 17.7 ± 
1.3 m m less than that of controls ( P < 0.001). After 16 weeks of recovery, this deficit was 
reduced to 2.6 ± 1.7 m m (P = NS). Because of this difference in bone size during the recovery 
period, tibial dry weight and ash weight were  norm alized to bone volum e. Tibial dry bone 
density (dry weight/volum e) and tibial bone m ineral density (ash weight/volum e) were both 
significantly decreased in the dexamethasone-treated animals at the end of treatment period (Fig. 
1, P < 0.001). During the recovery period, the rate of increase in bone density and bone m ineral 
density in the previously treated an imals exceeded that of controls ( P < 0.001), such that by 16 
weeks the dexam ethasone-treated anim als had recovered com pletely ( Fig. 1 , P = NS, dex vs. 
controls at 16 weeks). The transient decreas e in density seen one week after stopping 
dexamethasone reflects a more rapid increase in bone volume than in dry and ash weights during 
the early recovery period (data not shown). 
 
Biomechanical testing 
 
At the end of  treatm ent, resistance to three- point bending (ultim ate load and stif fness) was 
decreased significantly in the dexam ethasone-treated group com pared with controls (ultim ate 
load: 95 – 4 vs. 167 – 11 N, P < 0.001; stiffness: 91 – 5 vs. 159 – 16 N/m m, P < 0.001). The 
ultimate load and stiffness in both the treated and control groups increased significantly after 
stopping dexam ethasone ( P < 0.001). By 16 weeks of rec overy, there was no significant 
difference in ultim ate load and stiffness between the group previously treated with 
dexamethasone and the control group (ultimate load: 310 – 19 vs. 351 – 27 N, P = NS; stiffness: 
407 – 38 vs. 481 – 38 N/mm, P = NS).  
 
Metaphyseal trabecular bone 
 
Trabecular bone in the distal fem oral metaphysis developed significant osteoporosis by the end 
of the dexamethasone treatment. Specifically, the bone volume (BV/TV) in the treated group was 
35% of controls at w eek 0 of recovery ( Figs. 2A  and 3A, P < 0.001). By 16 weeks, the 
metaphyseal trabecular bone had recovered com pletely (156% of controls at 16 weeks, Figs. 2A 
and 3B, P = NS). The oxytetracycline-labeling pattern  indicated an initial growth rate of 1.52 
mm/week in the anim als previously treated w ith dexamethasone (12). Therefore, by 12 weeks 
of recovery, the exam ined region of m etaphyseal trabecular bone, located 11.5 m m below the 
growth plate, contained bone form ed afte r the dexam ethasone was stopped. Thus, the 
osteoporotic bone was replaced through endochondral bone formation. 
 
Trabecular bone density is a function of both trabecular num ber and trabecular thickness; 
quantitation of these param eters revealed that this recovery in m etaphyseal trabecular bone was 
due primarily to a recovery in trabecular number (Fig. 2B, P < 0.001). 
 
Metaphyseal cortical bone 
 
Cortical bone in the distal fem oral metaphysis was thinned by dexam ethasone at the end of the 
treatment period (52% of controls at 0 weeks, Fig. 2C and 3A, P < 0.001) but then recovered 
  
  
(92% of controls at 16 weeks, Figs. 2C and 3B, P = NS). The oxytetracycline-labeling pattern 
indicated an initial growth rate of  approximately 2 m m/week in the anim als previously treated 
with dexamethasone (12). Therefore, by 24 week s of recovery, new bone form ed by skeletal 
growth was located 48 m m below the growth plat e, the region where the m etaphyseal cortical 
measurements were m ade. Thus, the recovery in m etaphyseal cortical bone, which occurred 
rapidly by 2 weeks after stopping dexam ethasone ( Fig. 2C), was due to the form ation of new 
cortical bone through skeletal growth, rather than bone remodeling. 
 
Diaphyseal cortical bone 
 
At the end of the treatm ent period, the cortical width of the fem oral diaphysis was decreased in 
the dexam ethasone-treated anim als com pared with controls ( Fig. 4A , P < 0.01). This was 
accompanied by a decrease in total fem oral diameter (Fig. 4B, P < 0.01) without a difference in 
marrow diam eter ( Fig. 4C , P = NS). After the dexam ethasone was stopped, both the cortical 
width and the total fem oral diam eter recovered ( Fig. 4A , B, P = NS, dex vs. control at 16 
weeks). Marrow diam eter, a function of endost eal resorption, did not differ between the two 
treatment groups ( Fig. 4C , P = NS) throughout the recovery. Oxytetracycline-labeled cortical 
bone, which represents new periosteal bone fo rmed after stopping dexam ethasone, grew m ore 
rapidly in the anim als previously treated with dexam ethasone than in controls ( Fig. 4D , P < 
0.001). Therefore, the recovery in diaphyseal cor tical width was due prim arily to increased new 
periosteal bone formation, rather than decreased endosteal resorption (Fig. 3C). 
 
Epiphyseal trabecular bone 
 
Bone volum e (BV/TV) was decreased in the di stal fem oral epiphysis at the end of the 
dexamethasone treatm ent (64% of controls, Figs. 3A  and 5A, P < 0.05) but recovered by 16 
weeks after stopping dexam ethasone (94% of controls, Figs. 3B and 5A, P = NS). Because the 
rate of recovery appeared to peak betw een the first and second week after stopping 
dexamethasone, this tim e point was selected  for dynam ic m easurements. Histom orphometric 
analysis of oxytetracycline-labeled epiphyseal trabecular bone dem onstrated that the m ineral 
apposition rate during the second week of the recovery was significantly increased in the animals 
previously treated with dexam ethasone ( Fig. 5B , P < 0.05). Fluorescence m icroscopy at 16 
weeks revealed that all of the epiphyseal tr abecular bone in the treated anim als contained 
oxytetracycline label ( Fig. 3D ). Because oxytetracycline was adm inistered after stopping the 
dexamethasone, this finding indicates that the epiphyseal bone had been com pletely replaced 
during the recovery period. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
Transient glucocorticoid excess induced severe osteoporosis in prepubertal m ale rabbits. The 
histomorphometric findings were sim ilar to t hose seen in hum an glucocorticoid-induced 
osteoporosis, as previously reported (15). Thus , the rabbit appears to be a better m odel for 
glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis than the rat, which often develops increased bone density 
with glucocorticoid treatm ent (16;17). However, the rabbits, unlike hum ans, lost weight during 
the period of  glucocorticoid excess, which raises the possibility that decreased m echanical load 
and undernutrition m ay have contributed to the osteoporosis. Following discontinuation of 
  
  
glucocorticoid, tibial dry bone density (dry we ight/volume) and bone m ineral density (ash 
weight/volume) recovered com pletely as the animals approached near-final size. Strength 
(assessed by three-point bending) differed significan tly between groups at the end of treatm ent 
but not at the end of recovery. 
 
Histomorphometric analysis revealed that exce ss glucocorticoid induced osteoporosis in both 
trabecular and cortical bone. In the fem oral metaphysis, glucocorticoid decreased trabecular 
bone density. The decreased density was due prim arily to a decrease in trabecular num ber. After 
the anim al was released from  glucocorticoid excess, trabecular num ber and bone density 
recovered rapidly. In the growing rabbit,  m etaphyseal trabecular bone is produced by 
endochondral bone form ation. The bone form ed most recently is located closest to the growth 
plate. W ith tim e, the newly form ed trabecul ae are resorbed by osteoclasts to produce the 
medullary cavity. Oxytetracycline labeling indicat ed that the osteoporotic bone form ed during 
dexamethasone treatm ent was resorbed as the medullary cavity enlarged and was replaced by 
new bone form ed by endochondral o ssification at the growth plate. Thus, the recovery from  
osteoporosis was not due to the repair of pre-existing bone but rather to replacem ent through 
longitudinal bone growth. 
 
Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis also recovere d in the m etaphyseal cortex. Cortex in this 
region is formed by coalescence of trabeculae under the peripheral growth plate. Therefore, after 
stopping the dexam ethasone, the osteoporotic m etaphyseal cortex was replaced by new cortex 
formed by endochondral ossification. Thus, the recovery of m etaphyseal cortical bone, like 
trabecular bone, was due to skeletal growth, not remodeling. 
 
In the fem oral diaphysis, glucocorticoid excess significantly decreased cortical width. Norm al 
cortical growth occurs prim arily through pe riosteal bone form ation and endosteal bone 
resorption throughout childhood, with som e additional endosteal form ation in late adolescence 
(7). Therefore, the decreased cortical width coul d have been due to either decreased periosteal 
bone form ation or increased endosteal bone reso rption. In glucocorticoid-treated anim als, the 
cortical width was decreased while the m arrow diameter was unaffected, indicating that the 
major effect of glucocorticoid excess is not th e stim ulation of bone resorption but rather the 
inhibition of bone formation, as has been previously described (18). 
 
After stopping dexamethasone, diaphyseal cortical width recovered due to an increase in the rate 
of periosteal bone form ation. Therefore, as in the m etaphysis, the diaphyseal osteoporosis 
recovered through skeletal growth but the m echanism differed in the two regions. Recovery of 
metaphyseal osteoporosis involved replacement of osteoporotic bone with normal bone, whereas 
recovery of diaphyseal cortex involved an increas e in the rate of bone form ation. This increased 
periosteal growth m ay be an exam ple of a ge neral phenomenon, termed catch-up growth, in 
which increased growth follows the release from growth inhibition (19). 
 
Glucocorticoid also induced osteoporosis in the distal femoral epiphysis. After the glucocorticoid 
was discontinued, the osteoporotic epiphyseal tr abecular bone was replaced with new bone and 
thus recovered. In addition, the m ineral apposition rate was greater in the anim als previously 
treated with dexam ethasone, which suggests th at a m echanism sim ilar to catch-up growth 
contributed to recovery in this region also. 
  
  
 
Our findings suggest that alterations in bone  mass acquisition during early childhood m ay have 
little effect on peak bone density. This finding a ppears to contradict m any previous studies 
showing that altered bone m ass acquisition does influence adult bone density. For exam ple, 
retrospective clinical studies suggest that phys ical activity (2022), calcium  intake (23), and 
cranial irradiation (24, 25) during childhood and adolescence affect adult bone m ass. Similarly, 
prospective animal studies indicate that calcium  intake throughout growth influences peak bone 
mass in rats (26) and rabbits (10). The com monality am ong these studies is that they involve 
prolonged and uninterrupted environm ental conditions or, in the case of cranial irradiation, m ay 
have resulted in a perm anent alteration in horm onal milieu. Nevertheless, it has been suggested 
that any intervention during childhood that either  increases or decreases bone m ass acquisition, 
even tem porarily, m ay change the genetically predetermined bone phenotype and result in an 
altered peak bone mass (6, 27). 
 
The findings in the present study challenge this  concept. W e have shown that a period of 
decreased bone m ass acquisition due to glucocortic oid excess in early life does not result in an 
altered peak bone m ass. This com plete recovery might not occur following other disorders that 
impair bone m ineral acquisition, particularly if they are not associated with catch-up growth. 
However, clinical studies suggest that this prin ciple may, in fact, apply to other conditions in 
humans. In children, significant recovery from  osteoporosis occurs after treatm ent for Graves 
disease (28), juvenile arthritis (29), Crohn s disease (30), leukem ia (24), and Cushings 
syndrome (31). In these cases, the patients experien ced either complete cure or total remission of 
their underlying disease before the com pletion of growth. Similarly, after transient exposure to 
bone-altering conditions, such as increased cal cium intake during childhood, the juvenile 
skeleton has the tendency to return to its gene tically determined trajectory towards peak bone 
mass (32). In a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial involving identical twins, calcium  
supplementation for 3 years increased bone m ineral density in the prepubertal subjects (33). 
However, when these children were evaluate d 3 years after discontinuation of calcium  
supplements, there was no difference in bone density  between the twin pairs (32). These authors 
and others suggest that calcium  supplem entation increased bone density by decreasing bone 
remodeling (32, 34) and that discontinuation of  calcium led to a rebound in bone rem odeling, 
returning the skeleton to it previous percentile. 
 
The present study dem onstrates an additi onal m echanism underlying this phenom enon. Many 
areas of the juvenile skeleton are not simply rem odeled but actually replaced in toto  through 
skeletal growth. As a juvenile bone enlarges  and the m edullary cavity expands, bone form ed 
early in life, regardless of quality, is gradually resorbed and replaced by new bone through 
modeling. In addition, the catch-up growth that o ccurs after transient growth inhibition also 
contributes to the normalization of bone mass. Therefore, perturbations in bone m ass acquisition 
are not reversed solely by remodeling but rather by a combination of modeling and remodeling. 
 
If these findings generalize to hum ans, then bone m ass acquisition in early childhood m ay not 
influence peak bone m ass. Public health efforts to prevent osteoporosis em phasize calcium  
intake, weight-bearing exercise, and lim iting anti- bone therapies, such as glucocorticoid use, 
during the growing years. However, our findings suggest that such interventions during early 
childhood, although they might have other benefits, may not affect adult bone density.

Figure 1. Tibial dry bone density (A) and bone mineral density (B) after cessation of dexamethasone treatment 
(mean ± SE). Five-week-old rabbits received dexamethasone (0.5 mg/kg/day) subcutaneously for 5 weeks and were then

treated animals (n = 7–8 per time point). After dissection, tibial volume was measured by underwater weighing. Tibias

Fig. 2 
 
              
 
Figure 2. Bone volume (A) and trabecular number (B) in the distal femoral metaphysis after cessation of 
dexamethasone treatment (mean ± SE). Five-week-old rabbits received dexamethasone (0.5 mg/kg/day) subcutaneously 
for 5 weeks and were then allowed to recover for 0 to 16 weeks. Open circles, control animals (n = 8 per time point); 
closed circles, dexamethasone-treated animals (n = 7–8 per time point). Longitudinal sections were obtained from the 
distal femurs and BV/TV, and Tb.N were measured 1 mm interior to the cortex and 1 mm proximal to the growth plate in 
the distal femoral metaphysis by using computer-assisted histomorphometry. C) Width of the femoral metaphyseal cortex 
after stopping dexamethasone treatment (mean ± SE). Longitudinal sections were obtained from the distal femurs, and 
metaphyseal cortical width was measured 5–7 mm proximal to the growth plate in the distal femur. 
 
Fig. 3 
 
                      
 
Figure 3. Photomicrographs of the distal femur at the end of dexamethasone treatment (A) and 16 weeks (B) after 
cessation of dexamethasone. Five-week-old rabbits received dexamethasone (0.5 mg/kg/day) subcutaneously for 5 
weeks and were then allowed to recover. Untreated animals served as controls. Longitudinal coronal sections were stained 
with Goldners Trichrome. Scale bar, 3 mm. C) Photomicrograph of the femoral diaphyseal cortex 16 weeks after 
stopping dexamethasone. During the recovery, all animals received weekly injections of oxytetracycline to label newly 
formed bone. Diaphyseal cross sections were examined by fluorescence microscopy. The periosteal surface is at the upper 
edge; the endosteal surface, at the lower edge of the sections. Arrows indicate oxytetracycline-labeled fluorescent bone 
formed after stopping dexamethasone. Scale bar, 300 µm. D) Photomicrograph of the distal femoral epiphyseal trabeculae 
16 weeks after stopping dexamethasone. Unstained sections were examined by fluorescence microscopy. The section 
from a dexamethasone-treated animal contains only labeled bone, which indicates that all bone was formed after the 
dexamethasone was discontinued; b, bone tissue; m, marrow. Scale bar, 200 µm. 
 
Fig. 4 
 
                      
 
Figure 4. Cortical width (A), bone diameter (B), marrow diameter (C), new periosteal bone (D) of the femoral 
diaphysis after cessation of dexamethasone treatment (mean ± SE). Five-week-old rabbits received dexamethasone 
(0.5 mg/kg/day) subcutaneously for 5 weeks and were then allowed to recover for 0 to 16 weeks. Open circles, control 
animals (n = 8 per time point); closed circles, dexamethasone-treated animals (n = 7–8 per time point). Femoral cross 
sections were obtained, and marrow diameter was measured under a dissecting microscope. Cortical width and new 
periosteal bone were measured by using computer-assisted fluorescence microscopy. Bone diameter was the sum of the 3 
parameters.
